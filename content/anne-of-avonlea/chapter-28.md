# Chapter 28 — The Prince Comes Back to the Enchanted Palace

## Story Summary

Anne’s final day as teacher at the Avonlea school arrives. The pupils do very well in their semi-annual examination, and at the end of the day they give Anne a formal address and a writing desk as farewell gifts. Many of the children cry, and Anne is deeply moved.

After everyone leaves, Anne sits alone at her desk and looks around the schoolroom. She remembers the two years she has spent teaching there. She knows she has made mistakes, but she also knows that she has learned a great deal from her pupils. Leaving them makes college seem less exciting for a moment.

Early in the vacation, Anne goes to Echo Lodge for two weeks. Miss Lavendar has been feeling low, so Anne keeps her busy. They go shopping, and Anne persuades her to buy a new forget-me-not colored organdy dress. Planning and making the dress brings some of Miss Lavendar’s old energy back.

During Anne’s stay, Miss Lavendar gradually becomes more interested in ordinary plans again. The new dress, small household projects, and Anne’s company give her something pleasant to think about after the tired and lonely mood Anne noticed earlier in the summer.

Halfway through the visit, Anne returns to Green Gables for a day. In the evening she goes to see Paul Irving. Paul rushes out excitedly and tells her that his father has unexpectedly come home.

Stephen Irving is a tall, handsome middle-aged man with iron-gray hair and dark blue eyes. He thanks Anne for the influence she has had on Paul. He says Paul’s grandmother has cared for him well, but Anne has understood parts of the boy’s imagination and personality that his grandmother sometimes could not.

After Paul leaves to do a chore, Stephen asks Anne about Miss Lavendar. Paul had written to him about visiting her at Echo Lodge. Stephen admits that he has not heard much about Miss Lavendar for many years, but he clearly wants to see her again.

He asks Anne whether she will find out if Miss Lavendar is willing to receive him. Anne is thrilled by the request and goes to Echo Lodge the next morning.

Before Anne can explain fully, Miss Lavendar guesses that Stephen is home. Her face turns pale. When Anne says he wants to visit, Miss Lavendar agrees, although she tries to speak as if he were only an ordinary old friend.

Anne sends Stephen a note. All afternoon Miss Lavendar is restless, and Anne is just as nervous. Charlotta the Fourth realizes that something important is happening and finally learns that Stephen is coming.

That evening Miss Lavendar puts on her new organdy dress, and Anne arranges her hair. Stephen arrives and tells Anne that the house and garden seem almost unchanged from twenty-five years earlier.

Anne lets him into the parlor and then goes away with Charlotta so Miss Lavendar and Stephen can speak privately. The two younger women clean silver while they wait.

After about an hour, they hear the door. Fearing Stephen is leaving, they hurry to the window. Instead, they see Stephen and Miss Lavendar walking slowly toward the stone bench with his arm around her.

Anne and Charlotta understand what has happened. Stephen has asked Miss Lavendar to marry him, and she has accepted. Anne is overjoyed and predicts that there will be a wedding at the stone house before the maple leaves turn red.

## Conversation 1 — Asking to Reconnect

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Stephen: Paul mentioned Miss Lavendar in one of his letters.
Anne: Yes. She and I have become very close.
Stephen: I haven’t **kept in touch with** her for many years.
Anne: But you’ve been thinking about her?
Stephen: More than I expected. I’d like to **get back in touch** if she is willing.
Anne: Do you want me to ask her?
Stephen: Yes. I don’t want to **show up** without warning after so long.
Anne: I’ll speak to her tomorrow and **let you know** what she says.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- keep in touch with someone — continue communicating with someone over time. We moved to different cities but kept in touch.
- get back in touch — contact someone again after a period of no communication. I got back in touch with an old friend last year.
- show up — arrive at a place, sometimes unexpectedly. He showed up without calling first.
- let someone know — tell or inform someone. Let me know when you arrive.

## Conversation 2 — Waiting for an Answer

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Charlotta: How long have they been in there?
Anne: Nearly an hour. I’m trying not to **read too much into** the silence.
Charlotta: What if Stephen leaves without asking her?
Anne: We’ll have to **wait and see**.
Charlotta: I hate not knowing what is happening.
Anne: So do I, but we shouldn’t **listen in**.
Charlotta: You’re right. We’d better keep ourselves busy.
Anne: Exactly. Let’s **pass the time** by finishing the silver.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- read too much into something — give something more meaning than it may really have. Don’t read too much into one short message.
- wait and see — delay judgment until more information becomes available. We don’t know the result yet, so we’ll wait and see.
- listen in — secretly or unintentionally listen to a private conversation. It would be rude to listen in.
- pass the time — do something to make a period of waiting feel shorter. We played cards to pass the time.
