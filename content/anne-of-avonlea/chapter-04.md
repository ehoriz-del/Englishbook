# Chapter 4 — Different Opinions

## Story Summary

On the evening before the new school year begins, Anne walks part of the way home with Jane Andrews. At the Birch Path they meet Gilbert Blythe, and the three young teachers stop to talk about the following day. Jane will teach in Newbridge, Gilbert in White Sands, and Anne in Avonlea. Anne feels that Jane and Gilbert have one advantage: their pupils do not already know them. She worries that her former schoolmates may not respect her as a teacher.

The conversation soon turns to discipline. Jane believes that the main job of a teacher is to keep order and says plainly that she will whip pupils who refuse to obey her. Anne is shocked. She remembers that Miss Stacy maintained excellent order without whipping her students, while Mr. Phillips often used physical punishment and still failed to control the school. Anne insists that she wants to manage her pupils in a different way.

Anne hopes to win the children’s affection so that they will want to cooperate with her. Jane challenges this idea by asking what Anne will do if kindness does not work. Anne admits that she may sometimes have to punish a student, but she would rather keep a child in after school, assign lines to write, or speak to the student kindly and firmly than use physical punishment.

When Jane asks Gilbert for his opinion, he takes a middle position. He agrees with Anne that there are usually better ways to manage children, but he believes corporal punishment may occasionally be necessary as a last resort. His attempt to acknowledge both sides satisfies neither Jane nor Anne. Jane remains convinced that whipping is the quickest way to deal with misbehavior, while Anne repeats that she does not believe it is either right or necessary.

Anne explains that she wants to find and develop the good in each child. She says that influencing her pupils well matters more to her than simply producing high examination results. Jane, however, points out that the school inspector will judge the students on their academic performance. Anne replies that she would rather have former pupils remember her as someone who truly helped them than see her name on an official roll of honor.

After they part, Anne walks slowly back toward Green Gables, thinking seriously about the responsibility she will take on the next morning. When she hears Mrs. Rachel Lynde inside the house, she suspects that Mrs. Lynde has come to give her more advice. Instead of going in, Anne decides to visit Mr. Harrison.

Mr. Harrison immediately teases Anne about school discipline. He assumes she will use switches or a strap and is astonished when she says she intends to govern through affection rather than physical punishment. He argues that human nature has not changed and predicts that Anne will eventually lose her temper and punish one of her pupils. Anne refuses to give up her plan and says she will at least try her own method first.

By the end of the evening, all the conflicting advice has made Anne more anxious. She sleeps badly and appears so pale at breakfast that Marilla gives her ginger tea. Anne finally admits what is troubling her: she is afraid she may fail. Marilla responds practically, reminding her that she cannot expect to teach the children everything or correct all their faults immediately. Anne then prepares to face her first day as Avonlea’s teacher.

## Conversation 1 — Three New Teachers

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Jane: For me, teaching mostly **comes down to** keeping order. If students ignore the rules, there have to be consequences.
Anne: I agree that we need order, but I’d rather **win them over** than make them afraid of me.
Jane: And what if that doesn’t work?
Gilbert: I can **see both sides**. Most problems can be handled without physical punishment, but there may be rare exceptions.
Anne: I’d still rather **talk it through** with a student first.
Gilbert: That makes sense. Punishment should be a **last resort**, not the first response.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- come down to — have one main or essential cause, issue, or point. In the end, the decision comes down to cost and timing.
- win someone over — persuade someone to support, trust, or like you. She was skeptical at first, but the presentation won her over.
- see both sides — understand the arguments or feelings on each side of a disagreement. I see both sides, so I’m finding it hard to choose.
- talk something through — discuss a problem or decision carefully until it becomes clearer. Before we decide, let’s sit down and talk it through.
- last resort — the final option used only after other solutions have failed. Canceling the event should be a last resort.

## Conversation 2 — Anne and Mr. Harrison

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Mr. Harrison: So you’re really going to **go without** a switch or a strap?
Anne: Yes. I’m going to **stick with** my plan and try a different approach.
Mr. Harrison: What happens when a student **pushes you too far**?
Anne: I’ll deal with it, but I don’t want to **lose my temper**.
Mr. Harrison: You may **change your mind** once school actually starts.
Anne: Maybe. But I want to **give my approach a fair chance** first.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- go without — manage without having or using something. My phone was being repaired, so I had to go without it for two days.
- stick with — continue using or supporting a plan, decision, or choice. The new routine is difficult, but I’m going to stick with it for a month.
- push someone too far — continue pressuring or annoying someone until they reach their limit. He is usually patient, but don’t push him too far.
- lose your temper — suddenly become angry and stop speaking or behaving calmly. I was frustrated, but I tried not to lose my temper.
- change your mind — make a different decision after reconsidering something. I was going to stay home, but I changed my mind and went with them.
- give something a fair chance — try something seriously before deciding whether it works or is worthwhile. The new system feels unfamiliar, but let’s give it a fair chance.
