# Chapter 5 — A Full-fledged Schoolma’am

## Story Summary

Anne reaches the Avonlea school on the first morning of September feeling much more nervous than she wants her pupils to know. The children are already seated when she enters, and their bright, curious faces make her suddenly aware that she is no longer one of the students. She is now responsible for the entire room.

The night before, Anne had stayed up late preparing an opening speech about cooperation, learning, and the kind of school community she hoped to build. She had memorized it carefully. But when she stands in front of the class, every word disappears from her mind. After several uncomfortable seconds, she simply asks the pupils to take out their Testaments and begins the day.

As the students read, Anne looks around the room and notices the children who are new to Avonlea school. Anthony Pye immediately catches her attention because he watches her with a dark, unfriendly expression. Anne is determined to win his affection, partly because the Pyes have a reputation for being difficult.

Another new pupil is Paul Irving. He is ten years old but looks younger, with a thoughtful, unusually sensitive expression. When Anne smiles at him, he smiles back warmly. Without speaking, Anne feels an immediate connection with him and senses that he may understand imagination in a way many other children do not.

The first school day passes in a blur. Most of the children behave well, but Anne has two discipline problems. Morley Andrews is caught playing with a pair of trained crickets in the aisle. Anne makes him stand on the platform for an hour and takes the crickets away, later releasing them outside.

Anthony Pye creates the second problem by pouring water down Aurelia Clay’s neck. Anne keeps him inside at recess and speaks to him about how a gentleman should behave. She is kind but firm, exactly as she had planned. Anthony listens without showing any sign that her words have affected him and leaves with a scornful whistle.

By the time school ends, Anne is exhausted and discouraged. Nothing disastrous has happened, but the strain of the first day makes her wonder whether she will ever truly enjoy teaching. Before she can leave, Mrs. H. B. Donnell arrives with a complaint. She insists that Anne has pronounced the family name incorrectly and also demands that her son be called St. Clair instead of Jacob, even though the boy himself prefers Jacob.

After Mrs. Donnell leaves, Anne starts for home. Near the Birch Path she finds Paul Irving waiting for her with a small bunch of wild orchids called rice lilies. He tells Anne that he brought them because he thought she would like them and because he likes her. His simple gift immediately lifts Anne’s spirits.

At Green Gables, Marilla asks how the day went, but Anne says she is still too close to the experience to judge it clearly. Later Mrs. Rachel Lynde reports that she questioned the children and almost all of them said they liked their new teacher. The one exception is Anthony Pye. Anne refuses to be discouraged and says she still intends to win him over with patience and kindness.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- draw a blank — suddenly be unable to remember something you know. I knew her name, but when I introduced her, I completely drew a blank.
- pull yourself together — calm yourself and regain control of your emotions. Take a minute to pull yourself together before the presentation.
- settle in — become comfortable in a new place, situation, or routine. It took me a few days to settle in at my new job.
- stand out — be especially noticeable or impressive. One candidate really stood out during the interviews.
- get through — successfully complete or survive something difficult. I was nervous, but I got through the presentation.

## Conversation 2 — After School

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Marilla: You look exhausted. Was the day that bad?
Anne: Not exactly. I just feel like I **got off to a rough start**.
Marilla: First days are rarely perfect. You’ll learn as you go.
Anne: Anthony Pye doesn’t like me at all, but I’m going to **keep at it**.
Marilla: Good. Give yourself some time.
Anne: Paul’s flowers helped. A small thing like that can really **turn your day around**.
Marilla: Then perhaps all that patience will **pay off** sooner than you expect.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- get off to a rough start — begin a situation with difficulties or problems. The project got off to a rough start, but things are improving now.
- learn as you go — gain knowledge or skill while actually doing something. I had never managed a team before, so I learned as I went.
- keep at it — continue trying even when something is difficult. Your pronunciation is improving, so keep at it.
- turn something around — change a bad situation into a better one. A short break helped turn my afternoon around.
- pay off — produce a good result after effort, patience, or investment. All the extra practice finally paid off.
