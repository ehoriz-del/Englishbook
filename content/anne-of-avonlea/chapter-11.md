# Chapter 11 — Facts and Fancies

## Story Summary

Anne writes a long letter to her former Queen’s Academy friend Stella, telling her that teaching is much more interesting than she expected. Jane finds schoolwork monotonous, but Anne says something amusing happens almost every day. Her pupils often give answers that are completely different from what she expects, and she has to control her laughter until she gets home.

She gives Stella several examples. Jimmy Andrews cannot spell “speckled,” but says he knows what it means because it describes St. Clair Donnell’s freckled face. When Anne asks Lottie Wright how many candies she would have if she held three in one hand and two in the other, Lottie answers, “a mouthful.” Other pupils produce equally surprising ideas in history, nature study, and geography.

Anne says one of the hardest parts of teaching is getting children to express what they really think. During a stormy dinner hour, she asks the class what they want most. Some children want toys, ponies, or skates, but others give unexpected answers. Hester Boulter wants to wear her Sunday dress every day. Hannah Bell wants to be good without having to make an effort. Marjory White says she wants to be a widow because she thinks it would allow her to avoid both being called an old maid and being ordered around by a husband. Sally Bell says she wants a honeymoon, believing it is a special kind of bicycle.

On another day, Anne asks the younger pupils to tell her the naughtiest thing they have ever done. Their confessions include setting fire to carded wool, spending missionary money on candy, eating berries in a graveyard, and sliding down a roof in Sunday clothes. Anne includes these stories in her letter because she enjoys hearing the children’s own explanations of their behavior.

Anne then describes a writing assignment. She asks the fourth class to write real letters to her about any interesting person, place, or experience they choose. Ned Clay writes about cats. St. Clair Donnell describes the Avonlea Hall and points out that its bright blue color is what makes it strange.

Annetta Bell submits an unusually romantic letter praising Anne in elaborate language. Anne knows that Annetta could not have written it alone. The next day, she asks Annetta about it privately. Annetta admits that she copied parts of old love letters her mother had received from a former admirer, changing words so that the letter would appear to be written to a teacher. Anne explains that it is wrong to copy another person’s writing and present it as your own.

Anne saves Paul Irving’s letter for last because she considers him an unusually imaginative child. Paul writes about the “rock people” he imagines living along the shore. He describes Nora, the Twin Sailors, and the Golden Lady of the cave. In one adventure, the oldest Twin Sailor takes him across the sea to the sunset in an enchanted boat. Paul ends the letter by carefully adding that the story is not really true.

## Conversation 1 — Getting Students to Share Ideas

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Anne: The hardest part isn’t teaching facts. It’s getting students to **open up** about what they really think.
Gilbert: Do they usually **come up with** interesting answers?
Anne: Very interesting — sometimes so unexpected that I have to keep from laughing.
Gilbert: At least their answers **bring out** their personalities.
Anne: Exactly. I want them to learn how to **put their ideas into words**, not just memorize information.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- open up — begin to talk more freely about your thoughts or feelings. It took him a while to open up about what was bothering him.
- come up with — think of or produce an idea, answer, or plan. We need to come up with a better way to organize the schedule.
- bring something out — make a quality, feeling, or characteristic become more noticeable. Working with children really brings out her patience.
- put something into words — express an idea or feeling clearly in language. I know how I feel, but it is difficult to put it into words.

## Conversation 2 — Annetta’s Letter

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Anne: Annetta, I need to ask where this letter **came from**.
Annetta: I copied parts of some old letters I found at home.
Anne: You can’t **pass someone else’s writing off as** your own.
Annetta: I didn’t think you would **find out**.
Anne: I’m glad you told me. Next time, **stick to** your own words, even if they are simple.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- come from — have a particular source or origin. Do you know where this information came from?
- pass something off as something — falsely present something as being different from what it really is. He tried to pass the copied design off as his own work.
- find out — discover or learn information that was previously unknown. We found out about the change this morning.
- stick to something — continue using, following, or doing something instead of changing. Keep the explanation simple and stick to the facts.
