# Chapter 21 — Sweet Miss Lavendar

## Story Summary

School begins again in September, and Anne returns to the Avonlea classroom with more experience than she had a year earlier. She still cares deeply about being a good teacher, but she no longer expects every lesson or every child to fit one perfect theory. Davy and Dora are now among her youngest pupils. Davy enjoys school immediately and is pleased to sit beside Milty Boulter. Dora says she likes school too, but on the first evening she becomes afraid to go upstairs alone.

Anne soon discovers the reason. Dora’s older seatmate, Mirabel Cotton, has spent the day telling her stories about dead relatives, including an uncle who was supposedly seen walking through the house after he had been buried. Dora cannot stop thinking about the story. Anne stays with her until she falls asleep, and the next day she speaks privately to Mirabel. She does not punish her harshly, but she makes it clear that frightening stories should not be told to younger children who may take them seriously.

By October, Anne has settled back into the rhythm of school. One Friday, Diana receives an invitation from Ella Kimball to come to tea the next afternoon and meet a visiting cousin. No horse is available, so Anne suggests that they walk through the woods and take a route she believes will lead them to the West Grafton road.

The next afternoon they set out through brilliant autumn woods. Anne becomes so absorbed in the beauty of the day that she takes the wrong branch at a fork in the road. Instead of reaching West Grafton, the girls find themselves on the base line road in Middle Grafton. They are already late, but Diana decides they may as well continue rather than turn back.

At another fork, Anne and Diana see a narrow lane leading through tall fir trees. Because they are unsure which road to take, they decide to follow the lane and ask for directions. At the end stands a small stone house surrounded by an old-fashioned garden. Diana recognizes it as Echo Lodge, the home of Miss Lavendar Lewis.

Inside, the girls meet Charlotta the Fourth, the young girl who helps Miss Lavendar. The table has been prepared for six people, and Anne assumes guests are expected. When Miss Lavendar appears, however, she explains that no one was coming. She had felt lonely and had simply pretended she was having a tea party.

Anne is delighted to learn that Miss Lavendar also enjoys imagining things. Miss Lavendar invites Anne and Diana to stay, and the three quickly become comfortable together. After tea, Miss Lavendar shows them the echoes across the river and explains why her young helpers have all been called “Charlotta.”

Miss Lavendar’s old furniture, pale blue china, flowers, and quiet rooms make Echo Lodge feel separate from ordinary village life. During tea, she explains that several young helpers have all been called “Charlotta” in her mind because they looked alike. The current helper is actually named Leonora, but Miss Lavendar calls her Charlotta the Fourth.


As the girls prepare to leave, Anne casually mentions Paul Irving. Miss Lavendar realizes that Paul is Stephen Irving’s son and briefly loses her cheerful expression. Anne remembers too late that Miss Lavendar and Stephen were once engaged. Miss Lavendar gives each girl lavender and invites them back. As Anne and Diana walk away, they look back and see her sitting alone in the garden.

## Conversation 1 — Losing the Way

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Diana: I’m pretty sure we **took a wrong turn** back there.
Anne: I think you’re right. I was paying more attention to the trees than the road.
Diana: We could **turn back**, but we’ve already walked a long way.
Anne: Let’s keep going for a few minutes and see where this lane **leads to**.
Diana: What if we get even farther off course?
Anne: Then we’ll **ask around** as soon as we find a house.
Diana: All right. Since we’ve **come this far**, we may as well find out what’s ahead.
Anne: Exactly. Sometimes a wrong turn can still lead somewhere interesting.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- take a wrong turn — choose the wrong road or direction. We took a wrong turn and ended up several miles from the hotel.
- turn back — return in the direction you came from. The weather got worse, so we decided to turn back.
- lead to — take someone or something to a place or result. This path leads to the lake.
- ask around — ask several people in order to get information. I’ll ask around and see if anyone knows a reliable plumber.
- come this far — reach a point after making a considerable effort or journey. We’ve come this far, so we shouldn’t quit now.

## Conversation 2 — Meeting Miss Lavendar

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Anne: I expected Miss Lavendar to be very formal, but we **hit it off** almost immediately.
Diana: She certainly knows how to **make people feel at home**.
Anne: I think it helped that she was willing to **open up** about her imaginary tea party.
Diana: I was surprised she admitted that to people she had just met.
Anne: So was I, but it made her seem more genuine.
Diana: And you clearly **took to** her right away.
Anne: I did. By the end of the afternoon, I didn’t want to **tear myself away**.
Diana: We should definitely go back and see her again.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- hit it off — become friendly with someone very quickly. We met at a conference and hit it off right away.
- make someone feel at home — make someone feel relaxed, welcome, and comfortable. They made us feel at home from the moment we arrived.
- open up — begin to talk more freely about personal thoughts or feelings. He finally opened up about what had been worrying him.
- take to someone — begin to like someone naturally and fairly quickly. The children took to their new teacher almost immediately.
- tear yourself away — make yourself leave something enjoyable even though you would rather stay. I had to tear myself away from the conversation to catch my train.
