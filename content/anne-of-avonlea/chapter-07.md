# Chapter 7 — The Pointing of Duty

## Story Summary

One mild October evening, Anne is trying to write down some of her thoughts after finishing her schoolwork. Gilbert arrives at Green Gables and finds her frustrated with the difficulty of putting her ideas into words. Anne explains that teaching leaves her little free time, especially after she has corrected exercises and compositions.

Gilbert tells her that she is doing well as a teacher, but Anne says Anthony Pye still does not like or respect her. Anthony usually obeys, yet he does it with an attitude that makes Anne feel he is only tolerating her authority. She has tried patience and kindness but has not yet been able to win his confidence.

Anne is much happier with Paul Irving, whom she considers unusually imaginative and gifted. Gilbert says he also enjoys teaching and has learned a great deal from the experience. They compare reports they have heard from parents, including criticism of their methods.

Anne tells Gilbert about her continuing difficulty with St. Clair Donnell’s name. The boy originally ignored her whenever she called him St. Clair because he preferred to be called Jake. Anne kept him after school and explained that his mother had specifically asked her to use St. Clair. He finally agreed to answer to the name when Anne used it, although the other boys still call him Jake.

Their conversation turns to the future. Gilbert has decided that he wants to become a doctor. He wants to fight disease, pain, and ignorance and hopes to contribute useful work to the world. Anne says she would like to add beauty and happiness to people’s lives and make their days pleasanter because she has been part of them.

After Gilbert leaves, Anne prepares tea for Marilla, who has gone to visit Mary Keith. Marilla returns tired and worried. Mary is becoming weaker, and the future of Davy and Dora is still unsettled. Their uncle has finally written from a lumber camp, but he cannot take the children until spring, when he expects to marry and have a home for them.

Marilla believes Mary wants her to keep the twins for the winter, although Mary has not directly asked. Marilla hesitates because Davy and Dora are only six and would bring a great deal of responsibility. She is especially worried about Davy, who already has a reputation for mischief.

Anne strongly supports taking the children. She reminds Marilla that the twins need a stable home and that there is no guarantee they would receive good care elsewhere. Marilla finally agrees that taking them is probably her duty. She warns Anne that the decision will also mean extra work for her, especially sewing and mending because Marilla’s eyes prevent her from doing it. Anne dislikes sewing but agrees to do her share.

## Conversation 1 — A Difficult Student

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Gilbert: Anthony still doesn’t seem to respect you?
Anne: No. I keep trying to **get through to** him, but he has already made up his mind about women teachers.
Gilbert: At least the rest of the class is responding well.
Anne: Mostly. I’m also trying to **keep up with** all the grading.
Gilbert: Don’t let yourself **fall behind**. You need some time for your own work too.
Anne: I know. I’m still figuring out how to balance everything.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- get through to someone — make someone understand or accept what you are trying to communicate. I explained it three times before I finally got through to him.
- keep up with — stay at the same pace as work, information, or responsibilities. I receive so many messages that I can barely keep up with them.
- fall behind — fail to keep up with the expected amount of work or progress. She missed a week of class and fell behind.
- figure out — understand something or find a solution after thinking about it. I’m trying to figure out the best way to organize the schedule.

## Conversation 2 — The Twins Need a Home

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Marilla: Their uncle can’t take them until spring, and Mary is getting worse.
Anne: Then perhaps we should **take them in** for the winter.
Marilla: Two six-year-olds are a serious responsibility.
Anne: I know, but someone has to **step in**.
Marilla: I need to **think it over** before I decide.
Anne: Of course. But if we take them, I’ll help **look after** them.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- take someone in — allow someone to live in your home, especially when that person needs help. Her aunt took her in while she looked for a new apartment.
- step in — become involved in order to help, take control, or solve a problem. The manager stepped in when the disagreement became serious.
- think something over — consider something carefully before making a decision. I need a day to think the offer over.
- look after — take care of a person, animal, or thing. Could you look after the dog while we are away?
