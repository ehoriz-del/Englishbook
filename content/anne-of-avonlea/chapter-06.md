# Chapter 6 — All Sorts and Conditions of Men . . . and women

## Story Summary

On a bright September day, Anne and Diana drive along the Newbridge road to collect subscriptions for the Avonlea Village Improvement Society. The society has now been formally organized, with Gilbert as president, Fred Wright as vice-president, Anne as secretary, and Diana as treasurer. Their first major project is to raise enough money to repair, re-shingle, and paint the Avonlea hall.

Anne deliberately chooses one of the more difficult roads to canvass because she feels responsible for the society and thinks she should take on some of the unpleasant work herself. Diana is less enthusiastic. She knows that several people along the route have strong opinions and may not welcome two young women asking for money.

Their first stop is the home of the Andrews sisters. Eliza Andrews refuses to contribute and speaks pessimistically about the society, Avonlea, and life in general. Her sister Catherine is much more encouraging but says little while Eliza is present. After Anne and Diana leave, Catherine secretly runs after them and gives Anne fifty cents from her egg money. She tells the girls that she believes the society can do good.

At Daniel Blair’s house, they find Mr. Blair struggling in the kitchen while his wife is away. He has been told to bake a cake for a visiting relative but has forgotten part of the recipe. Anne helps him mix the cake without asking for anything in return. Afterward, Mr. Blair gives four dollars to the hall fund.

At the home of Mrs. Theodore White, Anne and Diana are asked to walk into the house over a path of newspapers so they will not bring dirt onto the freshly cleaned floors. Mrs. White listens to their request and gives two dollars. Other visits are less successful. Some people complain about the hall, the society, or their neighbors, and Simon Fletcher refuses even to open the door.

The girls become discouraged, but several later households contribute generously. They stop for tea at the Dicksons’ home, where they hear that Lorenzo White has just become the father of a son after having seven daughters. Anne immediately sees that he is likely to be in an unusually generous mood and insists on visiting him before the opportunity passes.

Anne’s judgment is correct. Lorenzo White is delighted about his new son and tells Anne to put him down for one dollar more than the largest donation she has received. When Anne tells him that Daniel Blair gave four dollars, Lorenzo gives five. He then proudly takes Anne and Diana inside to see the new baby.

Although Anne is tired by the time she returns home, she makes one final visit to Mr. Harrison. He says he approves of the Improvement Society but refuses to contribute any money. Anne ends the long day with a collection of very different reactions from the people of Avonlea.

## Conversation 1 — Asking for Support

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Diana: I knew some people would **turn us down**, but I didn’t expect so many strong opinions.
Anne: We only need enough people to **chip in** for the hall repairs.
Diana: Do you think the community will **come through** in the end?
Anne: I do. We can’t **count on** everyone, but several people have already been generous.
Diana: I hope you’re right. Asking for donations is harder than I expected.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- turn someone down — refuse a request, offer, or application. They turned down our request for extra funding.
- chip in — contribute money, time, or effort as part of a group. Everyone chipped in to buy a farewell gift.
- come through — provide the help, support, or result that was needed. We were worried about the deadline, but the team came through.
- count on — rely on someone or something with confidence. You can count on Maya to finish what she promises.

## Conversation 2 — One More Stop

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Diana: We’ve been out all day. Do we really need to visit one more house?
Anne: Lorenzo is in a good mood today, so I think we should **make the most of** the opportunity.
Diana: You always **go out of your way** when you care about a project.
Anne: Maybe, but we’ve already come this far. I want to **follow through**.
Diana: All right. Let’s stop there before we head home.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- make the most of — use an opportunity, situation, or resource as effectively as possible. We only have one afternoon there, so let’s make the most of it.
- go out of your way — make a special or extra effort to do something. She went out of her way to make the new employee feel welcome.
- follow through — do what is necessary to complete something you started or promised. The plan is good, but we need someone who will follow through.
- come this far — reach the current stage after significant time or effort. We’ve come this far, so we shouldn’t quit now.
