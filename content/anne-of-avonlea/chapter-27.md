# Chapter 27 — An Afternoon at the Stone House

## Story Summary

Anne prepares to visit Echo Lodge wearing a new pale green muslin dress. It is the first colored dress she has worn since Matthew’s death. Davy admires it and asks to come with her, but Anne explains that she is walking and that Paul Irving is going too.

Davy says he likes Paul much better than he used to. He is no longer as jealous of Paul’s good behavior and even says he hopes to catch up with him one day. He also tells Anne and Marilla how Paul fell into a brook at school after trying to prove that his naturally curly hair was not put into curl papers by his grandmother.

When Anne and Paul reach the stone house, they find Miss Lavendar and Charlotta the Fourth working in the garden. Miss Lavendar is delighted to see them. She notices how much Paul has grown and is reminded again of his father, Stephen Irving.

Paul is especially excited because the weather is finally calm enough for the echoes across the river. On his earlier visit, the wind had prevented him from hearing them properly. Before going outside, however, Miss Lavendar insists that everyone eat.

During the meal, Paul talks seriously about rules at home, especially his grandmother’s ideas about Sunday behavior. His grandmother does not approve of his imaginary “rock people” on Sundays, while Anne has told him that beautiful thoughts can also be good thoughts. Paul is unsure whose advice he should follow. Anne tells him that his grandmother has more experience and that the difference between their ideas may not be as great as it seems.

After lunch, Paul goes to experiment with the echoes while Anne and Miss Lavendar sit together under the poplar tree. Miss Lavendar tells Anne how sorry she is that Anne will leave for college. She says that making friends can be painful because people eventually go away.

Anne refuses to accept that idea. She reminds Miss Lavendar that letters and vacations will keep them connected. She also notices that Miss Lavendar seems tired and unhappy.

When Charlotta the Fourth appears with news of early strawberries in a nearby pasture, Miss Lavendar becomes more cheerful. Anne and Charlotta go to pick them. While they are alone, Charlotta tells Anne that she is worried about Miss Lavendar. She says Miss Lavendar has not been herself since Paul’s earlier visit and has lost interest in many of the little activities she once enjoyed.

Charlotta is especially disturbed because Miss Lavendar barely reacted when a valuable old bowl was accidentally broken. Anne decides that Miss Lavendar needs more company and promises to spend a week at Echo Lodge during her vacation.

Back at the stone house, they have strawberries and cream for tea. Later, Miss Lavendar sits with Paul and listens to him talk about his imaginary people. Paul notices that she sometimes looks at him as if he reminds her of someone.

Miss Lavendar admits that he does. Paul then tells her that he thinks she would make a wonderful mother. Miss Lavendar tells him about the imaginary boy she has dreamed of for years. Paul understands immediately, and the two talk easily about the dream child. Anne finds them together and is reluctant to interrupt the quiet moment.

## Conversation 1 — Two Different Points of View

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Paul: Sometimes Grandma and you give me different advice.
Anne: That can happen when people **look at things differently**.
Paul: But how do I know which person to listen to?
Anne: First, try to understand what each person **means by** what they say.
Paul: What if the rules still seem different?
Anne: Then you should **take into account** who has more experience in that situation.
Paul: So I shouldn’t assume one person must be completely wrong?
Anne: Exactly. Sometimes two opinions can **come down to** almost the same idea.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- look at something differently — consider the same situation from another point of view. We look at the problem differently, but we both want a solution.
- mean by something — intend a particular meaning when saying something. What do you mean by “more flexible”?
- take something into account — consider an important fact before deciding. We need to take cost into account.
- come down to something — have one main point or basic cause. In the end, the decision comes down to timing.

## Conversation 2 — Noticing That Someone Is Struggling

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Charlotta: I’m worried about Miss Lavendar. She hasn’t been herself lately.
Anne: I noticed that too. She seems to have **lost interest in** things she usually enjoys.
Charlotta: Even when I broke her favorite bowl, she hardly reacted.
Anne: That doesn’t sound like her.
Charlotta: I don’t know how to **cheer her up**.
Anne: Maybe she needs more company and a change in her routine.
Charlotta: Do you think that would help?
Anne: I hope so. I’ll **come stay with** you for a week and we’ll see if she **comes around**.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- lose interest in something — stop feeling interested in something you once enjoyed. He lost interest in the project after several delays.
- cheer someone up — make someone feel happier. I called her because I wanted to cheer her up.
- come stay with someone — visit and live temporarily in someone’s home. My cousin is coming to stay with us next week.
- come around — gradually return to a better mood, condition, or opinion. She was quiet at first but came around after lunch.
