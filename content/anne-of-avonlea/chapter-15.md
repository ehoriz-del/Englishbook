# Chapter 15 — The Beginning of Vacation

## Story Summary

On a quiet June evening, Anne locks the Avonlea schoolhouse after completing her first school year as a teacher. She has been hired again for the following year, and two months of summer vacation are beginning. She feels satisfied and peaceful as she leaves with a basket of flowers.

Anne has continued visiting Matthew’s grave regularly. Although most people in Avonlea rarely speak of him now, she has not forgotten the quiet man who first gave her the affection and encouragement she had needed as a child. She walks toward the cemetery with flowers for his grave.

At the bottom of the hill, Paul Irving is waiting for her. He is also going to the cemetery. He has geraniums for his grandfather’s grave and white roses that he wants to place nearby in memory of his mother, whose grave is far away. It is exactly three years since his mother died.

Paul tells Anne that he still misses his mother deeply. Anne understands why he does not want the sadness to disappear completely, because forgetting the pain would feel too much like forgetting the person. Paul says his father also misses her, but talking about her is difficult for him.

As they walk, Paul talks about his approaching eleventh birthday. His father has written that he is sending a special present, and Paul suspects that it has already arrived because his grandmother has suddenly begun keeping a bookcase drawer locked. He also jokes about his grandmother’s large servings of porridge and tells Anne about the imaginary “rock people” he described in his school letter.

Paul explains that Anne would not be able to see his own rock people, but he believes she could imagine rock people of her own. He tells her that they are both the kind of people who can create such imaginary worlds.

At the cemetery, Anne puts her flowers on Matthew’s grave. She also visits Hester Gray’s grave. Since discovering Hester’s deserted garden during the spring picnic, Anne has begun placing flowers there too. This time she brings white roses from Hester’s own old garden.

Mrs. Allan meets Anne in the cemetery, and the two walk home together. Mrs. Allan congratulates Anne on completing a successful school year. Anne says she has fallen short of many of the ideals and theories she had when she began teaching, especially because she lost her temper and punished Anthony Pye.

Mrs. Allan tells Anne that people do not always live up perfectly to their ideals, but that ideals are still worth having. Anne says she has had to give up many of her original theories. They also talk about Anne’s future. Anne does not know whether she will ever attend college because Marilla’s eyesight is still limited and Davy and Dora may remain at Green Gables.

Anne says she tries not to become dissatisfied by thinking too much about possibilities that are still far away. She is grateful for her work, Paul, the twins, and her friends. By the time she and Mrs. Allan reach home, Anne is ready to begin the summer vacation without knowing exactly what the future will bring.

## Conversation 1 — Anne and Paul

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Paul: It’s been three years, but I still miss my mother just as much.
Anne: I’m glad you can **open up about** her when you need to.
Paul: I don’t want to forget her. I want to **hold on to** the memories.
Anne: You can do that and still **move forward** with your life.
Paul: I think talking about her helps me **work through** some of the sadness.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- open up about something — talk more freely about a personal thought, feeling, or experience. She finally opened up about why she had been so worried.
- hold on to something — keep something rather than letting it go, physically or emotionally. I still hold on to a few letters from my grandfather.
- move forward — continue with your life or plans after a difficult experience or delay. The project had problems, but we need to learn from them and move forward.
- work through something — deal with a difficult problem or feeling gradually. It took time to work through the disappointment.

## Conversation 2 — Looking Ahead

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Mrs. Allan: You’ve **wrapped up** your first year of teaching.
Anne: Yes, and I’m really **looking forward to** the vacation.
Mrs. Allan: You worked hard, even when things didn’t **live up to** your plans.
Anne: I’ve had to **let go of** quite a few theories.
Mrs. Allan: That doesn’t mean you should give up your ideals.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- wrap something up — finish or complete something. Let’s wrap up the meeting with the final two questions.
- look forward to — feel pleased or excited about something that will happen in the future. I’m looking forward to seeing everyone this weekend.
- live up to something — be as good as expected or meet a standard or ideal. The restaurant did not live up to the excellent reviews.
- let go of something — stop holding onto an idea, expectation, feeling, or object. Sometimes you have to let go of a plan that no longer works.
