# Chapter 3 — Mr. Harrison at Home

## Story Summary

Anne crosses the fields to Mr. Harrison’s house carrying the twenty dollars from the cow sale and the nut cake she hopes will serve as a peace offering. She expects an unpleasant confrontation. When Mr. Harrison sees her approaching, he suddenly disappears into the house and shuts the door, which makes Anne even more nervous. In fact, he is embarrassed about the angry way he spoke to her the day before.

When he lets Anne in, he is surprisingly polite. The visit nearly becomes awkward again when Ginger, his parrot, repeats the insulting phrase Mr. Harrison had used about Anne’s red hair. Mr. Harrison tries to excuse the bird’s language by explaining that Ginger once belonged to his sailor brother and has picked up many bad expressions.

Anne finally tells Mr. Harrison that she has come to confess something about the Jersey cow. He assumes the animal has entered another crop and repeatedly guesses what damage she may have caused. Anne asks him not to interrupt because she is already nervous, and then she tells the whole story.

She explains how she shut Dolly in the pen, later saw a Jersey cow in his oats, chased it out with Diana, and impulsively sold it to Mr. Shearer for twenty dollars. Then she reveals the worst part: when she returned home, Dolly was still in the pen. The cow she sold was Mr. Harrison’s.

Mr. Harrison is astonished but much less angry than Anne expected. He tells her not to worry and admits that he himself can be too quick-tempered. Anne offers him the twenty dollars or Dolly in exchange. Mr. Harrison decides that he would rather take Dolly, since Anne has wanted to get rid of her anyway.

The tension disappears, and Mr. Harrison invites Anne to stay for tea. Anne’s nut cake becomes part of the meal. During their conversation she learns more about Ginger and realizes how much company the parrot provides for Mr. Harrison, despite its terrible language. Mr. Harrison also asks about the Village Improvement Society and says he is interested in seeing what the young people will do.

Anne defends Avonlea when he points out that the village and its people have faults, but their disagreement is friendly rather than hostile. After tea, Anne insists on washing the dishes. As she prepares to leave, Mr. Harrison invites her to visit again from time to time.

Anne returns to Green Gables relieved. The meeting she had expected to be humiliating and unpleasant has turned into a friendly visit, and she believes that she and Mr. Harrison can now get along well as neighbors.

## Conversation 1 — Anne Comes Clean

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Anne: Mr. Harrison, I need to **come clean** about what happened with the cow.
Mr. Harrison: All right. I’ll **hear you out**. What happened?
Anne: I thought the cow in your field was Dolly, so I sold her to Mr. Shearer.
Mr. Harrison: You sold my cow?
Anne: I did, and I’m really sorry. I brought the money, and I want to **make up for** the mistake.
Mr. Harrison: Well, accidents happen. We’ll **work something out**.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- come clean — admit the truth about something you have done, especially when it is uncomfortable. I need to come clean about why the report was late.
- hear someone out — listen to everything someone wants to say before judging or responding. Please hear me out before you make a decision.
- make up for something — do something good to compensate for a mistake, loss, or problem. I stayed late to make up for the time I lost that morning.
- work something out — find a solution to a problem or disagreement. I’m sure we can work something out.

## Conversation 2 — An Unexpectedly Friendly Visit

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Mr. Harrison: You expected me to be furious, didn’t you?
Anne: I did. I thought the whole visit would **turn out** terribly.
Mr. Harrison: I can be too quick to speak sometimes. People just have to **take me as I am**.
Anne: We’ll probably **get along** better if we both make allowances.
Mr. Harrison: Fair enough. You should **drop by** again sometime.
Anne: I’d like that. I think I’m starting to **warm up to** Ginger too — a little.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- turn out — develop or end in a particular way. The meeting turned out much better than I expected.
- take someone as they are — accept a person’s character without expecting them to be different. We don’t agree on everything, but we take each other as we are.
- get along — have a friendly relationship with someone. My new neighbors are easy to get along with.
- drop by — make a short, informal visit. Feel free to drop by after work.
- warm up to someone or something — gradually begin to like or feel comfortable with someone or something. It took me a while to warm up to the new manager.
