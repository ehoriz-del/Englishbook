# Chapter 16 — The Substance of Things Hoped For

## Story Summary

During the summer vacation, Anne is sitting in the Green Gables kitchen reading an exciting letter when Davy comes in asking for bread and butter. While Anne fixes him something to eat, Davy announces that he does not want to go to heaven because he believes heaven is in Simon Fletcher’s attic. Anne eventually discovers that he has misunderstood something Milty Boulter heard at a funeral, and she patiently clears up the confusion.

The letter is from Priscilla Grant. Priscilla’s aunt, the well-known author Charlotte E. Morgan, is visiting Prince Edward Island. If the weather stays fine, Priscilla plans to bring Mrs. Morgan to Green Gables on Thursday for dinner and the afternoon.

Anne is thrilled. She and Diana have read Mrs. Morgan’s books for years and have often imagined what meeting her would be like. Anne immediately starts planning the visit in great detail. Marilla agrees to let Anne prepare the dinner herself but warns her not to make the occasion so elaborate that she creates unnecessary trouble.

Anne plans a formal menu with soup, roast chicken, vegetables, salad, lemon pie with whipped cream, coffee, cheese, and lady fingers. She also invites Mr. and Mrs. Allan and Miss Stacy because she knows they would enjoy meeting Mrs. Morgan.

Diana is nearly as excited as Anne. The girls decide to wear white muslin dresses because many of Mrs. Morgan’s heroines are described that way. Anne also wants the parlor filled with flowers and the dining table decorated with wild roses.

At the same time, Anne becomes worried about seven freckles that have appeared on her nose. Diana suggests trying lemon juice, and Anne follows the advice. Her concern about the freckles becomes another part of the intense preparation for Thursday.

The day before the visit, Anne cleans Green Gables thoroughly, including rooms Mrs. Morgan is unlikely to see. She bakes, prepares her dress, organizes the meal, and tries to keep track of every detail. Marilla quietly watches Anne put far more effort into the occasion than she considers necessary.

Davy remains a possible source of trouble. Anne makes him promise to behave well while the guests are there. In return, she promises to take him and Dora rowing to the shore for a picnic if he keeps out of mischief.

By Wednesday evening, nearly everything is ready. Anne is tired but satisfied, and she goes to bed imagining the long-awaited meeting with Mrs. Morgan.

## Conversation 1 — Planning an Important Visit

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Diana: You’ve been talking about this visit for days. Are you still **looking forward to** it, or are you mostly nervous now?
Anne: Both. I want to **plan everything out** so I’m not rushing around at the last minute.
Diana: That makes sense, but don’t **go overboard**. Mrs. Morgan probably isn’t expecting a formal banquet.
Anne: I know. I just want the afternoon to feel special.
Diana: Then focus on what matters and **set aside** the rest.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- look forward to — feel pleased or excited about something that will happen in the future. I’m looking forward to seeing everyone this weekend.
- plan something out — decide the details and steps of something in advance. We planned the whole trip out before booking anything.
- go overboard — do more than is necessary or reasonable because you become too enthusiastic. The decorations are lovely, but we don’t need to go overboard.
- set something aside — decide not to deal with or use something for the moment, or reserve it for a particular purpose. Let’s set the minor details aside and focus on the main problem.

## Conversation 2 — Keeping Everything Under Control

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Marilla: You’ve given yourself a lot to do before Thursday.
Anne: I know, but I’m trying to **keep on top of** everything.
Marilla: At least Diana is helping.
Anne: Yes. I can **count on** her to handle the flowers and salad.
Marilla: And if something small goes wrong?
Anne: I’ll deal with it. We can still **pull the dinner off** without making every detail perfect.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- keep on top of something — stay informed about or in control of work, responsibilities, or changing details. I use a calendar to keep on top of deadlines.
- count on someone — rely on someone with confidence. I can always count on her when a project gets difficult.
- pull something off — succeed in doing something difficult or impressive. We had very little time, but we managed to pull the event off.
- deal with something — take action to handle a problem or situation. We need to deal with the scheduling issue before Friday.
