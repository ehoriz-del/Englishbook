# Chapter 19 — Just a Happy Day

## Story Summary

Late in August, Anne has one of the quiet days she values most, filled with ordinary pleasures rather than a dramatic adventure. In the morning, she and Diana take Davy and Dora rowing down the pond to the sand shore, where the twins paddle in the water and gather sweet grass.

In the afternoon, Anne walks to the Irving home to visit Paul. Mrs. Irving is away, and Paul is pleased to see Anne because he does not like having tea alone.

Before they go inside, Paul asks Anne a serious question. He has overheard Mary Joe telling another girl that there may be something wrong with him because he talks about imaginary people and unusual ideas. Paul asks Anne whether she thinks he is “wrong in his upper story.”

Anne immediately tells him that she does not. She understands that Paul’s imagination is unusual, but she does not consider it a problem. She is also disturbed that an adult conversation has made him worry so much about himself.

Paul is visibly relieved. Anne tells him that if he ever feels troubled by such comments, he can come to Green Gables and talk to her. Paul trusts Anne because she listens without laughing at the things that matter to him.

Over tea, Paul talks about his grandmother, his father, and his mother, who died when he was young. He shows Anne the portrait of his mother that his father sent for his birthday. The picture hangs where he can see it from his bed.

Paul then explains more of the ideas that Mary Joe finds strange. He imagines the evening star as a lighthouse in fairyland, the new moon as a golden boat carrying dreams, violets as pieces of the sky, and buttercups as old sunshine. Anne follows his thoughts easily because she has always imagined the world in a similar way.

When Anne returns to Green Gables, Davy is jealous that she has spent so much time with Paul. He suggests that there is little point in trying to be good if Anne is going to like Paul better anyway.

Anne does not dismiss his jealousy. She explains that she cares about Paul and Davy in different ways and that Davy does not need to compete with another child for her affection. Davy gradually accepts the explanation.

Later, Anne walks toward the Dryad’s Bubble and meets Gilbert. They talk about the Avonlea Village Improvement Society and the changes that have taken place around the community. Anne also becomes aware that Gilbert is no longer simply the boy she competed with at school; he is growing into a young man.

Their conversation remains friendly and practical, but Gilbert’s private feelings for Anne are becoming deeper. Anne, however, still measures romance against an imaginary ideal and does not recognize what Gilbert feels.

## Conversation 1 — Taking a Hurtful Comment Seriously

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Paul: I know I probably shouldn’t have, but I **took Mary Joe’s comment to heart**.
Anne: I understand why. It would be hard to simply **brush something like that off**.
Paul: I kept wondering if she might be right.
Anne: I’m glad you **brought it up** instead of keeping it to yourself.
Paul: I knew you’d **take me seriously**, even if the question sounded strange.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- take something to heart — be deeply affected by criticism, advice, or a comment. Try not to take one negative review to heart.
- brush something off — treat something as unimportant and refuse to let it bother you. He brushed the criticism off and continued working.
- bring something up — introduce a subject so that it can be discussed. She brought the budget issue up at the end of the meeting.
- take someone seriously — treat someone’s ideas, feelings, or concerns as important and worthy of attention. Good managers take employee concerns seriously.

## Conversation 2 — Davy Compares Himself to Paul

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Davy: When you talk about Paul, I sometimes **feel left out**.
Anne: You don’t need to **compare yourself to** him.
Davy: But he always seems to say the right thing.
Anne: That doesn’t mean you have to **measure up to** Paul in every way.
Davy: Then what should I do?
Anne: Notice the good choices you make and **give yourself credit for** them.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- feel left out — feel excluded or not included. I felt left out when everyone made plans without me.
- compare yourself to someone — judge yourself by measuring your qualities or achievements against another person’s. Constantly comparing yourself to others can be exhausting.
- measure up to someone or something — be as good as a person, standard, or expectation. He worried that his work would not measure up to the team’s expectations.
- give yourself credit for something — recognize that you deserve praise for something you have done. Give yourself credit for how much progress you have made.
