# Chapter 22 — Odds and Ends

## Story Summary

At breakfast the morning after Anne’s visit to Echo Lodge, Marilla asks what Miss Lavendar Lewis is like now. She has not seen her for more than fifteen years but remembers her as a beautiful and unusual young woman. Anne says that Miss Lavendar’s hair is now white, yet her face and manner still seem youthful. She describes her as the kind of person who has never completely grown old inside.

Their conversation is repeatedly interrupted by Davy. He reaches across the table, complains that his spoonful of syrup is too small, and finally lifts his plate to lick up the last drops. Marilla is shocked by his manners. Davy argues that growing boys need more food and that Paul Irving has longer arms because he has had several more years to grow.

Marilla remembers that Miss Lavendar and Stephen Irving were once engaged. The engagement ended suddenly, but Marilla never learned the exact reason. Anne suggests that the trouble may not have been anything dramatic. She has begun to understand that small misunderstandings can sometimes create larger problems than people expect. She also asks Marilla not to tell Mrs. Lynde about the visit because she does not want Miss Lavendar’s private life turned into village gossip.

Marilla also mentions that Mrs. Lynde has less time for other people’s affairs because Thomas Lynde is seriously ill. Although Marilla often jokes about Mrs. Lynde’s strong personality, she admits that Rachel has been a capable and devoted wife. This part of the breakfast conversation reminds Anne that several households around Avonlea are changing in ways that are not yet fully settled.


Later, Davy gets into more trouble because he wants feathers from the rooster’s tail to make a headdress like one Milty Boulter owns. Marilla sees only disobedience, while Anne partly understands his desire. She remembers how strongly she once wanted puffed sleeves simply because other girls had them.

Anne and Marilla agree that Davy has improved since he came to Green Gables. He is still energetic and mischievous, but he gets into less serious trouble now that school gives him work and other boys to play with.

One concern remains: they have heard nothing from Richard Keith, Davy and Dora’s uncle in the West. Months earlier he had been expected to take the children, and Anne worries that any new letter might tell them to send the twins away.

A month later, a letter finally arrives, but it is from a friend of Richard Keith rather than from Richard himself. The friend explains that Richard has died of consumption. In his will, he left two thousand dollars to Marilla in trust for Davy and Dora. The interest can be used for their care until they are grown.

Anne is sorry that Richard has died, but she is relieved that the twins can remain at Green Gables. Marilla is relieved for a practical reason as well: she had wanted to keep them but had worried about the cost as they grew older.

Davy and Dora are delighted when they learn that Green Gables will now be their permanent home. Dora has only one fear. Remembering Mirabel Cotton’s ghost story, she quietly asks Anne whether Uncle Richard might walk around houses after being buried. Anne reassures her.

## Conversation 1 — Looking at Davy More Fairly

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Marilla: Davy has a remarkable ability to **get himself into trouble** before breakfast.
Anne: True, but I think we should **take his age into account** sometimes.
Marilla: You’re always ready to explain why he did something.
Anne: Not excuse it—explain it. There’s a difference.
Marilla: And the rooster feathers?
Anne: He wanted to **fit in with** the other boys. I remember wanting things just because everyone else had them.
Marilla: He still has to **learn from** what happens when he breaks a rule.
Anne: I agree. Understanding him doesn’t mean letting him do whatever he wants.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- get yourself into trouble — become involved in a problem because of your own actions. He got himself into trouble by ignoring the instructions.
- take something into account — consider an important fact before making a judgment or decision. We need to take the weather into account before choosing a date.
- fit in with — feel accepted by a group or behave in a way that matches the group. It took her a few weeks to fit in with the new team.
- learn from something — use an experience to improve future behavior or decisions. We made mistakes, but we learned from them.

## Conversation 2 — News About the Twins

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Anne: We finally **heard from** someone who knew Uncle Richard.
Marilla: Yes. I’m afraid he **passed away** two weeks ago.
Anne: I’m sorry to hear that. I was also afraid the letter might say we had to send the twins away.
Marilla: Instead, he **left money to** them for their care.
Anne: Then they can stay here permanently?
Marilla: Yes. We can **look after** them without worrying so much about the cost.
Anne: I didn’t realize how much I had been worrying until the question was settled.
Marilla: Neither did I. Green Gables would feel very quiet without them now.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- hear from someone — receive a message or other communication from someone. I haven’t heard from her since last week.
- pass away — die; a polite and gentle expression. Her grandfather passed away peacefully last year.
- leave something to someone — arrange for someone to receive money or property after your death. She left the house to her niece.
- look after someone — take care of someone. Could you look after the children while I make dinner?
