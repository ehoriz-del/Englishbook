# Chapter 24 — A Prophet in His Own Country

## Story Summary

In May, Avonlea becomes interested in a set of short local items published in the Charlottetown Daily Enterprise under the name “Observer.” Many people decide that Charlie Sloane must have written them, partly because one item appears to make fun of Gilbert Blythe.

The assumption is wrong. Gilbert and Anne wrote the notes together. They included the joke about Gilbert on purpose so that people would be less likely to suspect him. Two items attract particular attention. One hints that a respected new citizen may soon marry a popular local woman. The other reports that Uncle Abe, an elderly Avonlea man known for making unsuccessful weather predictions, expects a violent thunderstorm on May twenty-third.

Uncle Abe is annoyed by the newspaper item because he says he did not name an exact date. Most people do not take the prediction seriously anyway. His forecasts have been wrong too many times.

Meanwhile, spring continues pleasantly. The Avonlea Village Improvement Society holds an Arbor Day and plants two hundred ornamental trees. At Green Gables, Davy and Dora receive small garden plots. Dora works carefully, but Davy repeatedly digs up his plants to see whether anything is happening below the soil.

On May twenty-third, the weather begins calmly. Later, however, Anne notices a huge black cloud moving rapidly toward Avonlea. It looks far more dangerous than an ordinary shower.

Harmon Andrews drives past the school and warns Anne that the storm is serious. Anne dismisses the pupils early. Children who live farther away are taken to the post office in Mr. Andrews’s wagon, while Anne hurries toward Green Gables with Davy and Dora.

They reach home just in time. The light disappears as the cloud covers the sun, and a violent hailstorm begins. Large hailstones smash windows, heavy branches strike the house, and thunder and lightning continue almost without a pause. Even Marilla, who is usually calm in a crisis, becomes badly frightened.

The storm lasts less than an hour, but the damage is extraordinary. Crops, gardens, apple blossoms, and young trees are cut down or destroyed. Most of the trees planted by the Improvement Society are ruined.

Davy is terrified at the first crash and promises not to fight another boy as planned. Once the danger passes, however, he quickly begins talking normally again. Dora remains calmer throughout the storm, holding Anne’s hand and repeating her prayers.

Afterward, the damage is not limited to Green Gables. Gilbert reports that the storm was severe at White Sands, where several frightened pupils fainted or became hysterical. Around Avonlea, people struggle to replace broken window glass, repair buildings, and understand how such destruction happened so quickly.


Mr. Harrison’s house is struck by lightning. The chimney and floor are damaged, and Ginger’s cage is knocked over. Ginger, the parrot who had insulted Anne many times, is killed.

Anne visits Mr. Harrison afterward and finds him holding the dead bird. She had never liked Ginger, but she understands that the parrot had been Mr. Harrison’s closest companion.

By evening, Gilbert arrives and reports that White Sands was badly hit too. The old Boulter house, which the Improvement Society had wanted removed, has burned down after being struck.

One result is unexpectedly comic: because the storm occurred on the exact date printed in the newspaper, Uncle Abe suddenly becomes famous as a weather prophet. The prediction everyone had brushed aside is now remembered throughout Avonlea.

## Conversation 1 — A Prediction Everyone Ignored

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Gilbert: Most people **brushed off** Uncle Abe’s warning because his earlier predictions were usually wrong.
Anne: And now this one has **turned out to be** completely accurate about the date.
Gilbert: Our little newspaper note also **threw everyone off**. No one seems to suspect us.
Anne: I’m less amused by that now that I’ve seen what the storm did.
Gilbert: I know. The joke has **taken on** a much more serious meaning.
Anne: It’s strange how quickly something harmless can change.
Gilbert: Especially when real events suddenly make people look at it differently.
Anne: I think I’ll be more careful before making fun of predictions in print again.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- brush something off — treat something as unimportant instead of taking it seriously. We brushed the warning off because nothing had happened before.
- turn out to be — eventually prove to be a particular thing or result. The small repair turned out to be a major problem.
- throw someone off — confuse or mislead someone. The false detail threw everyone off.
- take on — begin to have a new quality, meaning, or responsibility. The conversation took on a more serious tone.

## Conversation 2 — Dealing with the Damage

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Anne: I still can’t believe how much the storm **wiped out** in less than an hour.
Marilla: We were fortunate to **come through** it without anyone being hurt.
Anne: The trees, the garden, the windows—there’s damage everywhere.
Marilla: It will take time to **clean up after** a storm like this.
Anne: And Mr. Harrison lost Ginger.
Marilla: That may seem small compared with a farm, but the bird meant a great deal to him.
Anne: I know. I’m going over to see him.
Marilla: Good. Sometimes people need company while they **deal with** a loss.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- wipe something out — destroy or remove something almost completely. The flood wiped out several small farms.
- come through something — survive or successfully get through a difficult experience. She came through the operation well.
- clean up after something — remove or repair the mess caused by an event. It took days to clean up after the storm.
- deal with something — take action to handle a problem, difficulty, or loss. Everyone deals with disappointment differently.
