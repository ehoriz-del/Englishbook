# Chapter 1 — An Irate Neighbor

## Story Summary

Anne Shirley is now sixteen and a half and is preparing to begin work as the teacher at Avonlea school. One August afternoon, she sits outside Green Gables with a book of Virgil, intending to study. Instead, she begins imagining the kind of teacher she hopes to become. In her daydream, she sees herself inspiring students who will grow into successful adults and remember the influence she had on them.

Her thoughts are abruptly interrupted by Mr. J. A. Harrison, the Cuthberts’ new neighbor. He has recently bought the farm beside Green Gables and has already developed a reputation in Avonlea for being eccentric, untidy, outspoken, and difficult to please. He lives alone, keeps irregular household habits, and owns a badly behaved parrot named Ginger.

Mr. Harrison is furious because Anne’s Jersey cow, Dolly, has gotten into his oat field again. According to him, this is the third time. At first he assumes the cow belongs to Marilla, but Anne explains that Dolly is hers. She apologizes, yet she also points out that the fence between the two properties is partly Mr. Harrison’s responsibility and is not in very good condition.

The disagreement quickly becomes personal. Mr. Harrison makes an insulting remark about Anne’s red hair and accuses her of wasting time with a novel instead of watching the cow. Anne immediately fires back with a remark about his baldness. Afterward, however, she regains her composure. She tells him that she understands why he is angry and promises that Dolly will not get into his oats again. Mr. Harrison leaves, still irritated but somewhat less aggressive.

When Marilla returns, she and Anne discuss the problems of managing the farm before Mr. Barry takes it over. Their conversation then turns to Mary Keith, who is seriously ill, and to her six-year-old twins, Davy and Dora. Their future is uncertain because their mother may not recover and their uncle in British Columbia has not yet replied about taking them.

Mrs. Rachel Lynde soon arrives and, as usual, already knows about the trouble with Mr. Harrison. She also brings news about several newcomers to Avonlea. Two of them may soon become Anne’s pupils: Anthony Pye and Paul Irving. Paul’s father, Stephen Irving, once had a romantic relationship with Lavendar Lewis, who now lives alone at Echo Lodge.

Mrs. Lynde then questions Anne about another plan: the proposed Avonlea Village Improvement Society. Anne and Gilbert have been discussing ways to make the village more attractive, including removing an abandoned old house that has become an eyesore. Mrs. Lynde warns Anne that she may have her hands full once school begins and that people do not always appreciate attempts to improve their surroundings. Anne, however, has already set her mind on forming the society. The chapter closes with more school news: Anne learns that her friend Priscilla Grant will be teaching nearby in Carmody.

## Conversation 1 — Anne and Mr. Harrison

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Mr. Harrison: I’m not going to **put up with** your cow getting into my oats again.
Anne: I’m sorry she got in, but the fence on your side could use some repair.
Mr. Harrison: That’s beside the point. You need to **keep an eye on** your own animals.
Anne: I can **make allowance for** your being upset, but there’s no reason to insult me.
Mr. Harrison: Then make sure it doesn’t happen again.
Anne: I will. You have my word.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- put up with — tolerate something annoying or unpleasant. I can put up with the noise for an hour, but not all day.
- keep an eye on — watch someone or something carefully for a period of time. Could you keep an eye on the soup while I answer the door?
- make allowance for — be more understanding because you recognize a difficult circumstance. We should make allowance for the fact that she is new to the job.
- give someone a piece of your mind — tell someone angrily and directly that you disapprove of what they did. He was ready to give the contractor a piece of his mind.

## Conversation 2 — A New Project for Avonlea

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Mrs. Lynde: You’ll **have your hands full** once school starts. Why **take on** another project?
Anne: Because I’ve **set my mind on** starting the Improvement Society.
Marilla: You may **run into** resistance. People can be particular about change.
Anne: Then we’ll start small. If we agree on a sensible plan, we can **carry it through** gradually.
Mrs. Lynde: Well, once you’ve made up your mind, there’s usually no stopping you.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- take on — accept a new task, responsibility, or challenge. I don’t want to take on more work until this project is finished.
- have your hands full — be extremely busy dealing with responsibilities or problems. With two deadlines this week, I already have my hands full.
- set your mind on — decide firmly that you want to do or achieve something. Once Maya sets her mind on a goal, she rarely gives up.
- run into — encounter an unexpected problem, difficulty, or person. We ran into a few technical problems during the update.
- carry something through — complete a plan or task despite difficulty. The idea is promising, but we need enough time to carry it through properly.
