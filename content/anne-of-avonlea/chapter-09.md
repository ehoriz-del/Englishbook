# Chapter 9 — A Question of Color

## Story Summary

One November evening, Anne visits Mr. Harrison and finds him angry with Mrs. Rachel Lynde. She has asked him for money toward a carpet for the church vestry, and he has responded by calling her a busybody. Anne tells him that people often dislike one another because they do not understand each other.

Mr. Harrison argues that he believes in telling people the truth. Anne answers that he tends to tell only the unpleasant part of the truth. She points out that he has often commented on her red hair but has never mentioned any of her features that he might consider attractive. She also reminds him that Mrs. Lynde may be outspoken but is generous and willing to help people in need.

The conversation turns to the Avonlea Village Improvement Society. Anne tells Mr. Harrison that the society has raised enough money to re-shingle and paint the hall. The members have chosen green for the walls and dark red for the roof. Roger Pye is responsible for obtaining the paint, and Joshua Pye has been hired to do the work because the Pye families made their donations conditional on his receiving the job.

Joshua finishes shingling the roof and begins painting the hall. Because the building stands on the muddy lower road and is hidden by trees, almost nobody sees the work while it is being done.

When Mrs. Lynde finally drives past the hall after Joshua has finished, she is shocked. Instead of the planned green, the building has been painted a deep, brilliant blue, while the roof is red. She immediately spreads the news through Avonlea.

Gilbert and Fred hurry to Green Gables, where Anne, Diana, and Jane are already upset. The group discovers that the mistake probably came from a confusion over paint numbers. The desired green was number 147, but Roger Pye apparently obtained number 157 after receiving the number through his son John Andrew. Father and son each insist that the other is responsible for the error.

Anne is convinced that the bright blue hall will make the Improvement Society a public joke. The members also learn that they still have to pay Joshua Pye because he was hired to paint the building and was never specifically told what color it was supposed to be. A local magistrate confirms that Joshua cannot fairly be held financially responsible.

The reaction in Avonlea is not what Anne expects. Instead of laughing at the Improvers, many residents become angry on their behalf because they had contributed money to the project. Public support for the society increases. Major Spencer offers to improve the roadside in front of his farm, and Mrs. Hiram Sloane promises to keep her cow away from the flower bed the society hopes to create.

Mr. Harrison tries to encourage Anne by pointing out that the hall has at least been repaired and re-shingled. Anne remains unhappy about the color, and the bright blue hall becomes well known throughout the surrounding settlements.

## Conversation 1 — Telling the Whole Truth

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Anne: You and Mrs. Lynde seem to **rub each other the wrong way**.
Mr. Harrison: I just **speak my mind**. People know exactly where I stand.
Anne: But you usually **point out** what you dislike and leave out the good parts.
Mr. Harrison: Maybe I **come across as** harsher than I mean to.
Anne: Sometimes the way you say something matters as much as the fact itself.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- rub someone the wrong way — irritate or annoy someone, often because of personality or manner. His sarcastic tone really rubs me the wrong way.
- speak your mind — say honestly and directly what you think. She is not afraid to speak her mind in meetings.
- point out — draw attention to a fact, detail, or problem. He pointed out an error in the final report.
- come across as — give other people a particular impression. The email came across as more critical than I intended.

## Conversation 2 — The Blue Hall

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Diana: I still can’t believe the hall **ended up** blue.
Anne: Neither can I. We planned every detail, and one wrong number **threw everything off**.
Gilbert: People are angry about the mistake, but they’re also starting to **back us up**.
Anne: I thought everyone would laugh at us.
Gilbert: Instead, they want us to **keep going** with the society.
Anne: Then I suppose we’ll have to focus on the next project.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- end up — finally be in a particular situation, place, or condition, often unexpectedly. We took the wrong road and ended up on the other side of town.
- throw something off — disrupt a plan, schedule, calculation, or expected result. One late delivery threw the entire schedule off.
- back someone up — support someone or confirm what they are saying. My manager backed me up during the discussion.
- keep going — continue despite difficulty, disappointment, or tiredness. The first attempt failed, but we decided to keep going.
