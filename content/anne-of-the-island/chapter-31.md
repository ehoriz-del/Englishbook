# Chapter 31 — Anne to Philippa

## Story Summary

Anne writes a long letter to Philippa from Valley Road, where she is spending the summer teaching school. She is staying at a small house called Wayside with Janet Sweet, the woman Mrs. Skinner mentioned during Anne’s ride from the station.

Anne immediately likes Janet. Janet is a cheerful, kind woman with soft brown hair touched with gray, rosy cheeks, and bright blue eyes. She is also an excellent cook. Anne jokes that Janet is the kind of old-fashioned cook who does not worry about anyone’s digestion as long as she can serve plenty of rich food.

Janet likes Anne too, partly because she once had a younger sister named Anne who died. When Anne first arrived, Janet was surprised to discover that Anne had red hair because Janet had imagined her as dark-haired like her own sister. Anne briefly feels offended by the word “red,” but quickly decides not to judge Janet for it.

Wayside is a small white house set in a hollow below the road. An orchard and flower garden grow together between the road and the house. The front walk is edged with clam shells, Virginia creeper climbs over the porch, and moss grows on the roof.

Anne’s bedroom is very small and opens off the parlor. Over the bed hangs a gloomy picture of Robert Burns at Highland Mary’s grave. Anne jokes that the picture may be responsible for her strange dreams. On her first night there, she even dreams that she has lost the ability to laugh.

The parlor is also small and heavily shaded by a large willow tree. It contains chair covers, bright floor mats, neatly arranged books and cards, dried grasses, and five preserved coffin plates on the mantel. The plates belonged to members of Janet’s family and to a hired man who once died in the house. Anne finds them so gloomy that she jokes they may eventually drive her mad.

Even so, Anne tells Janet she likes the house. This pleases Janet, especially because the regular teacher, Esther Haythorne, had criticized the shaded rooms and the feather beds. Esther also prefers a very plain diet, while Anne happily eats Janet’s generous breakfasts and dinners.

Janet tells Anne that she may use the parlor whenever young men come to visit. Anne doubts that many will. The only young man she has noticed nearby is Sam Toliver, a tall, thin hired boy from the neighboring farm.

One evening Sam sits on the garden fence for about an hour while Anne and Janet do needlework on the porch. He says very little. He offers Anne a peppermint, recommends peppermints for a cold, and comments on the number of grasshoppers. Anne finds the visit amusing because conversation with Sam is almost impossible.

Anne also compares herself with Esther, the teacher she is replacing. Janet had found Esther difficult to please because Esther worried about health rules, disliked heavy meals, and objected to the dark parlor and feather bed. Anne is much more willing to accept the house exactly as it is. She enjoys Janet’s cooking, sleeps comfortably in the feather bed, and treats the unusual decorations as something to laugh about rather than criticize. This difference helps Janet become comfortable with Anne very quickly.

Anne then tells Phil that there is a romance in Valley Road. She jokes that she seems to have a strange connection with love stories involving older people. She remembers how she helped Miss Lavendar and Stephen Irving come together and how she encouraged other slow-moving courtships.

This time, however, Anne says she intends to remain only an observer. The situation concerns Janet, although Anne does not yet explain all the details in the letter.

Anne admits that she already tried once to help the romance along and made a terrible mistake. Because of that experience, she has decided not to interfere again.

She ends the letter by promising to tell Phil the whole story when they meet. For now, Phil receives Anne’s lively description of Wayside, Janet, Sam Toliver, and the mysterious romance that Anne is trying very hard not to manage.

## Conversation 1 — Settling Into Wayside

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Janet: I hope the room is comfortable enough for you.
Anne: It is. I’m already **settling in**.
Janet: Esther never liked that room.
Anne: Really?
Janet: She couldn’t **put up with** the feather bed or the shade.
Anne: Neither bothers me.
Janet: Then I think we’ll **get along** just fine.
Anne: I think so too.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- settle in — become comfortable in a new place. I settled in quickly after moving.
- put up with — tolerate something unpleasant or annoying. I can’t put up with that noise for long.
- get along — have a friendly relationship. We get along very well.
- bother someone — annoy or trouble someone. The cold weather doesn’t bother me.

## Conversation 2 — Sam’s Visit

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Anne: Sam sat on the fence for nearly an hour.
Janet: Did he say much?
Anne: Hardly anything. He just **showed up** with peppermints.
Janet: That sounds like Sam.
Anne: I kept trying to **keep the conversation going**.
Janet: And?
Anne: It never really **got off the ground**.
Janet: You’ll have to get used to his long silences.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- show up — arrive or appear. He showed up without calling first.
- keep something going — continue something without letting it stop. She tried to keep the conversation going.
- get off the ground — begin successfully. The discussion never really got off the ground.
- get used to — become familiar or comfortable with something. You’ll get used to the quiet eventually.
