# Chapter 12 — “Averil’s Atonement”

## Story Summary

Anne begins working seriously on the short story she mentioned to Diana. One evening, while Anne and Diana are sitting near the brook, Anne explains that she has finally found a plot that seems right for a heroine named Averil Lester. She has already written several pages and has spent a great deal of time choosing names for the other characters.

Diana wants to help, so Anne allows her to name a small hired boy in the story. Diana first suggests a grand name, Raymond Fitzosborne, but Anne thinks it sounds too elegant for a boy who feeds pigs and does farm work. They finally choose Robert Ray, with Bobby as a shorter name.

Anne says she is not writing mainly to make money. She hopes the story will be published and bring her some recognition. Diana asks whether the story will have a happy ending. Anne says she is not sure. She thinks a sad ending might seem more romantic, but she remembers hearing that unhappy endings are difficult to write well. Diana strongly prefers a happy ending and wants the main couple to marry.

For the next two weeks, Anne works hard on the story. Some days she is excited by a new idea, and on other days she becomes frustrated because the characters do not seem to behave as she planned. She complains that Averil keeps doing unexpected things, which forces Anne to rewrite earlier parts of the story.

When the story is finished, Anne reads it to Diana in the porch room at Green Gables. Diana cries during the sad parts, which pleases Anne, but she is disappointed that Maurice Lennox, the villain, dies at the end. Diana actually likes Maurice better than the hero, Perceval Dalrymple. Anne insists that Maurice must be punished, while Diana suggests that Anne could have allowed him to change.

The story is called “Averil’s Atonement.” Anne asks Diana for honest criticism. Diana says that the scene in which Averil makes a cake feels too ordinary compared with the rest of the story. Anne defends the scene because she thinks it provides humor.

Next Anne reads the story to Mr. Harrison. His comments are much more direct. He says there is too much description and advises Anne to remove many of the long, flowery passages. Anne reluctantly rewrites the story several times and cuts most of them, although she refuses to remove one sunset scene at first.

Mr. Harrison also criticizes the setting and the characters. He says Anne has written about rich city people she does not know and that her characters do not speak like real people. He thinks Perceval talks too much and says Maurice is more interesting. Anne is annoyed by his comments, especially his plain way of talking about her carefully created romantic characters.

Even so, some of Mr. Harrison’s criticism affects her. Anne finally sends the story to a large magazine. Diana is confident that it will be accepted. For a week, Anne imagines how proud she will be if the story appears in print.

Instead, the manuscript comes back with a printed rejection slip. No explanation is given. Diana is angry on Anne’s behalf and tells her to send it somewhere else. Anne removes the sunset description and sends the story to the Canadian Woman.

The second magazine rejects it quickly as well. This time Anne gives up. She puts the manuscript in the old trunk where she keeps stories from the Story Club and gives Diana a copy because Diana asks for one.

Later Mr. Harrison asks whether the story was accepted. Anne says no and declares that she will never write another story. Mr. Harrison tells her not to stop completely. He suggests that if she writes again, she should use people and places she knows, make the characters speak more naturally, and avoid making every villain completely bad.

Anne still says she plans to teach after Redmond instead of writing stories. Mr. Harrison then tells her she should think about finding a husband after college. Anne becomes irritated and leaves.

## Conversation 1 — Finishing the Story

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Diana: How is the story **coming along**?
Anne: Slowly. I keep having to **go back over** parts of it.
Diana: Why?
Anne: Averil keeps doing things I didn’t plan.
Diana: Can’t you just **change things around**?
Anne: I can, but then other scenes stop making sense.
Diana: At least you’re **sticking with** it.
Anne: I am. I really want to finish it.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- come along — make progress or develop. How is your new project coming along?
- go back over — review something again. I went back over my notes before the test.
- change something around — rearrange or modify parts of something. We changed the schedule around to make it easier.
- stick with — continue doing something even when it is difficult. She stuck with the course and finished it.

## Conversation 2 — The Rejection

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Diana: The story came back?
Anne: Yes. The editor **turned it down**.
Diana: Are you going to send it somewhere else?
Anne: I might **give it another shot**.
Diana: Good. One rejection doesn’t mean you should **give up**.
Anne: I’ll cut the sunset scene and send it to another magazine.
Diana: That sounds reasonable.
Anne: If they reject it too, I may **put it away** for a while.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- turn something down — reject an offer, request, or piece of work. The magazine turned down my article.
- give something another shot — try something again. I failed once, but I’m going to give it another shot.
- give up — stop trying. Don’t give up after one difficult week.
- put something away — store something and stop using it for the time being. I put the old manuscript away in a drawer.
