# Chapter 5 — Letters from Home

## Story Summary

For about three weeks, Anne and Priscilla continue to feel like strangers at Redmond. Then the different parts of college life suddenly begin to fit together. The professors, classes, studies, social events, and unfamiliar students no longer seem like disconnected pieces. The freshmen develop a strong class identity, complete with their own interests, ambitions, rivalries, and class spirit.

Their confidence rises sharply after the annual Arts Rush, a traditional competition against the Sophomores. The Sophomores have won for the previous three years, but this time the freshmen defeat them. Gilbert plays an important role in the victory by organizing their strategy and introducing tactics that confuse the older students. His leadership earns him the presidency of the freshman class.

Gilbert also receives an invitation to join Lambda Theta, known informally at Redmond as “the Lambs.” Being invited as a freshman is unusual and considered an honor. Before becoming a member, however, Gilbert must complete an initiation. He spends a day walking through Kingsport’s main business streets wearing a sunbonnet and an enormous flowered kitchen apron. Gilbert accepts the embarrassment with humor and even bows politely to women he knows. Charlie Sloane, who has not been invited to join, tells Anne that he would never humiliate himself in such a way.

Anne and Priscilla also become part of Redmond’s social life more quickly than most first-year women, largely because of Philippa Gordon. Phil comes from a wealthy and well-connected Nova Scotia family, and her charm gives her easy access to different social groups. Because she is genuinely fond of Anne and Priscilla and has no interest in excluding people, she takes them with her wherever she goes. As a result, the two Island girls soon meet many more people than they expected.

Phil continues to amuse them. She has many admirers and talks about them constantly, but she is also intelligent and performs well academically despite her busy social life. Even a mathematics professor who dislikes female students cannot defeat her in class. Anne, however, clearly surpasses Phil in English. Anne finds the work of her first year relatively easy because of the serious studying she and Gilbert did during their two years of teaching in Avonlea.

Although Anne enjoys Redmond more each week, she never forgets home. Her happiest moments are often the ones when letters arrive from Avonlea. Before the first batch reaches her, home seems impossibly far away. Once she begins reading the letters, the distance becomes easier to bear. The old life and the new life no longer feel completely separate.

Anne receives letters from Jane Andrews, Ruby Gillis, Diana, Marilla, Mrs. Lynde, and Davy. Jane writes neatly and correctly but manages to say almost nothing Anne wants to know. She describes the weather, her crocheting, a new dress, and her headaches, while ignoring Anne’s questions about the school.

Ruby’s letter is much more emotional. She says everyone misses Anne and asks about the young men at Redmond. Most of the letter describes Ruby’s own admirers. A short postscript bothers Anne, however, because Ruby mentions that Gilbert seems to be enjoying Redmond, judging from his letters. Anne immediately notices that Gilbert has been writing to Ruby. She tells herself he has every right to do so, but the thought still irritates her. What Anne does not know is that Ruby wrote first and Gilbert only replied out of politeness.

Diana’s letter quickly improves Anne’s mood. It contains plenty of news from Avonlea, although Anne thinks there is rather too much about Fred. Marilla’s letter is restrained and practical, with very little gossip or open emotion. Even so, Anne can feel the steady affection of Green Gables behind every line.

Mrs. Lynde sends church and neighborhood news in her usual energetic style. She reports several comic incidents, including an unfortunate visiting minister who ends up riding Mr. Harrison’s troublesome pig before falling off near the brook. She also gives Anne updates about Davy’s behavior and reminds her not to study too hard or neglect warm clothing.

Davy’s letter is full of spelling mistakes, questions, complaints, and strange stories. He asks Anne to tell Marilla not to tie him to the bridge rail when he goes fishing, reports that Jane Andrews is stricter than Anne was as a teacher, and describes various local disasters with complete seriousness. By the time Anne finishes all the letters, Green Gables feels much closer. She is now enjoying Redmond, but the weekly arrival of news from home remains one of the most important parts of her new life.

## Conversation 1 — Settling Into Redmond

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Priscilla: Redmond finally feels less confusing.
Anne: I agree. Things are starting to **fall into place**.
Priscilla: Phil has certainly helped us **fit in** socially.
Anne: She has. She seems to know everyone.
Priscilla: And Gilbert has really **stepped into** a leadership role.
Anne: Winning the Arts Rush certainly helped.
Priscilla: Do you still miss Avonlea as much?
Anne: I miss it, but I’m no longer letting homesickness **take over** my days.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- fall into place — begin to make sense or develop in a satisfactory way. After a confusing first week, everything started to fall into place.
- fit in — feel accepted or comfortable within a group. It took time, but she eventually fit in with her new classmates.
- step into — begin taking on a new role or responsibility. He stepped into a leadership role after the manager left.
- take over — become dominant or gain control. I try not to let anxiety take over before presentations.

## Conversation 2 — Letters From Home

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Phil: You always look happier when the mail arrives.
Anne: Letters from home really **keep me connected** to Avonlea.
Phil: Even when they’re full of ordinary news?
Anne: Especially then. Little details **bring everything back**.
Phil: Do they ever make you more homesick?
Anne: Sometimes, but they also **cheer me up**.
Phil: So they help you balance both parts of your life.
Anne: Exactly. I don’t feel as if I have to **leave one behind** to enjoy the other.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- keep someone connected — help maintain a close relationship or sense of connection. Weekly calls keep me connected to my family.
- bring something back — make a memory, feeling, or image return strongly. That smell brought back memories of my childhood.
- cheer someone up — make someone feel happier. Her message cheered me up after a difficult day.
- leave something behind — move on from something or stop including it in your life. Starting a new job does not mean you have to leave old friendships behind.
