# Chapter 5 — Letters from Home

## Story Summary

For the first three weeks at Redmond, Anne and Priscilla continue to feel like strangers. Then the college begins to seem more familiar. Professors, classes, studies, students, and social events stop feeling like unrelated pieces. The freshmen develop a strong class identity with their own interests, rivalries, ambitions, and class yell.

Their confidence rises after they win the annual Arts Rush against the Sophomores, who had won for the previous three years. Gilbert Blythe organizes the freshman strategy and introduces new tactics that help his class succeed. After the victory, he is elected president of the freshman class. He is also invited to join Lambda Theta, known at Redmond as “the Lambs,” an honor rarely given to a freshman.

Before joining, Gilbert has to complete an initiation. He spends an entire day walking through the main business streets of Kingsport wearing a sunbonnet and a large flowered kitchen apron. Gilbert accepts the embarrassment cheerfully and even removes his bonnet politely when he meets women he knows. Charlie Sloane, who has not been invited to join the group, tells Anne that he would never humiliate himself in such a way. Priscilla laughs at the idea of Charlie wearing the same outfit.

Anne and Priscilla also become involved in Redmond’s social life more quickly than most first-year women. Philippa Gordon is largely responsible. Phil belongs to a wealthy and well-known Nova Scotia family, and her beauty and lively personality give her access to many groups on campus. Wherever she goes, she brings Anne and Priscilla with her, so the two Island girls soon have a much wider circle of acquaintances than they expected.

Phil continues to talk openly about her many admirers. When Anne teases her about Alec and Alonzo, Phil says neither has a serious rival yet and that she still writes to both every week. She admits that the young man at Redmond she would most like to attract is Gilbert, but he pays little attention to her. She jokingly blames Anne, then says she has become too fond of Anne to hold it against her.

Despite her busy social life, Phil does well academically. Even the mathematics professor, who dislikes women students, cannot defeat her in class. Anne does better than Phil in English. Anne finds much of the first-year work manageable because of the serious studying she and Gilbert did during their two years of teaching in Avonlea.

Letters from home become the happiest part of Anne’s week. Her first group of letters comes from Jane Andrews, Ruby Gillis, Diana Barry, Marilla, Mrs. Rachel Lynde, and Davy. Jane’s letter is extremely neat but gives Anne almost none of the information she wants. Jane does not discuss the Avonlea school or answer Anne’s questions. Instead, she writes about crocheting lace, the weather, her new dress, and her headaches.

Ruby’s letter tells Anne how much she is missed and gives long reports about Ruby’s admirers. A postscript catches Anne’s attention because Ruby says Gilbert seems to be enjoying Redmond, judging from his letters. Anne is irritated to learn that Gilbert has been writing to Ruby. She does not know that Ruby wrote first and that Gilbert merely answered her politely. Diana’s lively letter soon improves Anne’s mood, although Anne thinks it contains rather too much about Fred. Marilla’s letter is quieter and contains almost no gossip.

Mrs. Lynde’s letter is full of church news, warnings, and neighborhood stories. She complains about the temporary ministers filling the vacant Avonlea pulpit and advises Anne not to study too hard. She also describes an accident involving one of Mr. Harrison’s pigs. The pig gets into the back porch just as a visiting minister arrives, runs between his legs, and carries him down the hill until he falls off near the brook. Mrs. Lynde and Marilla help him up, but he is angry and seems to blame them for the incident.

Davy’s letter is filled with misspellings, questions, and complaints. He asks Anne to tell Marilla not to tie him to the bridge rail when he goes fishing, says Jane Andrews is stricter than Anne was as a teacher, and reports that he frightened Mrs. Lynde with a jack-o’-lantern. He also asks about several strange stories and sick neighbors. After finishing the letters, Anne wonders what Mrs. Lynde would think of Philippa.

## Conversation 1 — Settling Into Redmond

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Priscilla: Redmond finally feels less confusing.
Anne: I agree. Things are starting to **fall into place**.
Priscilla: Phil has definitely helped us **fit in** socially.
Anne: She has. She seems to know everyone.
Priscilla: And Gilbert has really **stepped into** a leadership role.
Anne: Winning the Arts Rush certainly helped.
Priscilla: Do you still feel homesick?
Anne: Sometimes, but I’m not letting it **take over** my days anymore.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- fall into place — begin to make sense or develop in a satisfactory way. After a confusing first week, everything started to fall into place.
- fit in — feel accepted or comfortable within a group. It took some time, but she eventually fit in with her new classmates.
- step into — begin taking on a new role or responsibility. He stepped into a leadership role after the manager left.
- take over — become dominant or gain control. I try not to let anxiety take over before presentations.

## Conversation 2 — Letters From Home

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Phil: You always look happier when your mail arrives.
Anne: Letters from home **keep me connected to** Avonlea.
Phil: Even when they’re full of ordinary news?
Anne: Especially then. Small details **bring everything back**.
Phil: Do they ever make you more homesick?
Anne: Sometimes, but they also **cheer me up**.
Phil: So you can enjoy Redmond without forgetting home.
Anne: Exactly. I don’t have to **leave one behind** to appreciate the other.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- keep someone connected to — help maintain a relationship or sense of connection. Weekly calls keep me connected to my family.
- bring something back — make a memory, feeling, or image return strongly. That smell brought back memories of my childhood.
- cheer someone up — make someone feel happier. Her message cheered me up after a difficult day.
- leave something behind — move on without continuing to include something in your life. Starting a new job does not mean leaving old friendships behind.
