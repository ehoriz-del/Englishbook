# Chapter 24 — Enter Jonas

## Story Summary

During the summer, Philippa writes Anne a long letter from Prospect Point, where she is staying at a boardinghouse near the shore. Phil admits that she has neglected her letters because she has been busy and tired, but she finally decides to catch Anne up on everything that has happened.

Phil begins with one of her usual comic stories. She recently visited a neighbor who later mentioned that a hired boy might have scarlet fever. That night Phil became convinced that she had caught the disease. She woke with a headache, sore throat, and fever, read a medical book, and decided she had every symptom. By morning she felt perfectly well again and realized she had frightened herself.

Phil then describes the people at the boardinghouse. Cousin Emily runs the place, and most of the guests are older women. One woman talks constantly about illnesses. Another speaks in such a sad voice that Phil expects her to cry at any moment. Another laughs at almost everything. Phil finds them amusing but sometimes difficult.

The most important person in the letter is a young man named Jonas Blake. Jonas is a theology student from St. Columbia and is serving the local mission church for the summer.

Phil says that Jonas is physically very unattractive. He is tall, thin, awkward, with long legs, light hair, green eyes, and large ears. At first she cannot imagine being interested in someone who looks so different from the handsome men she usually notices.

However, Phil quickly becomes friendly with him. Jonas has studied at Redmond, so they have college life in common. They go fishing and boating together and take walks along the beach. Phil discovers that he has a beautiful voice and a warm, good-natured personality.

The older women at the boardinghouse do not entirely approve of Jonas because he laughs and jokes more than they think a future minister should. They also notice that he enjoys Phil’s company.

Phil is surprised to realize that Jonas’s opinion matters to her. She does not want him to think of her as shallow or foolish. This is unusual because Phil usually enjoys being playful and does not worry much about what men think of her character.

One Sunday, Phil goes to hear Jonas preach. She expects to find the situation amusing because it is difficult for her to imagine her cheerful companion in the pulpit.

Instead, the sermon changes the way she sees him. Jonas speaks seriously and sincerely, and Phil realizes how strongly he believes in his work. She begins to understand that becoming a minister is not simply a career choice for him. He feels called to do it.

Phil becomes very self-conscious while listening. She starts comparing herself with the kind of woman she imagines Jonas would want to marry. In her mind, such a woman would be serious, steady, practical, and ready to support a minister’s work. Phil does not think she fits that image at all.

After church, Jonas catches up with her on the road. Phil tells him that he was born to be a minister. Jonas says he once tried to imagine another life, but eventually accepted that ministry was the work he believed he should do.

His answer affects Phil deeply. In the letter, she tries to make jokes about the situation, but her feelings are clear. She says Jonas is poor, not handsome, and completely different from the men she once thought she might marry.

At first Phil tells Anne not to suggest that she has fallen in love with Jonas. She insists that the idea is impossible.

Then she adds a postscript. In it, she admits that she is afraid the impossible thing may actually be true. She is happy, worried, and uncertain at the same time. She wonders whether Jonas could ever love her and whether she could become a suitable minister’s wife.

Phil also tells Anne that Jonas is very popular with the people who know him well, even though some of the older women at the boardinghouse think he is too cheerful for a future minister. The more time Phil spends with him, the less important his appearance becomes to her. She begins measuring him by his kindness, humor, seriousness, and the way he treats other people.

## Conversation 1 — Phil Meets Jonas

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Phil: Jonas isn’t the kind of person I usually **go for**.
Anne: What do you mean?
Phil: He’s not handsome, and he’s completely different from Alec and Alonzo.
Anne: But you seem to like him.
Phil: We **hit it off** almost immediately.
Anne: So you enjoy being with him?
Phil: Very much. Somehow he **brings out** a more serious side of me.
Anne: That sounds important.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- go for — be attracted to or usually choose a certain type. I don’t usually go for very formal people.
- hit it off — quickly become friendly and comfortable with someone. We hit it off the first time we met.
- bring out — cause a quality in someone to appear more clearly. Good friends bring out the best in each other.
- be different from — not be the same as something or someone else. This job is very different from my last one.

## Conversation 2 — After the Sermon

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Phil: I thought I understood Jonas, but today I **saw him in a new light**.
Anne: What changed?
Phil: I heard him preach.
Anne: And?
Phil: It made me **look at myself differently** too.
Anne: That sounds serious for you.
Phil: Don’t tease me. I’m trying to **sort out** what I feel.
Anne: Take your time.
Phil: I’m afraid I may have **fallen for** him.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- see someone in a new light — begin to understand or view someone differently. After hearing her speak, I saw her in a new light.
- look at something differently — consider something from a different point of view. The experience made me look at my plans differently.
- sort something out — understand or solve a confusing situation. I need time to sort out my feelings.
- fall for — begin to fall in love with or become strongly attracted to someone. I think she has fallen for him.
