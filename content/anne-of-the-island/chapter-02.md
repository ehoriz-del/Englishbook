# Chapter 2 — Garlands of Autumn

## Story Summary

Anne’s final week in Avonlea passes quickly and is filled with farewells. She makes goodbye calls, receives visitors, finishes sewing and packing, and tries to complete all the small tasks that must be done before she leaves for Redmond. Some people are enthusiastic about her plans, while others think she is becoming too ambitious and feel obliged to warn her against college life.

The Avonlea Village Improvement Society holds a farewell party for Anne and Gilbert at the Pye home. The entire old group attends. Diana is there with Fred Wright, Jane Andrews comes as usual, Ruby Gillis looks especially attractive, and Charlie Sloane stays close to Anne whenever possible. Billy Andrews spends much of the evening sitting in a corner and watching Anne with obvious admiration. Even Josie Pye is unusually pleasant and tells Anne that her new dress is becoming.

Anne knows there will be a party, but she does not know that she and Gilbert will receive a formal address and gifts for founding the A.V.I.S. Moody Spurgeon MacPherson reads the address in a serious manner. Anne receives a volume of Shakespeare’s plays, and Gilbert is given a fountain pen. Anne is surprised and moved by the praise for the work they have done in the village.

Later in the evening, Gilbert says something sentimental to Anne while they are eating supper on the veranda. Anne is uncomfortable with the change in his manner and decides to punish him by being particularly friendly to Charlie Sloane. She allows Charlie to walk her home. The plan immediately becomes unpleasant for Anne. Gilbert walks away with Ruby Gillis, and Anne can hear them laughing together while Charlie talks continuously beside her. When Anne finally reaches her room, she tells herself that she is simply tired.

The next evening Gilbert comes to Green Gables. Anne has spent the day packing her trunk and sewing, but she is also discouraged because six women have called to say goodbye. Each one has managed to raise a different concern about Redmond. Mrs. Peter Sloane has suggested that four years of college may be too demanding for Anne’s health. Mrs. Eben Wright has reminded her how expensive it will be. Mrs. Jasper Bell has warned that college might make her proud, while Mrs. Elisha Wright has said that the fashionable girls at Redmond may look down on a country girl from Avonlea.

Anne admits that she knows these comments should not matter, but they have affected her mood. Gilbert says she is tired and invites her for a walk through the woods beyond the marsh. As they leave, Mrs. Rachel Lynde and Marilla watch them from the kitchen window. Mrs. Lynde predicts that Anne and Gilbert will marry someday. Marilla says they are still young, but Mrs. Lynde points out that Anne is eighteen and that Gilbert’s feelings are obvious. She also mentions Charlie Sloane and says she would never advise Anne to marry a Sloane.

Anne and Gilbert walk through the Haunted Wood and talk about old memories. Gilbert carefully avoids the romantic tone that has recently made Anne uneasy, and the two fall back into their familiar school-day friendship. Beyond the marsh, Gilbert shows Anne the reason he wanted to bring her there. In the middle of the pines and beeches stands a wild apple tree that he discovered in blossom the previous spring.

The tree is now covered with reddish apples. Gilbert climbs up to pick some while Anne sits on a fallen tree. The apples turn out to be unusually good. They remain there until evening changes into moonlight, then start back toward Green Gables through Lover’s Lane.

Gilbert asks whether Anne still feels discouraged about Redmond. Anne says she does not. She is now convinced that she will enjoy college and expects to have four good years there. Gilbert asks what she plans to do after those four years. Anne says she does not know and does not want to know yet. There will be another bend in the road when the time comes. They continue home in comfortable silence, while Gilbert privately wonders whether Anne will ever return his feelings.

## Conversation 1 — After the Farewell Visits

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Anne: I know those women meant well, but their comments really **got to me**.
Gilbert: Hearing the same doubts all day can **wear you down**.
Anne: I started wondering whether college was a mistake.
Gilbert: Don’t let other people **talk you out of** something you’ve worked for.
Anne: I’m trying not to.
Gilbert: You’re exhausted. That makes it easier to **second-guess** yourself.
Anne: You may be right.
Gilbert: Come for a walk. You need a break from all of this.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- get to someone — affect someone emotionally, usually in an upsetting way. The criticism got to me more than I expected.
- wear someone down — gradually make someone tired, discouraged, or less confident. Constant delays wore the whole team down.
- talk someone out of — persuade someone not to do something. My friends tried to talk me out of quitting.
- second-guess yourself — question your own judgment after making a decision. Stop second-guessing yourself and trust the preparation you did.

## Conversation 2 — After the Walk

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Gilbert: Feeling any better?
Anne: Much better. The walk helped me **shake off** the mood.
Gilbert: Sometimes getting away helps you **put things in perspective**.
Anne: It did. I’m ready to **follow through on** my Redmond plans.
Gilbert: Good. And what happens after college?
Anne: I’ll **deal with** that when the time comes.
Gilbert: No detailed five-year plan?
Anne: Absolutely not. One bend in the road at a time.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- shake off — get rid of a negative feeling or effect. I went outside to shake off the stress of the meeting.
- put something in perspective — consider a situation in a more balanced way. A weekend away helped me put the problem in perspective.
- follow through on — carry out something you planned or promised. She followed through on her decision to return to school.
- deal with — handle a situation or problem. We’ll deal with the next stage when it arrives.
