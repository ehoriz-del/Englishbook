# Chapter 2 — Garlands of Autumn

## Story Summary

Anne’s final week in Avonlea passes quickly and is crowded with what she calls “last things.” She makes farewell visits, receives visitors, finishes packing, and tries to enjoy familiar places before leaving for Redmond. Some neighbors warmly support her plans, while others make comments that suggest they think a college education is unnecessary or even unwise for her. Anne knows that many of these people mean well, but their doubts still affect her more than she expects.

The Avonlea Village Improvement Society holds a farewell party for Anne and Gilbert at the Pye home. Everyone from their old circle is there, including Diana, Fred, Jane Andrews, Ruby Gillis, Charlie Sloane, Moody Spurgeon MacPherson, and Billy Andrews. Even Josie Pye behaves pleasantly and tells Anne that her new dress is becoming. Years earlier, Josie’s condescending manner would have hurt Anne, but now Anne is mature enough to find it amusing.

Anne and Gilbert are surprised when the Society presents them with a formal address thanking them for their work as founders of the A.V.I.S. Anne receives a volume of Shakespeare, while Gilbert is given a fountain pen. Anne is deeply touched by the appreciation. She has worked hard for the Society, and hearing that her efforts mattered to other people makes the evening especially meaningful.

The mood changes later when Gilbert says something sentimental to Anne while they are eating supper on the moonlit veranda. Anne, who has become uncomfortable whenever Gilbert’s manner suggests romantic feelings, decides to punish him by paying extra attention to Charlie Sloane. She allows Charlie to walk her home. Her plan immediately works against her. Gilbert walks off with Ruby Gillis, and Anne can hear the two of them laughing together while she is forced to listen to Charlie talk endlessly. She tells herself that she is simply tired, but she is clearly relieved the following evening when Gilbert appears at Green Gables rather than spending his last evening with Ruby.

Anne has spent the day packing and receiving six women who came to say goodbye. Each woman said something that made Anne question her decision to attend Redmond. One worried that college would damage her health. Another pointed out how expensive four years would be. Someone else warned her not to become proud or “spoiled” by education, while another suggested that fashionable Kingsport girls would make a country girl feel out of place. Anne knows logically that these comments reflect limited experience, but she is sensitive enough that repeated doubt has worn down her confidence.

Gilbert tells her that she is exhausted and invites her for a walk through the woods beyond the marsh. He points out that Anne is the first Avonlea girl to go to college, so it is natural that some people regard her choice as strange. Anne agrees that her common sense understands this, but admits that feelings do not always obey common sense.

As Anne and Gilbert leave together, Mrs. Lynde tells Marilla that she is certain the two will eventually marry. Marilla secretly hopes the same, although she dislikes hearing the matter discussed so casually. Mrs. Lynde says Anne is eighteen now and claims that Gilbert obviously loves her. She also mentions Charlie Sloane but makes it clear that, in her opinion, he is no match for Gilbert.

Meanwhile, Anne and Gilbert walk through the Haunted Wood, remembering childhood adventures. Gilbert carefully avoids the romantic tone that has recently made Anne uncomfortable and behaves like the old school friend she trusts. He leads her beyond the marsh to something he discovered in spring: a wild apple tree growing far from any orchard. It is now loaded with beautiful red-cheeked fruit.

They sit on a fallen tree while Gilbert picks apples, and the fruit turns out to be unusually delicious. The unexpected beauty of the tree and the peaceful walk restore Anne’s spirits. By the time they start home through Lover’s Lane, she is no longer discouraged. She says she is sure she will love Redmond and have four wonderful years there. When Gilbert asks what will happen after those four years, Anne refuses to plan so far ahead. There will be another bend in the road, she says, and she would rather wait to discover what lies beyond it.

## Conversation 1 — After Too Much Advice

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Anne: I know those women meant well, but their comments really **got to me**.
Gilbert: You’ve had the same doubts repeated all day. No wonder they **wore you down**.
Anne: I started wondering whether I was being foolish about college.
Gilbert: Don’t let other people’s fears **talk you out of** something you’ve worked for.
Anne: My common sense says the same thing.
Gilbert: Then maybe you need a break before you **second-guess** yourself any further.
Anne: A walk might help me clear my head.
Gilbert: Good. Let’s leave everyone else’s opinions behind for an hour.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- get to someone — affect someone emotionally, usually in an upsetting way. His criticism got to me more than I expected.
- wear someone down — gradually make someone tired, discouraged, or less resistant. Weeks of uncertainty wore the team down.
- talk someone out of — persuade someone not to do something. They tried to talk her out of applying for the position.
- second-guess yourself — question your own decision after making it. I keep second-guessing myself even though the plan is solid.

## Conversation 2 — The Walk in the Woods

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Gilbert: You seem more cheerful than when we started.
Anne: I am. Getting away for a while helped me **shake off** the mood.
Gilbert: Sometimes distance helps you **put things in perspective**.
Anne: And finding this apple tree certainly didn’t hurt.
Gilbert: So are you still worried about Redmond?
Anne: Not nearly as much. I’m ready to **follow through on** my plan.
Gilbert: And after four years?
Anne: I’ll **deal with** that bend in the road when I reach it.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- shake off — get rid of a negative feeling or effect. I went for a walk to shake off the stress of the meeting.
- put something in perspective — see a situation more realistically by considering the bigger picture. A few days away helped me put the problem in perspective.
- follow through on — complete something you have planned or promised. She followed through on her decision to return to school.
- deal with — handle a problem, responsibility, or situation. We’ll deal with the next stage when it arrives.
