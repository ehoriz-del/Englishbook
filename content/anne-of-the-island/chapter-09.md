# Chapter 9 — An Unwelcome Lover and a Welcome Friend

## Story Summary

Anne’s second term at Redmond passes even more quickly than the first. She enjoys her classes, friendships, social events, and student societies, but she also works hard because she wants to win the Thorburn Scholarship in English. The scholarship would allow her to return to Redmond the following year without using Marilla’s limited savings.

Gilbert is also competing for a scholarship. At the same time, he continues to visit 38 St. John Street often and accompanies Anne to many college events. Other students begin to connect their names romantically, which irritates Anne. Gilbert, however, no longer speaks to her in the openly serious way that previously made her uncomfortable. He behaves like the familiar friend she knew in Avonlea.

Several other Redmond students also show interest in Anne, although she never has the large group of admirers that surrounds Philippa. Anne enjoys talking with different students about classes and ideas, but she still finds Gilbert the easiest and most satisfying companion. She tells herself that she is pleased he appears to have abandoned his earlier romantic intentions, even though she sometimes wonders why he has changed.

One evening Charlie Sloane visits Anne at the boardinghouse. Sitting stiffly on one of Miss Ada’s carefully protected cushions, Charlie asks Anne whether she will promise to become Mrs. Charlie Sloane someday. Because Billy Andrews has already proposed through Jane, Anne is not as shocked as she might otherwise have been, but she is angry that Charlie seems to assume she should be pleased.

Anne refuses as politely as she can. Charlie does not accept the rejection gracefully. He makes several unpleasant remarks, and Anne loses her temper and answers sharply. Charlie leaves with an angry red face. Anne rushes upstairs, trips over Miss Ada’s cushions on the way, and throws herself onto her bed, furious and humiliated.

After that evening, Charlie keeps his distance. When he meets Anne at Redmond or on the street, he gives her a cold formal greeting. Their strained relationship continues for many months, until Charlie transfers his attention to another student and eventually becomes civil to Anne again.

A much more welcome development soon arrives in a letter from Stella Maynard, an old friend from Queen’s Academy. Stella has been teaching in a rural school for two years but has decided to give it up and attend Redmond. Because she completed the third year at Queen’s, she can enter Redmond as a Sophomore.

Stella writes at length about the difficulties of teaching several grades at once and dealing with parents who expect a schoolteacher to solve every problem. Then she presents a plan. She dislikes boardinghouses and does not want to spend three more years in one. She suggests that she, Anne, and Priscilla rent a small house in Kingsport and keep house together.

Stella also has a possible housekeeper. Her Aunt Jamesina is living alone because her only daughter has recently married and left for foreign mission work. Aunt Jamesina is lonely and is willing to come to Kingsport and keep house for the girls. Stella asks Anne and Priscilla to start looking for a suitable house before the term ends.

Anne and Priscilla immediately agree. They begin house-hunting before examinations, but every available place has a problem. Some houses are too large, others too small, too expensive, too far from Redmond, or in locations they do not want. Examinations come and go, and the end of the term approaches without success.

Meanwhile, Anne achieves one of her main goals: she wins the Thorburn Scholarship. She is relieved because it means she can return to Redmond without taking money from Marilla’s savings.

One spring afternoon, Anne and Priscilla walk through the park and discuss their unsuccessful search. They decide they may have to wait until autumn. On the way home they take their usual route along Spofford Avenue so they can look at Patty’s Place. This time something is different. A small sign hangs over the gate: “To Let, Furnished. Inquire Within.”

Anne and Priscilla stop in astonishment. Priscilla is afraid the rent will be far beyond what they can afford, but Anne insists that they must at least ask. It is too late to call that evening, so they decide to return the next day.

## Conversation 1 — Charlie’s Proposal

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Charlie: Anne, there’s something I’ve been meaning to **ask you about**.
Anne: All right. What is it?
Charlie: Would you consider marrying me someday?
Anne: Charlie, I’m sorry, but I don’t feel that way about you.
Charlie: You’re turning me down without even thinking about it?
Anne: I don’t want to **lead you on** when I already know my answer.
Charlie: I thought you’d at least consider it.
Anne: I’d rather **be clear about** where I stand.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- ask someone about — raise a subject with someone in order to get an answer or opinion. I wanted to ask you about the schedule.
- turn someone down — reject an offer, request, or romantic proposal. She turned down the job because the hours were too long.
- lead someone on — encourage someone to believe you are more interested than you really are. He did not want to lead her on.
- be clear about — communicate your position or intention directly. We need to be clear about what we can promise.

## Conversation 2 — Stella’s Plan

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Anne: Stella wants to come to Redmond next year.
Priscilla: That’s wonderful. What’s the plan?
Anne: She thinks the three of us should **team up** and rent a house.
Priscilla: I’d love to **get out of** boardinghouse life.
Anne: So would I. We should **look into** places now.
Priscilla: As long as we can find something affordable.
Anne: We’ll have to **rule out** anything too expensive or too far away.
Priscilla: Then let’s start this week.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- team up — work or join together with someone for a shared purpose. We teamed up to finish the project faster.
- get out of — leave or escape an unwanted situation. She wanted to get out of a long commute.
- look into — investigate or find more information about something. I’ll look into rental prices tomorrow.
- rule out — decide that an option is not possible or suitable. We ruled out the first apartment because it was too expensive.
