# Chapter 6 — In the Park

## Story Summary

One Saturday afternoon at Redmond, Philippa Gordon comes into Anne’s room and asks what Anne and Priscilla are planning to do. Anne says they are going for a walk in the park with Gilbert Blythe and Charlie Sloane. She should be sewing a blouse, but the fine autumn weather has made indoor work impossible to enjoy. Phil agrees to come, although she jokes that she will have to act as a “gooseberry” while the others walk in pairs.

Phil is in a slightly bad mood because of a mistake involving letters to Alec and Alonzo, the two young men she has been unable to choose between. She had written to both of them and, while hurriedly adding a postscript, accidentally put the message intended for Alec into Alonzo’s letter. Alonzo has replied angrily. Phil expects him to recover, but the incident has spoiled her day. She hopes the walk with Anne and Priscilla will improve her mood.

Before they leave, Phil mentions that Gilbert has been elected captain of the freshman football team. She refers to him as “that Gilbert of yours,” which irritates Anne. Phil also makes fun of Charlie’s appearance. Anne does not particularly admire Charlie, but because he is from Avonlea she feels that Phil has no right to ridicule him. Before the group leaves, Priscilla also tells Phil about Charlie’s visit to the boardinghouse the previous evening. Anne and Priscilla had tried to protect Miss Ada’s decorative cushions by moving them out of reach, but Charlie found one behind a chair and sat on it anyway, leaving it badly crushed. During the walk, Phil and Priscilla stay with Charlie, while Anne and Gilbert gradually fall behind the others.

Anne and Gilbert walk slowly beneath the pines on the road beside the harbor. They talk about the quiet atmosphere of the park and the pleasure Anne takes in being among trees. Their conversation turns briefly to happiness, difficulty, and the future. Gilbert speaks in a tone that makes Anne suspect he may be moving toward a personal declaration, so she quickly changes the direction of the conversation and points out that the others have reached the pavilion.

The five friends sit in the pavilion and watch the autumn sunset over Kingsport and the harbor. Across the water they can see William’s Island, with its fort and lighthouse. Philippa notices a soldier standing near the flag and says he looks as though he belongs in a romance. Priscilla then mentions that she and Anne have been trying to find heather in the park.

Phil explains that there is a small patch of heather there, one of only two she knows of in North America. According to the story she has heard, a Highland regiment once camped in the park. Seeds in the straw from the soldiers’ beds later took root and produced the heather. Anne is delighted by the story, but by then it is too late in the season for them to find the flowers.

As the group starts back, Gilbert suggests returning by Spofford Avenue, a wealthy residential street filled with large new houses. Phil says there is one small house there that she especially wants Anne to see. Unlike the grand neighboring homes, it is an old white frame house near the top of the hill, surrounded by pines. Red and gold vines cover part of it, green shutters frame the windows, and a tiny old-fashioned garden stands behind a low stone wall.

Anne is immediately attracted to the house. Phil points out the name painted over the arched gate: “Patty’s Place.” She explains that Patty Spofford, an elderly woman, owns the property and lives there with her niece. Wealthy people have repeatedly tried to buy the valuable lot, but Miss Patty has always refused to sell. Behind the house is an actual apple orchard, an unusual sight on fashionable Spofford Avenue.

Anne says Patty’s Place is one of the dearest houses she has ever seen and wonders whether she will ever be able to see the inside. Priscilla thinks that is unlikely. Anne admits that it probably is, but she says she has a strong feeling that she and Patty’s Place will somehow become better acquainted in the future.

## Conversation 1 — Joining the Saturday Walk

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Phil: Do you mind if I **tag along** with you this afternoon?
Anne: Not at all. We’d be glad to have you.
Phil: Good. I need something to **take my mind off** a letter problem.
Priscilla: What happened this time?
Phil: I mixed up part of two letters, and Alonzo did not **take it well**.
Anne: That sounds awkward.
Phil: It was. I’m hoping a long walk will **cheer me up**.
Priscilla: Then you came to the right place.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- tag along — go somewhere with another person or group, often informally. Do you mind if I tag along to the market?
- take your mind off — make you stop thinking about something unpleasant for a while. A walk helped take my mind off the problem.
- take something well — react calmly or positively to difficult news or criticism. He did not take the cancellation well.
- cheer someone up — make someone feel happier. Spending time with friends cheered her up.

## Conversation 2 — Discovering Patty’s Place

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Phil: There’s a little house ahead that really **stands out**.
Anne: Among all these huge houses? I see what you mean.
Priscilla: It looks completely different from everything around it.
Anne: I’ve already **fallen for** it.
Phil: It’s called Patty’s Place.
Anne: That name makes me like it even more.
Priscilla: Don’t **get your hopes up**. We’ll probably never see the inside.
Anne: Maybe, but I can’t **shake the feeling** that we will.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- stand out — be noticeably different from the people or things around it. The small cottage stood out among the modern buildings.
- fall for — become strongly attracted to someone or something. We fell for the apartment as soon as we saw the windows.
- get your hopes up — become too confident that something you want will happen. Don’t get your hopes up until the decision is official.
- shake the feeling — get rid of a persistent feeling or impression. I couldn’t shake the feeling that I had forgotten something.
