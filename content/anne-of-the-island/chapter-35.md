# Chapter 35 — The Last Redmond Year Opens

## Story Summary

Anne, Philippa, Priscilla, and Stella return to Patty’s Place for their final year at Redmond. The house is immediately noisy with luggage, conversation, and the excitement of seeing Aunt Jamesina and the cats again.

Rusty has lost another small piece of one ear, but Anne is delighted to see him. Aunt Jamesina welcomes the girls but quickly tells them to unpack and put the house in order instead of sitting among their trunks talking.

Phil jokes that young people now prefer to play first and work afterward. Aunt Jamesina reminds her that as the future wife of a minister, Phil may eventually need to be more careful about the expressions she uses.

The conversation turns to Phil’s engagement to Jonas Blake. Phil has told her family that she plans to marry him.

Her mother was very upset at first because Jonas is not the wealthy husband she had imagined for her daughter. Phil, who once had difficulty making decisions, remained firm. Her father was calmer, partly because his own father had been a minister.

Eventually Phil brought Jonas to Mount Holly to meet her parents. After they got to know him, both came to like him, although Phil’s mother continued making comments about the different future she had once expected for her daughter.

Phil tells her friends that none of this matters because she has Jonas.

Aunt Jamesina and the girls then talk about forgiveness, growing older, and old courtships. Anne tells everyone the story of John Douglas and Janet Sweet.

Phil also demands to hear about the mysterious romantic event Anne had mentioned in one of her letters. Anne acts out Sam Toliver’s proposal, and everyone laughs.

Aunt Jamesina says it is not good manners to make fun of men who have proposed, then admits that she used to do exactly the same thing.

The girls ask Aunt Jamesina about her own admirers. She surprises them by saying she still has three elderly widowers interested in her and warns them not to imagine that romance belongs only to young people.

She then remembers several men who courted her in her youth. One was so dreamy that he did not realize for a year that she had rejected him. Another believed he knew the answer to every question. Another was pleasant but slow to understand jokes and never actually proposed. Aunt Jamesina finally ends the discussion and orders the girls to unpack.

Soon the household settles into the demanding routine of the final college year.

Anne focuses on English, Priscilla studies the classics, and Phil works hard at mathematics. Graduation honors require serious study, and the girls sometimes become exhausted and discouraged.

On a rainy November evening, Stella comes to Anne’s blue room feeling especially low. Anne has also been tired of studying, so she has opened a collection of old stories written years earlier by the Avonlea Story Club.

The stories were written by Anne, Diana, Jane Andrews, Ruby Gillis, and their friends when they were younger. They are filled with dramatic deaths, beautiful heroines, expensive clothes, tragedies, and exaggerated emotion.

Now Anne finds them extremely funny.

She shows Stella one story in which a beautiful heroine wears a white satin nightgown decorated with seed pearls. Anne also finds one of her own old stories called “My Graves,” about a minister’s wife who travels from place to place and loses child after child. Anne had intended to kill all nine children in the story but finally allowed the ninth to survive as an invalid because she ran out of terrible ideas.

Stella laughs so much that her bad mood begins to disappear.

As Anne continues looking through the old papers, she remembers the summer days when the Story Club met near the trees and brook in Avonlea.

Then she finds a short piece written on wrapping paper. She remembers writing it after falling through the roof of the Cobb duckhouse years earlier.

The piece is a small imaginative dialogue involving flowers, birds, and the spirit of a garden.

Anne begins by laughing at it, but then she reads it more carefully. Her expression changes. After Stella leaves, Anne smooths out the crumpled pages instead of putting them back with the other childish stories.

She looks at the old manuscript and makes a decision about it, ending the evening with the words, “I believe I will.”

## Conversation 1 — Back at Patty’s Place

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Phil: I can’t believe we’re all **back together** again.
Anne: And Rusty clearly missed us.
Aunt Jamesina: You can celebrate after you **put away** those suitcases.
Phil: We just got here.
Aunt Jamesina: Exactly. **Get it over with** now.
Priscilla: She has a point.
Anne: All right. We’ll unpack before we settle down.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- back together — reunited after being apart. It’s nice to have everyone back together.
- put something away — return something to its proper place. Please put your books away.
- get something over with — finish something unpleasant or necessary so it is no longer waiting. Let’s get the cleaning over with.
- settle down — become calm or begin focusing seriously. After dinner, we settled down to study.

## Conversation 2 — Finding the Old Story

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Stella: Why are you laughing so much?
Anne: I **dug out** our old Story Club papers.
Stella: Are they really that bad?
Anne: They’re wonderfully terrible.
Stella: What’s that one?
Anne: Something I wrote years ago. I almost **threw it aside**.
Stella: But you didn’t.
Anne: No. I think I may **do something with** it.
Stella: Then keep it. You never know.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- dig something out — find something stored or hidden away. I dug out some old photographs.
- throw something aside — put something away or reject it without much attention. He read the note and threw it aside.
- do something with — use, develop, or take action with something. I think I can do something with this idea.
- you never know — used to say something unexpected may happen. Keep the ticket—you never know.
