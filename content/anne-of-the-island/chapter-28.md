# Chapter 28 — A June Evening

## Story Summary

Anne is home at Green Gables in June. One evening she comes through the orchard and finds Marilla and Mrs. Rachel Lynde sitting on the front steps. Dora is studying, while Davy sits on the grass looking miserable.

Anne asks Davy what is wrong. He says he is tired of living because the temporary teacher, Miss Carson, has given him ten arithmetic problems to finish by Monday. Davy believes it is unfair to have to work on Saturday, especially because his friend Milty Boulter does not intend to do the assignment.

Marilla tells Davy that he must complete the work. Mrs. Lynde defends Miss Carson and says she is a sensible young woman. Anne jokes that she prefers people who have at least a little nonsense in them. She promises to help Davy with the arithmetic so he can finish in time to go fishing.

Davy quickly becomes more cheerful. He then begins talking about the funeral Marilla and Mrs. Lynde attended earlier that day. The dead woman, Aunt Atossa, had been known for her sharp tongue and difficult personality. Davy had hoped something dramatic might happen at the funeral, but Mrs. Lynde says everything was peaceful.

Mrs. Lynde adds that very few people were truly sorry Aunt Atossa was gone. Anne is disturbed by the idea of dying without leaving anyone who will miss you.

The conversation soon turns to Diana’s wedding, which is only five days away. Anne says it feels strange and even a little terrible to think of Diana getting married. Mrs. Lynde does not understand why Anne should feel that way because Fred Wright is a good man with a good farm.

Anne agrees that Fred is good, but she jokes that he is almost too good. Mrs. Lynde and Marilla do not share Anne’s romantic way of thinking.

Marilla is quietly disappointed about something else: Anne’s refusal of Gilbert Blythe has become known around Avonlea. No one is sure how the news spread, but people are talking about it. Gilbert’s mother, who used to be warm toward Anne, now treats her more coldly.

Mrs. Lynde has also heard that Anne has another admirer at college who is rich and handsome. This is Roy Gardner. Mrs. Lynde worries that Anne might choose money and appearance over a better match, although Marilla knows Anne would not marry simply for money.

Anne eventually leaves the others and walks down to the Dryad’s Bubble. She sits among the ferns at the foot of the large white birch where she and Gilbert spent many summer hours together in earlier years.

Gilbert is not in Avonlea. He has returned to his newspaper job for the summer, and he does not write to Anne. Anne tries not to admit how much his absence affects her.

Roy writes to her twice a week. His letters are beautifully written and full of romantic language. Anne tells herself that she feels more in love with Roy whenever she reads them.

However, she remembers one day when she received an envelope in Gilbert’s familiar handwriting. Her heart jumped when she saw it, and she hurried home to open it. The envelope contained only a typed copy of a college society report, not a personal letter. Disappointed, Anne threw it aside and then wrote an especially affectionate letter to Roy.

As the evening grows darker, Anne looks toward Orchard Slope. She can see the light from Diana’s room through the trees.

The Barry household is already busy with wedding preparations. Anne will be Diana’s bridesmaid, just as they promised each other years ago, and Gilbert will be Fred’s best man.

Anne is happy for Diana and enjoys the excitement, but she knows their friendship will change. Diana’s new home will be two miles from Green Gables, and the two friends will no longer share their lives in exactly the same way.

Sitting under the birch tree, Anne cries quietly. She thinks about Diana’s marriage, Gilbert’s absence, and how different Avonlea feels from the world of their childhood.

## Conversation 1 — Davy’s Homework

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Davy: I have ten math problems to finish by Monday.
Anne: That’s why you look so miserable?
Davy: It’s going to **take up** my whole Saturday.
Anne: Not if we **work through** them together.
Davy: You’ll help me?
Anne: Of course. We can **get them done** before you go fishing.
Davy: Then I won’t **fall behind**.
Anne: Exactly. It’s not as bad as it feels right now.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- take up — use a lot of time or space. The assignment took up my whole afternoon.
- work through — deal with something step by step. We worked through the problem together.
- get something done — complete a task. I want to get the report done today.
- fall behind — fail to keep up with expected progress. He missed class and fell behind.

## Conversation 2 — Thinking About Change

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Anne: Diana will be married in five days.
Marilla: You’ve known that for months.
Anne: I know, but now it’s really **sinking in**.
Marilla: You’ll still see her.
Anne: Of course. But things won’t **stay the same**.
Marilla: Life moves on.
Anne: I wish some things didn’t have to **change so much**.
Marilla: You’ll **adjust to** the new situation in time.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- sink in — gradually become fully understood or emotionally real. The news did not sink in until later.
- stay the same — remain unchanged. Things cannot always stay the same.
- change so much — become very different. The neighborhood has changed so much.
- adjust to — become comfortable with a new situation. It took me a few weeks to adjust to the new routine.
