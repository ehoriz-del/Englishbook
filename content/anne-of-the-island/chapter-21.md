# Chapter 21 — Roses of Yesterday

## Story Summary

Anne spends two weeks in Bolingbroke with Philippa at Mount Holly, the Gordon family home. The house is busy with visitors, drives, dances, picnics, and boating parties. Alec and Alonzo are both often there, and Phil continues to joke about not knowing which one she should eventually marry. Anne refuses to choose for her and tells Phil that she must make that decision herself.

Although Anne enjoys the visit, she still feels uncomfortable whenever she thinks about Gilbert. Since refusing his proposal, she has lost the easy friendship they once had. At Mount Holly there is so much activity that she has little time to think about him, but the sadness has not completely disappeared.

The most important part of Anne’s stay is a visit to the small yellow house where she was born. Anne has imagined the house many times, so seeing it in real life is deeply meaningful to her. It is not grand. It is a simple rented house in a quiet street, with a lilac tree near the gate and muslin curtains in the windows.

A tall, thin woman answers the door. She remembers Walter and Bertha Shirley, Anne’s parents, who lived there many years before. She tells Anne that both of them died of fever not long after Anne was born. The woman also remembers hearing that their baby was taken in by the Thomas family.

When Anne tells her that she was that baby, the woman is surprised. She studies Anne’s face and says that Anne resembles both of her parents. She remembers Walter’s red hair and says Anne has Bertha’s eyes and mouth. She also remembers that Bertha had been a much-loved teacher.

The woman invites Anne and Phil inside and allows them to look through the house. Anne goes upstairs to the small east room where she was born. The room is simple, but Anne is deeply moved by being there. She learns that her mother loved the sunrise and that Anne was born just as the morning light came through the window.

Before Anne leaves, the woman gives her a small packet of old letters that had been found in a closet. The top letter is addressed to Miss Bertha Willis, Anne’s mother before her marriage. Anne is overwhelmed because she owns almost nothing that belonged to her parents.

The woman also explains that the family’s furniture was sold to pay medical bills after Anne’s parents died. Mrs. Thomas received Bertha’s clothes and small personal things, but none of them survived. The letters are therefore especially precious to Anne.

Before returning to Mount Holly, Anne visits the cemetery alone. Walter and Bertha Shirley are buried together in one grave. Anne places white flowers there and then returns to Phil’s home.

In her room, Anne carefully reads the letters. There are only about a dozen. Some were written by Walter and some by Bertha during the periods when they were apart before their marriage. They are not formal or important historical letters. They are private notes filled with affection, trust, and everyday thoughts.

The most meaningful letter for Anne is one Bertha wrote shortly after Anne’s birth while Walter was away briefly. In it, Bertha proudly describes the baby and writes about how much she loves her. Anne realizes that this was probably one of the last letters her mother ever wrote.

That evening Anne tells Phil that the day has changed something for her. For the first time, her parents feel like real people rather than distant names connected with a sad story. The letters have given her a personal connection to Walter and Bertha that she never had before.

Although the visit to Mount Holly is cheerful, Anne does not allow Phil to turn the trip into a discussion about Gilbert. Phil still believes Anne made a mistake by refusing him, but Anne does not want to argue about it. Instead, she gives most of her attention to the Gordon family, their guests, and finally to the visit she has wanted to make for years. The quiet yellow house affects her far more strongly than the busy social life at Mount Holly.

## Conversation 1 — Visiting the Old House

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Phil: Is this really the house where you were born?
Anne: Yes. I’ve imagined it so many times, and now I’m finally **standing in front of** it.
Phil: Does it look the way you expected?
Anne: Almost. I’m trying to **take it all in**.
Phil: Do you want me to come inside with you?
Anne: Please. I’m not sure I could **go through with** this alone.
Phil: Of course. We’ll take our time.
Anne: Thank you. This means more to me than I can explain.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- stand in front of — be positioned directly before something. We stood in front of the old house for several minutes.
- take something in — fully absorb or understand what you are seeing or experiencing. I needed time to take it all in.
- go through with — do something difficult that you have planned. She decided to go through with the visit.
- take your time — do something slowly without rushing. Take your time and look around.

## Conversation 2 — Reading the Letters

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Phil: You’ve been reading for a long time.
Anne: I know. I don’t want to **rush through** these letters.
Phil: Do they tell you much about your parents?
Anne: More than I expected. They really **bring them to life**.
Phil: That must be emotional.
Anne: It is, but I’m glad these letters **turned up**.
Phil: You finally have something that belonged to them.
Anne: Yes. I’ll never **let go of** them.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- rush through — do something too quickly. I didn’t want to rush through such an important letter.
- bring someone to life — make a person seem vivid or real. The old stories brought her grandparents to life.
- turn up — appear or be found unexpectedly. An old photograph turned up in a drawer.
- let go of — stop holding or give up something. She never wanted to let go of the letters.
