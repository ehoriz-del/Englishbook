# Chapter 1 — Back to Windy Poplars

## Story Summary

Anne begins her second year in Summerside by writing to Gilbert from the tower room at Windy Poplars. She is still adjusting to the fact that the beautiful summer they spent together has ended, but returning to Summerside is not the lonely experience it was the year before. Windy Poplars now feels familiar. Her tower room, favorite chair, high bed, and even Dusty Miller on the kitchen window-sill give her the pleasant sense of coming back to a second home.

Aunt Kate, Aunt Chatty, and Rebecca Dew are openly glad to see her again. Rebecca, who is not usually sentimental, simply says that it is good to have Anne back. That brief statement means a great deal because Rebecca’s affection is usually expressed through practical actions rather than emotional speeches. Anne realizes that during her first year she has become part of the household rather than merely a boarder.

The most joyful reunion is with Little Elizabeth. Anne meets her at the green gate, and the child is almost overcome with happiness. Elizabeth says she had been afraid Anne might somehow have reached “Tomorrow” without her. As before, “Tomorrow” is Elizabeth’s private name for the future she imagines—a life in which she will be free, loved, and surrounded by beautiful possibilities. Anne does not correct the fantasy. She enters into it with her.

When Anne asks how Elizabeth spent the summer, the girl says she spent it thinking about all the wonderful things that will happen in Tomorrow. The answer reminds Anne that Elizabeth’s imaginative world is not childish nonsense. It is a way of protecting hope in a home where affection is limited and where much of her life is controlled by adults. Anne’s return restores one of the few relationships in which Elizabeth can speak freely.

The two go up to the tower room and read together. At the moment Elizabeth is fascinated by elephants, so they add an elephant park to the fairyland map they began the previous year. Anne jokingly imagines Gilbert looking superior about such fantasies, but she insists that the world will always need fairies and that someone must keep supplying them. Her comment shows that imagination remains one of her deepest values. For Anne, imagination is not the opposite of maturity. It is a way of enlarging life.

School also feels different this year. The pupils seem pleased to have her back, and even Jen Pringle now treats her as a friend. Jen asks Anne to help make tin halos for angels in a Sunday-school concert. The request is almost comic when compared with the hostility of the previous year. Anne does not dwell triumphantly on her victory over the Pringles; instead, she enjoys the ease of a relationship that once seemed impossible.

The school curriculum promises to be more interesting, too. Canadian history has been added, and Anne is preparing a short talk on the War of 1812. She reflects, with the confidence of someone living before the First World War, that such battles belong safely to the past. Her belief is historically ironic for modern readers, but in the chapter it shows the peaceful assumptions of Anne’s world.

Another important plan is the reorganization of the High School Dramatic Club. The students need funds, so Anne and several helpers will canvass local families for subscriptions. Anne is paired with Lewis Allen, one of the older boys at the school. Lewis is hardworking, intelligent, and ambitious. He has very little money, but he refuses to complain about his situation. He spent the summer doing farm work and now earns his board by doing housework and waiting on tables.

Anne admires his determination. Lewis hopes to attend Queen’s after finishing High School, and every dollar matters. He is also interested in photography. A magazine called *Country Homes* is offering a twenty-five-dollar prize for the best photograph of an attractive farmhouse. Lewis hopes to combine the Dramatic Club canvassing trip with his search for a prize-winning subject. If he wins, he could buy a badly needed suit and overcoat.

Anne worries that Lewis works too hard and is not especially strong physically. She decides that Windy Poplars should invite him for Sunday supper from time to time. Since Aunt Kate and Aunt Chatty are careful about money, Anne quietly offers to cover the extra cost. Rather than asking Rebecca directly, Anne discusses the matter within Rebecca’s hearing. Rebecca immediately protests that it would be shameful not to feed a hardworking boy trying to educate himself. She even offers to sacrifice part of her wages. In the end, Lewis will come to supper, and no one has to lose anything. The whole exchange is humorous, but it also shows the generous heart beneath Rebecca’s sharp manner.

A smaller domestic episode involves Aunt Chatty, who secretly tells Anne that she longs to buy a beaded cape. Aunt Kate thinks she is too old for such a fashionable item, and Chatty’s feelings are hurt. Anne strongly rejects the idea that age should prevent someone from wearing something that gives genuine pleasure. She encourages Chatty to buy the cape and promises to help smooth things over with Kate.

By the end of the chapter, Anne is alone in the tower room, surrounded by silence. The second year has begun quietly, but several themes are already clear. Anne is no longer fighting to belong in Summerside; she belongs. She has deeper relationships, more confidence at school, and a wider circle of people she cares about. At the same time, she continues to notice the lonely, overlooked, or ambitious people around her and looks for practical ways to make their lives easier.

This chapter is therefore less about a dramatic event than about returning to a place that has changed because Anne herself has changed within it. Windy Poplars is not Green Gables, but it has become meaningful. Summerside is no longer simply a place where Anne teaches. It is a community in which she now has history, affection, and responsibilities.

## Vocabulary & Nuance

- **reconcile yourself to** — accept an unpleasant fact that cannot be changed easily.
- **rapturous** — showing overwhelming joy or delight.
- **disdainful** — showing that you think something is unworthy or foolish.
- **curriculum** — the subjects and material taught in a school.
- **canvass** — visit or contact people to ask for support, votes, donations, or opinions.
- **plucky** — brave, determined, and cheerful in difficulty.
- **build someone up** — make someone stronger or healthier.
- **put up the money** — provide the money needed for something.
- **make room for** — create space, time, or opportunity for someone or something.
- **smooth something over** — make a disagreement less serious or easier to manage.

## Conversation 1 — Coming Back to a Place That Changed

Mina: Was it strange going back after being away all summer?
Anne: A little. But it didn’t feel like returning to a strange town anymore.
Mina: So Summerside has **grown on you**?
Anne: Very much. I used to count the days until I could leave. Now I have people there I miss.
Mina: That’s how you know a place has become part of your life.
Anne: Exactly. Sometimes belonging **creeps up on** you before you notice it.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- grow on someone — become more appealing over time.
- creep up on someone — develop gradually without being noticed at first.
- settle back into — become comfortable again with a familiar routine.
- look forward to — feel pleased about something that will happen.
- make a place your own — become comfortable enough that a place feels personal.

## Conversation 2 — Supporting Someone Quietly

Anne: Lewis works incredibly hard, but I don’t want to embarrass him by making a fuss.
Rebecca: Then don’t make a fuss. Just feed him.
Anne: I thought we could invite him for supper twice a month.
Rebecca: Good. A young man studying that hard needs something decent to eat.
Anne: I can **cover the extra cost**.
Rebecca: Nonsense. We’ll **work it out**.
Anne: I knew you would say that.
Rebecca: Then why did you ask?
Anne: I didn’t exactly ask. I just happened to mention it where you could hear me.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- cover the cost — pay the amount required.
- work something out — find a practical solution.
- make a fuss — give something excessive attention.
- help someone out — provide useful assistance.
- get by — manage with limited money or resources.
