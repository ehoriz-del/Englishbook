# Chapter 13 — Two Weeks in Tomorrow

## Story Summary

When the second school year ended, Anne returned to Avonlea carrying both relief and a small disappointment. Gilbert would not be there for the summer. He had gone west to work on a new railway, and Green Gables would feel different without the possibility of meeting him on a road or finding him unexpectedly at the door. Even so, the familiar landscape still worked its old magic. The lake caught the summer light, the Haunted Wood waited in deep green shade, and home required no effort from her.

This time Anne brought Little Elizabeth. After careful persuasion, Mrs. Campbell had allowed the child to spend two weeks at Green Gables. Elizabeth treated the permission almost as a miracle and asked Anne to introduce her as Miss Elizabeth because the title felt suitable for such an important journey. Anne agreed with complete seriousness, remembering that she too had once wanted a different name to match the person she imagined herself becoming.

From Blight River onward, the trip seemed to Elizabeth like the beginning of another world. Fields moved past the windows, wild roses appeared along fences, and roads curved toward views she had never seen. She wore a new pink gingham dress and brown boots, but even new clothes became unimportant beside the freedom of going somewhere simply because it would be pleasant.

When they reached the Green Gables lane, Elizabeth became quiet with excitement. Tomorrow, the country of all her hopes, had always existed beyond ordinary life. Now she felt she had crossed its border. Fourteen days stretched ahead, and she counted them as fourteen separate Tomorrows.

Marilla approved of her manners, Mrs. Rachel Lynde studied her with interested attention, and Davy and Dora accepted her without ceremony. Elizabeth soon discovered that Green Gables had rules, but the rules did not feel like surveillance. People spoke freely when they had something to say, and laughter belonged to the life of the house instead of being treated as a disturbance that needed to be controlled. A child could move from one room to another without feeling that every footstep required permission.

On the first night Elizabeth and Anne slept in the little porch gable. Elizabeth whispered that she almost wished she did not need sleep because every sleeping hour would reduce the holiday. She listened to the sea and the wind around the eaves. Darkness at The Evergreens had often made her afraid. Here, with Anne nearby and a houseful of familiar voices below, the night seemed protective rather than threatening.

The days filled themselves. Elizabeth climbed into the Duchess apple tree with a book, gathered flowers, followed insects, watched bees, and treated every ordinary summer detail as discovery. She ate strawberries and cream with Dora, examined red currants as if they were jewels, and stood among June lilies at twilight until someone had to call her in. The people around her began collecting images of Elizabeth without intending to. She seemed to appear everywhere the season was most beautiful.

Her laughter changed most of all. At The Evergreens she had sometimes wanted to make noise simply because silence was required. Anne told her she could be as noisy as she wished at Green Gables. Once the permission existed, rebellion became unnecessary. Elizabeth laughed because things were funny, not because laughter itself felt forbidden. One windy afternoon, the hens provided enough comedy to leave her breathless, and soon everyone nearby was laughing too.

No one gave Elizabeth a formal lesson in happiness. She helped frost cakes, polish brass, cut biscuits, carry small things, and take part in the ordinary work of the household. That inclusion mattered more than special entertainment. At The Evergreens, she was often treated as a child who must be managed. At Green Gables, she was also a person whose presence contributed to the life of the house.

At night she sometimes compared the two homes after Anne had fallen asleep. She could not fully explain why Green Gables felt freer when Marilla certainly had rules of her own. The difference was not the absence of authority but the presence of affection, which changed the meaning of correction, expectation, and even ordinary household rules. Rules inside affection felt different from rules enforced mainly through fear.

The fortnight passed with the speed Anne had expected and Elizabeth had dreaded. On the journey back, the same roads that had seemed full of promise blurred through tears. Elizabeth wondered whether anything could ever be as happy again. Anne did not tell her to be grateful and stop crying. She knew the sadness measured the value of what the child had experienced.

Green Gables seemed quieter after Elizabeth left. Even Mrs. Lynde admitted she missed her. A bowl of berries, a flower in the garden, or a place under a tree could suddenly call up the image of a small golden haired girl discovering ordinary happiness with complete attention.

Later in the summer Katherine Brooke came to stay, bringing her dog and news of a different kind of departure. She had resigned from Summerside High. Teaching had never suited her, and she no longer intended to remain in it simply because it was the life she had already built. She planned to attend Redmond for secretarial training and create a path toward work that might eventually allow travel.

One evening Anne and Katherine sat in a clover field while the light faded over Avonlea. Katherine said, with a humor that would once have been impossible for her, that life owed her something and she intended to collect. She felt younger than she had a year earlier because the future was no longer one straight road she hated. Anne was delighted for her, though the thought of returning to Summerside without Katherine left a hollow place in the coming school year.

Anne and Katherine stayed in the clover field while the sunset faded. Katherine laughed as she spoke about the future, and Anne tried to imagine Summerside High without her at the next staff meeting. Katherine would go to Redmond in the fall for secretarial training, while Anne would return to Windy Poplars for her third and final year. The summer still lay ahead of them, but their daily companionship at school had already come to an end.

## Vocabulary & Nuance

- **surveillance** — close observation intended to control or monitor behavior.
- **alternative** — another possible choice or way of doing something.
- **contribute** — add something useful or valuable to a shared situation.
- **permission** — approval that allows someone to do something.
- **profession** — a type of work requiring skill or training.
- **take part in** — participate in an activity.
- **carry something back** — return with an idea, memory, or object.
- **move on from** — leave a stage or situation behind.
- **open up a possibility** — create a new possibility.
- **set out on** — begin a journey or new course of action.

## Conversation 1 — Elizabeth at Green Gables

Elizabeth: Nobody tells me to be quiet every few minutes.
Anne: You can **take part in** the house instead of feeling as if you are always being supervised.
Elizabeth: Even helping with biscuits feels exciting.
Anne: That is because ordinary things feel different when you belong.
Elizabeth: I wish I could carry all of this back with me.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- throw yourself into — become actively and enthusiastically involved in something.
- take something away from — retain an idea, memory, or lesson from an experience.
- feel at home — feel naturally comfortable and accepted.
- grow accustomed to — gradually become comfortable with a situation.
- make the most of — use an opportunity as fully as possible.

## Conversation 2 — Katherine Chooses a New Direction

Anne: You really resigned?
Katherine: I did. I am done treating one qualification as a life sentence.
Anne: And Redmond is next?
Katherine: Yes. I am ready to **set out on** something I actually chose.
Anne: That sounds like the best kind of beginning.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- set out on — begin a journey or new course.
- move on from — leave a stage behind.
- branch out — try a new area or direction.
- follow through — complete a plan or commitment.
- start over — begin again in a new way.
