# Chapter 11 — Hazel’s Accusation

## Story Summary

Anne is in the tower room at Windy Poplars on a mid-June evening, correcting examination papers. She has a severe cold and has spent so much time wiping her nose that it is red and painful. The beautiful evening outside cannot improve her mood. She tells Dusty Miller that a cold in June is especially unfair and comforts herself by thinking that in two weeks she will be back at Green Gables.

While Anne is working, Hazel Marr arrives unexpectedly. Hazel has just returned from Kingsport and is clearly angry. She comes into the tower room almost immediately and accuses Anne of trying to take Terry away from her.

Anne is shocked. Hazel says that she knows Anne told Terry that Hazel did not love him and wanted to end their engagement. Anne points out that Hazel herself had said exactly those things in this same room. Hazel reacts as though Anne has misunderstood something obvious. She says she had only been in a mood and never expected Anne to take her dramatic words seriously.

Hazel complains that Anne should have understood the way young girls sometimes talk. She also says Anne is older and should have known better. Anne, who is already uncomfortable because of her cold and busy with examination papers, becomes increasingly exasperated.

Hazel continues pacing around the room. She accuses Anne of jealousy and says Anne has deliberately damaged her happiness. She talks as if her whole future has been destroyed. She reminds Anne that she had trusted her and had considered her a true friend.

Anne tries to make Hazel sit down and speak calmly, but Hazel refuses. She says she cannot sit quietly when her life is in ruins. She even suggests that Anne may have become jealous of younger women because she is older.

Anne has to control her temper. She tells Hazel that if she cannot speak sensibly, she should leave because Anne has work to do.

Hazel then attacks Anne’s appearance and character. She says she should have known from Anne’s red hair and green eyes that Anne was dangerous. She claims she never imagined Anne would go so far as to cause trouble between Hazel and Terry.

Hazel begins crying. She says she had already planned the wedding and imagined four bridesmaids in pale blue silk dresses. She says she does not know whether she hates Anne or pities her more.

Anne listens with growing disbelief. She remembers that Hazel had recently told her she did not want to marry Terry at all. Now Hazel speaks as though Anne has destroyed the greatest romance of her life.

Hazel says her mother will be devastated because everyone thought the engagement was perfect. Anne, unable to resist a little humor, suggests that Hazel wait for another moonlit night and see whether her feelings change again.

Hazel is offended and insists that Anne does not understand real suffering. She says her feelings are deep and that only shallow people recover easily from emotional pain.

She explains that before leaving for Kingsport she had told Terry to be kind to Anne while Hazel was away. When Hazel came home, Terry told her he no longer loved her and believed their engagement had been a mistake. He also told Hazel that Anne had said Hazel wanted to be free.

Anne answers that her intentions were honorable. By this point, she begins to see the absurd side of the situation and laughs partly at herself as well as at Hazel.

Hazel says she spent the night walking the floor. She has also heard people gossiping about Terry’s interest in Anne. According to Hazel, people have noticed that Terry has been spending time with Anne while Hazel was away.

Hazel asks why Anne could not leave Terry alone when Anne already has Gilbert. She wants to know what she ever did to deserve such treatment.

Anne finally tells Hazel that both she and Terry need a good spanking. Anne still tries to explain that Hazel herself had said she wanted the engagement ended, but Hazel refuses to listen.

Hazel says she has been betrayed in both love and friendship. She also suddenly denies many of the things she said during her earlier visit. When Anne reminds her about the nursing career, the millionaire patient, and the Mediterranean honeymoon she once imagined, Hazel acts as if she has no idea what Anne means.

Hazel now says she is not ambitious at all. Her highest wish, she claims, has always been to become a loving wife and create a happy home.

Anne realizes that continuing the conversation will accomplish nothing. She tells Hazel that she is busy and that the interview should end.

Hazel moves toward the door in a highly dramatic manner. Before leaving, she tells Anne that she leaves her to her conscience.

Once Hazel is gone, Anne stops working and thinks carefully about everything that has happened.

She admits to herself that she has made mistakes. Hazel had flattered her, admired her, and treated her as if she were unusually wise. Anne had enjoyed being admired more than she had realized.

She also has to admit that she liked the idea of solving Hazel and Terry’s problem. She believed she understood both young people and could save them from a foolish engagement.

Anne now sees that neither Hazel nor Terry had been as certain as they sounded. Their feelings changed quickly, and Anne had entered a situation that was much less simple than she thought.

She remembers Rebecca Dew’s warnings about interfering in other people’s romances. Anne gives herself a firm private lecture and accepts that she still has things to learn.

Then she returns to her examination papers. One student has written that a seraph is an animal found in Africa, which gives Anne a final small comic distraction after the exhausting visit.

## Vocabulary & Nuance

- **accusation** — a statement claiming that someone has done something wrong.
- **exasperated** — extremely annoyed and frustrated.
- **betrayal** — the act of breaking someone’s trust.
- **infatuation** — a strong but often short-lived romantic attraction.
- **flattery** — praise that may be excessive or intended to influence someone.
- **burst in** — enter suddenly and forcefully.
- **calm down** — become less angry or emotional.
- **walk out** — leave suddenly, often because of anger.
- **get carried away** — become too excited or emotionally involved.
- **take your medicine** — accept the unpleasant consequences of your own mistake.

## Conversation 1 — When Advice Backfires

Mina: I tried to help two friends with their relationship, and now both of them are angry with me.
Daniel: Did they ask you to get involved?
Mina: One of them did, but now she says she never meant it seriously.
Daniel: That is exactly why relationship advice can **backfire**.
Mina: I thought I was helping.
Daniel: You probably were trying to help, but sometimes people change their minds faster than we expect.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- backfire — produce the opposite result from what was intended.
- get involved — become part of a situation.
- change your mind — decide differently later.
- calm down — become less emotional.
- sort something out — resolve a difficult situation.

## Conversation 2 — Admitting Your Own Part

Anne: I still think Hazel is being unfair, but I made mistakes too.
Rebecca: Such as
Anne: I enjoyed being treated as if I understood everything.
Rebecca: Flattery can do that.
Anne: And I got carried away with the idea that I could fix their engagement.
Rebecca: At least you can admit it now.
Anne: I would rather learn the lesson once than repeat it.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- get carried away — become too excited or involved.
- own up to — admit responsibility.
- learn from — gain useful understanding from an experience.
- step back — stop being deeply involved and look at a situation more calmly.
- move on — continue after a difficult experience.
