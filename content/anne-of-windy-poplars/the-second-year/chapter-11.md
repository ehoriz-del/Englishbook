# Chapter 11 — Hazel Turns on Anne

## Story Summary

By the middle of June, the school year was almost finished, but Anne had little energy for anticipation. A heavy cold had settled on her just when the weather became beautiful, and she spent the evening in the tower room correcting examination papers with a sore throat, an aching head, and a handkerchief always within reach. Outside, the harbor lay under a soft green sky and lilacs scented the air. Anne regarded the contrast between the lovely evening and her miserable condition as a personal insult.

Dusty Miller occupied the window ledge while she worked, occasionally turning his head as if supervising the papers. Anne tried to comfort herself by thinking of Green Gables, where she would be in two weeks, free for a while from examinations, meetings, and students who confused historical figures with imaginary creatures. She had just finished marking one particularly hopeless answer when quick footsteps sounded below.

Hazel Marr entered almost at once. She had returned from Kingsport earlier than Anne expected, and the adoration that usually softened her face had disappeared. She stood in the room with the dramatic stillness of someone who had rehearsed anger on the way over. Then, without easing into the subject, she accused Anne of trying to take Terry away from her.

Anne stared at her and sneezed. For several seconds the charge seemed too absurd to answer. Hazel pressed on. Terry had told her that Anne said Hazel no longer loved him and wanted to end the engagement. Anne admitted she had said exactly that because those were the words Hazel herself had used in the tower room only a few weeks earlier.

Hazel reacted as though literal memory were a form of betrayal. She insisted she had been in a mood. She had spoken dramatically because young women sometimes did. Anne, being older and supposedly wiser, should have understood that such emotions were temporary. Hazel had never imagined that Anne would carry private words directly to Terry and treat them as a settled decision.

The criticism was unfair, yet it landed close enough to truth to make Anne uncomfortable. She tried to slow the conversation and asked Hazel to sit down, but Hazel was too agitated. She moved around the room describing a future that had supposedly been destroyed. There had been plans for a wedding, pale blue dresses for bridesmaids, family excitement, and a picture of married happiness that now seemed to have collapsed because Terry believed she wanted freedom.

Hazel's anger broke into tears as quickly as it had risen. She dropped into a rocking chair and worried about what the scandal would do to her mother, who had been delighted by the engagement. Then she described the misery of returning from Kingsport only to hear people talking about Terry's interest in Anne. To Hazel, the gossip proved that everyone had been watching while she was away. Anne, fighting another sneeze and a growing sense of absurdity, could see that facts and drama had become hopelessly tangled together.

Then Hazel's anger became more personal as she suggested that Anne had been jealous and pointed to the time Terry had spent with her as evidence of motives Anne had never possessed. Why, Hazel demanded, should a woman who already had Gilbert need the attention of somebody else's fiancé. The accusation was ridiculous, but Anne's cold had left her with very little patience. She had to make a deliberate effort not to answer with the sharpness Hazel was inviting.

Anne reminded her that she had once spoken passionately about careers, independence, and the fear of marrying too soon. Hazel brushed those ambitions aside as if they belonged to another person. She had always wanted to be a wife, she now declared, and the rest had merely been passing ideas. Anne could hardly believe she was listening to the same girl who had recently imagined herself as a nurse, traveler, and possible nun.

Hazel finally moved toward the door with wounded dignity. Anne, thoroughly exasperated, suggested that both Hazel and Terry needed a good dose of common sense, but Hazel insisted that she was not angry, only deeply hurt. She spoke of betrayed love and betrayed friendship as if both had been destroyed beyond repair. Anne, tired from her cold and from trying to untangle the same facts yet again, told her there was nothing to be gained by prolonging the interview.

At the door Hazel turned for one final dramatic farewell and told Anne she would leave her to her conscience. Then she was gone. Anne sat alone in the tower room, sneezed several times, laid down her pen, and decided that her conscience deserved an honest hearing. Hazel's accusations were exaggerated, but Anne could not pretend she herself had behaved with perfect judgment.

She admitted that Hazel's admiration had flattered her. Being treated as wise, sympathetic, and uniquely capable of understanding other people had been pleasant. She had also enjoyed the idea of stepping into a confused situation and putting it right for two younger people. Rebecca Dew had warned her against meddling, but Anne had liked the role too well to listen carefully enough.

Having made the admission, Anne did not continue punishing herself. She picked up her pen and returned to the examination papers, feeling wiser, sadder, and considerably older than she had before Hazel arrived. Almost at once schoolwork restored her sense of proportion. Myra Pringle had defined a seraph as an African animal. Anne laughed, marked the answer, and went on correcting papers.

## Vocabulary & Nuance

- **agitated** — visibly anxious, upset, or emotionally disturbed.
- **betrayal** — an act that violates trust or loyalty.
- **accountability** — responsibility for one’s own actions and decisions.
- **literal** — based on the exact meaning of words rather than an implied meaning.
- **self-knowledge** — clear understanding of one’s own motives, strengths, and weaknesses.
- **call someone out** — directly challenge someone about behavior you believe is wrong.
- **take over** — assume control or responsibility.
- **own up to** — admit responsibility for something.
- **get caught up in** — become too involved in a situation or emotion.
- **step back** — reduce involvement in order to see a situation more clearly.

## Conversation 1 — Hazel Confronts Anne

Hazel: You told Terry I wanted out.
Anne: I repeated what you told me because I thought you meant it.
Hazel: You should have known I was upset, not making a final decision.
Anne: Then I should have **stepped back** and told you to speak to him yourself.
Hazel: Instead you got involved in something that was not yours to manage.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- step back — reduce involvement and gain perspective.
- want out — want to leave a relationship or commitment.
- get involved in — become part of a situation.
- speak for — express another person’s position or wishes.
- sort out — resolve confusion or disagreement.

## Conversation 2 — Anne Reflects Afterward

Anne: I meant to help, but I still crossed a boundary.
Friend: Good intentions can make that hard to see.
Anne: I got **caught up in** being the person who could fix everything.
Friend: At least you can own up to that.
Anne: And next time I can leave the decision where it belongs.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- get caught up in — become too involved in something.
- own up to — admit responsibility.
- cross a boundary — go beyond an appropriate limit.
- leave something with someone — allow the responsible person to handle it.
- learn from — use an experience to improve future judgment.
