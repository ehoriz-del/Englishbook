# Chapter 9 — A Letter to Elizabeth’s Father

## Story Summary

By March, the secret wedding at Elmcroft had become one more Summerside story that people repeated with diminishing excitement. Dovie and Jarvis were safely married, Franklin Westcott had behaved far better than anyone expected, and Anne had gone back to the ordinary rhythm of school, Windy Poplars, and evening letters to Gilbert. Then a small cream-colored note arrived and gave Rebecca Dew something new to discuss.

Miss Minerva Tomgallon had invited Anne to supper.

Rebecca read the news in Anne's face before Anne had finished taking off her gloves. An invitation to Tomgallon House, she declared, was no common social call. The Tomgallons had once stood above nearly everyone in Summerside, and Minerva, the last of the family, still lived in the great Queen Street mansion as though the old importance had never quite faded. Anne had seen the house often enough from the road. Its many chimneys, green shutters, and stained-glass window gave it dignity, but there was something withdrawn about it too, as if the house had turned inward and was living on its memories.

Rebecca immediately began considering what Anne should wear. Anne listened with amusement, yet her mind kept drifting elsewhere. The invitation promised an interesting evening, but another matter had been troubling her for weeks, and unlike Miss Minerva's supper it could not be treated as a curiosity.

Little Elizabeth was nearly ten now. Her visits to Windy Poplars were freer than they had once been, and the summer at Green Gables had given her memories bright enough to last through many dreary days. Even so, Anne could see more clearly than ever what would happen when she herself left Summerside. Mrs. Campbell provided Elizabeth with good clothes, careful meals, and a respectable home. The Woman made sure rules were obeyed. What neither of them seemed able to provide was the ordinary tenderness Elizabeth wanted so desperately and tried so hard not to ask for.

One afternoon the child told Anne, with complete seriousness, that she had written a letter to God.

Anne's first impulse was to smile, but something in Elizabeth's face stopped her. Prayer had not brought her father back, Elizabeth explained, and God must receive so many prayers that perhaps a letter would attract more attention. She had written asking that her father be brought home and made to love her. Then, on the way back from school, she had stood in an empty lot, looked up at the sky, and read the letter aloud.

Anne listened without interrupting. By the time Elizabeth finished, the familiar anger she sometimes felt toward The Evergreens had been replaced by something quieter and more determined. A child should not have to invent a better method of reaching heaven because the adults around her had failed to explain why her father was absent.

That evening Anne sat for a long time at her desk before beginning a second letter. This one was not for Gilbert. Rebecca had once heard the name of the company whose Paris branch Pierce Grayson managed, and although Anne did not know his private address, she decided to send the letter through the firm and trust that it would reach him.

She wrote carefully. It would have been easy to accuse a man she had never met, but Anne knew there were parts of the Grayson and Campbell history she did not understand. Instead she stayed with what she did know. She described Elizabeth as she had come to know her, imaginative, sensitive, hungry for affection, and increasingly aware of the coldness in the house where she lived. She told Pierce that his daughter longed for him and dreamed about him. Then, after reading the page twice, Anne added the sentence she had been avoiding. He ought to come for his child.

Once the envelope was sealed, doubt returned. Perhaps Pierce would consider the letter an outrageous intrusion. Perhaps he had reasons for remaining away. Perhaps the letter would disappear somewhere between Prince Edward Island and Paris and nothing would ever come of it. Anne carried it to the post anyway. She knew herself well enough to understand that if she did nothing, she would wonder for years whether caution had become cowardice.

Windy Poplars, fortunately, offered a less serious drama almost at once.

Aunt Kate and Aunt Chatty had finally acted on Rebecca's endless complaints about Dusty Miller. Since Rebecca had spent the summer declaring that the cat disturbed her kitchen, shed hair where no respectable cat should, and expected doors to be opened for him at impossible hours, the widows arranged a new home with Mrs. Edmonds. Anne came back from school one evening and discovered that Dusty was gone.

She was genuinely sorry, though Aunt Kate reminded her that the arrangement was supposed to please Rebecca. Everyone waited for the pleasure to appear.

Rebecca returned at dusk, went through her usual evening routine, and eventually called for Dusty. Aunt Kate told her there was no need. The cat had been given away.

For a moment Rebecca seemed unable to understand the words. Then indignation swept over her with such force that Anne had to turn her face aside. Dusty Miller, Rebecca announced, had a home already. He belonged at Windy Poplars. She had raised him from a kitten, worried over him, fed him, and stood outside in winter calling him when any sensible creature would have come in hours earlier. Mrs. Edmonds would never care for him properly. The idea that Rebecca herself had demanded his removal was dismissed as though it were a malicious rumor.

Within minutes she had threatened to leave the household. Aunt Kate, completely defeated by the logic of a woman whose complaints had somehow become proof of devotion, promised that Dusty would be brought back. The next day Aunt Chatty returned with a covered basket. Rebecca received the restored cat with the solemn triumph of someone whose rights had been publicly recognized.

Anne suspected the widows might have understood Rebecca better than they admitted. After Dusty's return, complaints about him became remarkably rare.

That night Anne sat again in the tower room, smiling over the absurdity of the cat affair while thinking about the letter already traveling east. Rebecca had needed the threat of loss before she could admit how deeply she cared. Anne wondered whether Pierce Grayson would discover something equally important when her letter reached him.

There was no answer yet, and Anne refused to build hope out of silence. She closed the desk, chose the green voile she would wear to Tomgallon House, and turned her attention to the invitation waiting for the following evening. Still, before blowing out the lamp, she looked once toward the dark shape of The Evergreens beyond the trees. Somewhere far away, a letter was carrying Elizabeth's name toward a father who had been absent much too long.

## Vocabulary & Nuance

- **intrusion** — involvement in a private matter that may not be welcome.
- **emotional neglect** — a failure to provide enough affection, attention, or emotional support.
- **misgiving** — a feeling of doubt about whether an action is wise or right.
- **diplomatic** — careful in wording so that honesty does not create unnecessary offense.
- **compelling** — powerful enough to demand attention or action.
- **indignation** — anger caused by something felt to be unfair or insulting.
- **contradiction** — a situation in which behavior and stated beliefs do not match.
- **acknowledge** — admit that something is true or important.
- **reconcile** — bring opposing feelings, ideas, or people into a more workable relationship.
- **unresolved** — not yet settled, answered, or completed.

## Conversation 1 — Anne Decides to Write

Anne: I keep asking myself whether writing to him is overstepping.
Rebecca: Maybe. But leaving things exactly as they are is a decision too.
Anne: I do not want to make accusations about a family history I do not fully understand.
Rebecca: Then do not. Tell him what you know about the child and let him answer for himself.
Anne: That feels fair. I can speak up for Elizabeth without pretending I know everything.

### Phrasal Verbs & Expressions

- **overstep** — go beyond an appropriate limit.
- **leave things as they are** — allow a situation to continue unchanged.
- **speak up for** — support or defend someone who may have less power.
- **reach out to** — make contact with someone, often to offer or request help.
- **leave it up to** — allow another person to make the final decision.

## Conversation 2 — Rebecca Learns Dusty Is Gone

Rebecca: You actually gave him away without telling me
Aunt Kate: You have been saying for months that he drives you crazy.
Rebecca: That does not mean I wanted him removed from his own home.
Aunt Kate: If we bring him back, are you going to keep complaining
Rebecca: I may have to rethink a few things.
Anne: That sounds remarkably close to a confession of affection.

### Phrasal Verbs & Expressions

- **give away** — give something to another person permanently.
- **drive someone crazy** — annoy or frustrate someone greatly.
- **bring back** — return someone or something to a former place.
- **rethink** — consider again and possibly change one’s view.
- **come close to** — nearly reach a certain point or admission.
