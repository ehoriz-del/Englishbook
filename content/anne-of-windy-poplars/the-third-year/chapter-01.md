# Chapter 1 — Back at Windy Poplars

## Story Summary

Anne came back to Summerside in early September for her third and final year as principal of the High School. The town looked almost exactly as she remembered it, but the return did not feel quite the same. Summer had ended too quickly, Gilbert was far away again, and Katherine Brooke was no longer waiting somewhere in Summerside for one of their long evening conversations. Anne settled into the tower room at Windy Poplars and opened her writing desk. As soon as she began a letter to Gilbert, the familiar room seemed to welcome her home.

There were new faces at school. A young junior teacher had joined the staff, a cheerful, rosy woman whom Anne liked at once. She was friendly and pleasant, and working with her would probably be easy. Still, Anne knew she would not take Katherine’s place. Katherine had been difficult, wounded, sharp, and surprising. Their friendship had grown slowly through misunderstanding and honesty, and Anne missed the feeling that there was always another layer of Katherine waiting to be discovered.

Windy Poplars had changed in smaller ways too. The old red cow had died during the summer, and Aunt Kate, Aunt Chatty, and Rebecca Dew had decided not to replace her. From now on they would buy milk and cream from Mr. Cherry. Rebecca spoke of the change with more feeling than she wanted anyone to notice. The cow had been part of the daily life of the house for years, and its absence made the yard seem strangely empty.

Anne thought at once of Little Elizabeth. For a long time Elizabeth had used the milk errand as an excuse to come through the gate and spend a few precious minutes at Windy Poplars. She had always needed some practical reason for leaving The Evergreens. Now that the cow was gone, that little excuse had disappeared. Fortunately, Mrs. Campbell had become less severe about Elizabeth’s visits. The child could sometimes come simply because she wanted to see Anne, and that small change gave Anne hope.

Another household matter worried her much more. Aunt Kate quietly mentioned that Dusty Miller might have to go. Rebecca had complained about the cat so often during the summer that the widows had begun to think the only peaceful solution was to find him another home. Anne protested immediately. Dusty Miller might leave hairs on chairs, appear where he was not wanted, and irritate Rebecca beyond reason, but he belonged to Windy Poplars. Anne could not imagine the tower room without his soft paws on the floorboards or the kitchen without him waiting hopefully near Rebecca’s feet.

Rebecca pretended that Anne’s feelings were foolish. A sensible household, she said, should not be ruled by a cat. Yet there was something in the way she spoke that made Anne suspect the matter was not as simple as it sounded. For the moment Dusty remained, and Anne decided not to argue further.

Before the school week had properly settled into its rhythm, Anne was asked to do a favor for Mrs. Raymond. Mrs. Raymond had moved to Summerside the previous winter and was known for being attractive, fashionable, and rather proud of her ideas about children. She had also been generous to the school Dramatic Club, and Anne felt grateful for that kindness. So when Mrs. Raymond asked whether Anne could spend Saturday with her eight-year-old twins while she attended a funeral in Charlottetown, Anne agreed without much hesitation.

The twins were Gerald and Geraldine. Anne had seen them before and remembered two beautiful children with pale golden hair, clear blue eyes, and faces so innocent that they might have belonged in a church window. She imagined a lively but pleasant day. She had always liked children, and years of teaching had given her confidence. A pair of energetic eight-year-olds did not seem like a serious challenge.

Rebecca Dew reacted as if Anne had announced plans to spend Saturday in a cage with two young tigers. She stopped what she was doing and gave Anne a long, doubtful look. Gerald and Geraldine, she warned, were famous all over the neighborhood. Their angelic faces had deceived many people before. Mrs. Raymond believed too strongly in allowing children to be natural, and in Rebecca’s opinion the twins had become more natural than civilization could safely manage.

Rebecca had stories to prove it. Once the minister’s wife had called at the Raymond house. As she was leaving, a shower of Spanish onions had come down the stairs, and one had knocked off her hat. Mrs. Raymond had treated the affair as an unfortunate result of high spirits. Rebecca clearly thought stronger measures were needed.

Anne laughed, but Rebecca did not. She reminded Anne that the twins quarreled, shouted, invented dangerous games, and rarely accepted ordinary limits. Anne replied that children often behaved better when they felt understood. Patience and kindness, she believed, could accomplish a great deal.

Rebecca gave a short sound that expressed doubt more clearly than a speech. Loving children was one thing, she said. Spending an entire day alone with Gerald and Geraldine was another.

Anne refused to be discouraged. She had faced the Pringles, managed difficult students, and survived more than one Summerside misunderstanding. She would not begin her final year by being afraid of two small children. Even so, when she returned to the tower room that evening, Rebecca’s warning stayed in her thoughts.

She looked around the room she had grown to love. Outside, September light rested softly on the trees. Inside, Dusty Miller had found a comfortable place near her chair as though no one had ever discussed giving him away. Anne smiled and returned to her letter to Gilbert.

Her third year in Summerside had begun quietly, but already the old life was shifting around her. Katherine was gone. Little Elizabeth was growing older. Dusty Miller’s future was uncertain. And on Saturday Anne would discover whether Gerald and Geraldine were really the small angels they appeared to be, or whether Rebecca Dew had been telling the truth all along.

## Vocabulary & Nuance

- **reconciled** — willing to accept a situation that cannot easily be changed.
- **protest** — express strong disagreement or objection.
- **suitable** — right or appropriate for a particular purpose.
- **permissive** — allowing a great deal of freedom and setting few limits.
- **unmanageable** — very difficult to control.
- **settle back into** — become comfortable again in a familiar routine.
- **give away** — transfer something to another person without payment.
- **look after** — take care of someone.
- **take someone in** — deceive someone by appearing different from what you really are.
- **make light of** — treat something as less serious than it really is.

## Conversation 1 — Returning to a Familiar Place

Mina: Does coming back feel strange after the summer?
Anne: A little. I have **settled back into** the tower room quickly, but Summerside feels different without Katherine.
Mina: At least Windy Poplars is familiar.
Anne: Yes. I know the house so well now that even the smallest changes stand out.
Mina: Such as the cow being gone?
Anne: And Dusty Miller possibly being given away. I am not happy about that at all.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- settle back into — become comfortable again with a familiar routine.
- stand out — be especially noticeable.
- get used to — become accustomed to something.
- miss out on — lose the chance to experience something.
- hold on to — keep something rather than let it go.

## Conversation 2 — A Warning About the Twins

Rebecca: Those children may look like angels, but do not let that fool you.
Anne: I have looked after plenty of children.
Rebecca: Not Gerald and Geraldine.
Anne: You think they will be impossible?
Rebecca: I think you will **find out** soon enough.
Anne: I still believe patience will help.
Rebecca: Then I hope your patience can **hold up** until supper.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- find out — discover the truth.
- hold up — remain strong or effective.
- fool someone — deceive someone.
- get away with — avoid punishment for something wrong.
- wear someone out — make someone extremely tired.
