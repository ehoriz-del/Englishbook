# Chapter 14 — Goodbye to Windy Poplars

## Story Summary

On June twenty-seventh, Anne sat in the tower room at Windy Poplars and began a letter to Gilbert with the knowledge that it would be the last one written there. For three years the room had been the private place where she turned the life of Summerside into stories for him. Soon there would be no need to describe her days across a distance. In a few weeks they would be together, making the home they had imagined for so long.

The thought filled Anne with happiness, but it did not make leaving easy. When she first arrived in Summerside, three years had seemed almost endless. Now the time had disappeared with surprising speed. The early struggle with the Pringles felt remote enough to belong to another version of herself. The family that had once worked against her now treated her with affection. One of the Pringle girls had even brought roses with a note calling Anne the sweetest teacher in the world. The gesture made Anne laugh, but it also reminded her how completely relationships could change.

School was ending in a series of farewells. Jen Pringle was heartbroken that Anne was leaving, though Anne expected Jen to create an interesting life wherever she went. Other students were preparing for university, work, society, or further study. Anne listened to their plans with the strange mixture of pride and detachment teachers often feel when students begin moving beyond the classroom. She had influenced them, but their futures were no longer hers to direct.

The most important absence was already visible next door. Little Elizabeth had left The Evergreens with Pierce Grayson. He would not return to Paris but planned to live in Boston with his daughter. Elizabeth had cried when she said goodbye to Anne, yet the tears belonged to separation rather than despair. She was going with a father who had recognized how much he had missed and was determined not to repeat the mistake.

Mrs. Campbell and the Woman blamed Anne for the entire change. Anne accepted the accusation without regret. A home that had never given Elizabeth open affection could not claim that good clothes and rules were enough. Elizabeth herself decided that she would be Betty most of the time in her new life, though she joked that she might become Lizzie whenever she was lonely for Miss Shirley. Anne firmly rejected the possibility of Lizzie under any circumstances.

Before Elizabeth left, Anne gave her their map of fairyland as a keepsake. The gift seemed appropriate because they had spent years building imaginary roads toward freedom, and now Elizabeth had taken a real one. With that goodbye behind her, Anne began noticing the ordinary details of Windy Poplars more intensely. She would miss the view from the tower room, where she could see both sunrise and sunset. She would miss the winds that seemed to have different personalities as they moved around the house. She would miss the awkward little bed she had learned to climb into, the blue cushion, the garden, and the familiar sounds below the stairs.

Most of all, she would miss the people. Aunt Chatty's soft eyes filled whenever Anne mentioned departure. Aunt Kate remained more controlled, but her affection was unmistakable. Rebecca Dew responded through food. For a week she prepared Anne's favorite dishes, brought out the company china, and used enough eggs for repeated angel cakes to prove that ordinary economy had temporarily surrendered to love. Even Dusty Miller seemed to watch Anne as if he understood another member of the household was preparing to disappear.

Letters from Katherine added another kind of satisfaction. Katherine had found work as private secretary to a widely traveled member of Parliament and was enthusiastic about the life ahead. She continued to credit Anne with changing her direction. Anne knew the change had ultimately been Katherine's own work, but she allowed herself to be glad that friendship had helped make another future imaginable.

Summerside itself seemed determined to say goodbye through invitations. People asked Anne to supper, including some who would never have approached her during the difficult first months. She visited Tomgallon House again, where Miss Minerva supplied another excellent meal and another collection of tragedies. This time the old woman gave Anne a beautiful aquamarine ring that had been hers since girlhood. Anne accepted it as a sign of affection rather than family grandeur.

As the final days passed, Anne grew tired of examinations, farewell visits, and the phrase for the last time. She walked once more through the old graveyard, looked toward the Storm King, and let familiar paths become memories. At the same time, Green Gables pulled at her imagination. She planned to spend her first week home doing almost nothing useful. She would walk through the Haunted Wood, look for wild strawberries, sit under the stars, and recover from months of responsibility before Gilbert returned.

On the last morning, the hardest goodbye waited inside Windy Poplars. Rebecca was nowhere to be seen. Aunt Kate handed Anne a letter instead. Rebecca had written because she did not trust herself to speak. The language was grand, formal, and clearly influenced by the etiquette books she loved, but the emotion beneath it was entirely her own. She thanked Anne for three years of kindness, offered solemn wishes for her future marriage, and ended with a simple blessing.

Anne's eyes filled as she read. The elaborate phrases made her smile, but they did not weaken the sincerity. Rebecca, who could complain about a cat for years rather than admit she loved him, had once again hidden tenderness behind an indirect form.

Anne asked Aunt Kate to tell Rebecca she would never forget her and promised to return in future summers. Aunt Chatty wept openly, while Aunt Kate held herself together long enough to say that they would keep memories nothing could take away. Then Anne climbed into the carriage and began to leave Spook's Lane. Windy Poplars stood behind her, white and green among the trees, already turning from home into memory. She looked back for one final sign, and a large white bath towel suddenly appeared at the tower window, waving with such determination that there could be no doubt who held it. Rebecca Dew had found a way to say goodbye after all, and Anne laughed through her tears as the carriage moved on. The farewell was ridiculous, affectionate, and completely right. Windy Poplars had never been grand in the manner of Tomgallon House, but it had given Anne something more valuable than grandeur. It had given her a living household in which affection appeared through arguments, cakes, secret kindnesses, worried advice, cat complaints, and, at the very end, a bath towel flying from a window.

Ahead waited Gilbert and the house of dreams. Behind her remained three years she had not expected to love so much. Anne had always believed that life was full of bends in the road. Leaving Summerside did not erase what lay behind the bend. It simply allowed her to carry it forward.

## Vocabulary & Nuance

- **detachment** — enough emotional distance to view a situation with perspective.
- **keepsake** — an object preserved because it carries personal memories.
- **unmistakable** — so clear that it cannot reasonably be interpreted another way.
- **wistful** — gently sad because something valued is ending or out of reach.
- **retrospective** — looking back on and reconsidering past experience.
- **sincerity** — honest and genuine feeling that is not performed for effect.
- **elaborate** — highly detailed or carefully developed, sometimes more than necessary.
- **understatement** — expression that deliberately presents something as less intense than it is.
- **sentimentality** — strong emotional attachment, sometimes expressed in an idealized way.
- **continuity** — the connection that allows meaning and identity to carry through change.

## Conversation 1 — Anne Reflects on Leaving

Gilbert: You sound happy and sad at the same time.
Anne: I am. I cannot wait for what comes next, but I do not want to pretend these years meant less just because they are ending.
Gilbert: You do not have to choose between looking back and moving forward.
Anne: I know. I think I am finally learning how to carry both at once.
Gilbert: That sounds like a better kind of goodbye.

### Phrasal Verbs & Expressions

- **look back on** — remember and reflect on something from the past.
- **move forward** — continue into the next stage of life.
- **carry something with you** — keep the emotional value or lesson of an experience.
- **come to an end** — finish.
- **make peace with** — accept something emotionally after difficulty or uncertainty.

## Conversation 2 — Rebecca Cannot Say Goodbye

Aunt Kate: Rebecca wrote you a letter because she says she cannot trust herself to say goodbye properly.
Anne: That may be the most Rebecca thing she could possibly do.
Aunt Chatty: She has been holding it together all week.
Anne: Please tell her she does not have to. I know exactly what she means to me.
Aunt Kate: I think she knows, but that will not stop her from pretending otherwise until the last possible minute.

### Phrasal Verbs & Expressions

- **hold it together** — keep control of one’s emotions under pressure.
- **mean a great deal to someone** — be deeply important on an emotional level.
- **keep up appearances** — maintain an outward impression that hides stronger private feelings.
- **part with** — separate from someone or something valued, often reluctantly.
- **carry something forward** — preserve the value or lesson of an experience while moving into a new stage.
