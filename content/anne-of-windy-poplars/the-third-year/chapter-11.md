# Chapter 11 — A Night at Tomgallon House

## Story Summary

What begins as an unusual supper invitation becomes a far stranger adventure when Anne is forced to spend the night at Tomgallon House. The evening continues after supper with firelight, crocheting, knitting, and Miss Minerva’s endless procession of family stories. Anne is polite, but inwardly she grows more and more overwhelmed. Every pleasant object seems linked to some sorrowful anecdote. Even the beauty of the rooms becomes oppressive because it is never allowed to exist by itself. Everything in the house must serve memory.

As the night deepens, the weather worsens. Wind and storm gather outside, making any return to Windy Poplars impossible. Miss Minerva decides at once that Anne must stay. Anne would have preferred almost any other solution, but there is none. Her hostess leads her toward the bedchamber prepared for guests, and here the real comedy of fear begins. The room is no ordinary bedroom but the old chamber of Aunt Annabella, one of the tragic Tomgallon ancestors. Miss Minerva gives Anne a full account of Aunt Annabella’s unhappy history before leaving her there.

The chamber contains everything an imaginative young woman might wish to avoid on a stormy night. There are old portraits, heavy curtains, ancient furniture, mirrors that catch unexpected reflections, a towering bed, and the sense that generations of memory still cling to the walls. Anne, whose imagination is both her delight and her danger, tries to behave sensibly. She reminds herself that she is no child and that ghosts are not likely to appear simply because the wind howls in an old house. Yet once she is alone, all reason becomes difficult to maintain.

Ordinary sounds take on sinister meaning. The branches outside scrape or tap. Curtains stir. Shadows lengthen and change shape. The room’s grandeur seems transformed into threat. Anne wishes intensely for the familiarity of her own little tower room at Windy Poplars. She thinks about Rebecca Dew, the widows, and all the homely comforts she had perhaps undervalued. Tomgallon House, for all its magnificence, is a very poor place in which to feel lonely.

The chapter captures Anne at an age when she is no longer a child but not yet beyond youthful susceptibility to the atmosphere of romance and terror. She understands perfectly well that Miss Minerva’s histories have colored her mind, yet that knowledge does not free her. Indeed, part of the humor lies in Anne’s clear awareness that she is frightening herself. She almost watches her own imagination at work and still cannot stop it.

Miss Minerva, meanwhile, remains entirely unconscious of the effect she has produced. She regards Aunt Annabella’s room as an honorably suitable chamber and sees no contradiction between hospitality and a detailed rehearsal of former tragedies. That mismatch between intention and effect gives the chapter much of its charm. Miss Minerva means well. Anne is not in danger. Yet the emotional experience feels like a miniature trial.

At some point the situation changes from eerie discomfort to genuine exhaustion. Anne cannot remain fully on guard forever. She settles into the bed, perhaps still suspicious of every sound, but sleep finally comes. Morning brings relief and, with it, perspective. In daylight the room and house lose much of their supernatural force. What seemed nearly haunted by night looks merely old, stately, and overcrowded with memories.

This daylight transformation matters. It shows how powerfully setting, weather, and suggestion shape human feeling. Tomgallon House has not changed in substance, but Anne’s state of mind has changed everything. The chapter therefore works on two levels. It is first of all a delightful piece of atmospheric comedy. The reader smiles at Anne’s inward terror, especially because she knows so well that she is being unreasonable. At the same time it is a subtle study of imagination itself. Anne’s gift for feeling beauty and emotional depth also makes her vulnerable to shadows and stories.

The next morning Anne can laugh at the experience more easily than she could endure it in the moment. She returns from Tomgallon House with a stronger appreciation for the warm untidiness of Windy Poplars and for the ordinary domestic life that keeps fear in proportion. Miss Minerva’s world is grander but less habitable. It belongs to memory, silence, and ceremony rather than to laughter and ease. By staying there overnight Anne learns something important about herself as well. She remains imaginative enough to be haunted by a room, but sensible enough to laugh at her own nerves once morning comes. That balance between susceptibility and humor is one of the things that makes her such an engaging heroine.

## Vocabulary & Nuance

- **susceptibility** — the quality of being easily influenced or affected.
- **oppressive** — causing discomfort through heaviness or pressure.
- **sinister** — suggesting something threatening or evil.
- **magnificence** — great beauty and impressiveness.
- **perspective** — a calmer and more balanced way of seeing things.
- **settle down** — become calmer and more relaxed.
- **keep up** — continue at the same level.
- **give in** — stop resisting.
- **wake up** — stop sleeping and become conscious.
- **laugh off** — treat something as unimportant by joking about it.

## Conversation 1 — Anne Realizes She Must Stay

Miss Minerva: My dear, the storm has become too violent for you to go home.
Anne: Then I suppose I must **settle down** here for the night.
Miss Minerva: Certainly. Aunt Annabella’s room is prepared.
Anne: That sounds more impressive than comforting.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- settle down — accept a place or situation and become calmer.
- go home — return to one’s home.
- keep up — continue without stopping.
- blow up — suddenly intensify or explode, not literal here.
- see through — understand the truth about something.

## Conversation 2 — Anne Battles Her Imagination

Anne: I know perfectly well this room is harmless.
Anne: Still I may **give in** to panic if that curtain moves again.
Anne: No, I must be sensible.
Anne: At least in the morning I shall be able to laugh it all off.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- give in — stop resisting.
- laugh off — treat something by joking about it.
- lie down — put oneself in a resting position.
- wake up — become conscious after sleep.
- keep up — maintain something.
