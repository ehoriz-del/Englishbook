# Chapter 13 — Little Elizabeth Finds Tomorrow

## Story Summary

The violent interruption at the end of the previous chapter leads into one of the emotional climaxes of Anne of Windy Poplars. After the runaway team appears, there is a moment of confusion and danger, and Little Elizabeth is injured. Chapter 13 opens with her recovering in bed, her senses returning only gradually. The room seems to tilt. Voices float in and out. She is uncertain where she is and why. This disoriented beginning places the reader directly inside the child’s experience and heightens the tenderness of what follows.

As Elizabeth becomes more aware, she recognizes two voices. One is Anne’s. The other belongs to the unknown man from Flying Cloud. Their conversation slowly reveals the truth. Anne has discovered that the man is Pierce Grayson, Elizabeth’s father. He had come because of Anne’s letter, not because of accident or curiosity. He had wanted to see his daughter quietly before deciding how to proceed. Fate and Anne’s intervention have brought them together at the very moment when Elizabeth most needs care.

For Anne, the revelation is deeply moving. Her instinct that Elizabeth needed her father has been justified. Pierce, for his part, is filled with emotion and regret. The years of separation now seem unbearable in light of the actual child before him. He has not returned as an indifferent visitor. He has returned as a man suddenly confronted by his own loss. Anne can see this clearly. Montgomery allows the recognition to unfold gently, without melodrama, through fragments of speech, anxious tenderness, and the father’s steady presence at the bedside.

When Elizabeth fully wakes and understands that the kind man is her father, the emotional effect is powerful precisely because it is so simple. She does not meet an abstract family relation. She meets the man who gave her ice cream and looked at her with tenderness. The stranger of Tomorrow and the lost father turn out to be the same person. This joining of fantasy and reality is one of the loveliest touches in the novel. Elizabeth’s imaginative world is not destroyed by truth. Instead truth fulfills it.

Pierce quickly makes his intentions clear. He wants Elizabeth with him. He will not allow her to return to the cold limitations of her former life. He speaks decisively, and his decision has the force of moral restoration. The grandmother and the oppressive household that shaped Elizabeth’s daily fear are no longer going to determine her future. The father who was missing steps into his place at last.

Elizabeth’s questions are childlike but searching. Will he keep her always. Will they live together. Will the grandmother and the Woman come too. Each answer redraws the boundaries of her life. The most important thing she hears is not merely that her father exists but that he chooses her. He wants her with him. This active choosing gives the chapter its healing quality. Elizabeth is not being pitied from a distance. She is being claimed in love.

Anne stands slightly to the side during this reunion, and that is exactly as it should be. She has made the meeting possible, but she does not try to occupy the center. Instead she witnesses the happiness with deep gratitude. In many Anne books, her imagination and sympathy improve the lives of others. Here that pattern reaches one of its most satisfying forms. A letter written in quiet compassion has changed a child’s destiny.

The image of Tomorrow returns in a transformed way. Earlier Elizabeth had thought of Tomorrow as a mysterious region across distance, a place of freedom and delight where wishes might come true. Now she announces that she has found Tomorrow. The phrase is no longer childish fantasy. It names a real condition of hope fulfilled. Her future, once blocked and shadowed, has opened. The man who tells Anne he has found a treasure he did not know he possessed is expressing the same truth from the other side. Father and daughter are both discovering what should have belonged to them all along.

The chapter also closes an important moral pattern. The forces that once seemed fixed are not fixed after all. A severe grandmother, an absent father, old misunderstandings, and a lonely household had shaped Elizabeth’s life, but they are not the final authority. Human tenderness and responsible action can alter the course of events. Anne’s courage in writing the letter is therefore vindicated.

By the end of the chapter, the emotional tone is one of quiet triumph. Nothing noisy or theatrical is needed. A child in a bed, a father beside her, and Anne standing near are enough. When Elizabeth says she has found Tomorrow, the words gather up the symbolic meaning of several chapters. She has found not merely a pleasant outing but a father, a future, and a home in which affection will no longer be forbidden. It is one of the most luminous resolutions in the book and one of the clearest examples of Montgomery’s ability to unite sentiment, imagination, and moral clarity.

## Vocabulary & Nuance

- **disoriented** — confused about where one is or what is happening.
- **vindicated** — shown to be right or justified.
- **restoration** — the act of returning something to its proper condition.
- **luminous** — glowing with emotional brightness or beauty.
- **destiny** — the course a life seems meant to take.
- **wake up** — regain consciousness or stop sleeping.
- **turn out to be** — prove to be after uncertainty.
- **step into** — take on a role or responsibility.
- **take back** — return with one to another place.
- **belong to** — properly be connected with or owned by.

## Conversation 1 — Elizabeth Wakes

Elizabeth: Miss Shirley, who is that man
Anne: He is someone very important to you, dear.
Pierce: I am your father, and I have come to **take you back** with me.
Elizabeth: Then Tomorrow is real after all.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- take back — bring someone back with you.
- wake up — become conscious after sleep.
- turn out to be — prove to be.
- come back — return.
- look after — care for.

## Conversation 2 — Anne and Pierce Speak

Pierce: Your letter changed everything.
Anne: I only hoped you would **come back** and see her for yourself.
Pierce: Now that I have seen her, nothing will keep me away again.
Anne: Then the future has opened at last.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- come back — return.
- see for oneself — discover personally.
- step into — take up a role.
- make up for — compensate for something.
- carry through — complete successfully.
