# Chapter 2 — The Raymond Twins

## Story Summary

Saturday morning was bright and mild when Anne walked out to the Raymond cottage, carrying with her more confidence than caution. The house stood on a pleasant street that gradually wandered into the countryside, and its neat appearance did nothing to support Rebecca Dew's warnings. Mrs. Raymond was already dressed for the train in a striking hat covered with flowers. She looked far more like a woman setting out for an afternoon visit than someone bound for a funeral, but Anne had no time to study the contradiction. Two golden-haired children were sitting on the stairs, watching her with solemn blue eyes.

Gerald and Geraldine were beautiful enough to make suspicion seem almost unkind. Their expressions were so innocent that Anne briefly wondered whether Summerside had exaggerated their reputation. Mrs. Raymond introduced them fondly and reminded them that Miss Shirley had sacrificed her Saturday to stay with them. Both children promised to be good with grave sincerity. Their mother smiled as if the matter were settled.

On the way to the gate, Mrs. Raymond admitted that some people considered the twins spoiled. She herself preferred to think that they had high spirits and a healthy sense of freedom. She did not believe in breaking a child's will through constant punishment. Love, she said, was better than fear. Anne agreed with much of the principle, but Mrs. Raymond's final instructions were less philosophical. The twins must not sail their boats in the bathtub, and they must not go wading in the pond. Their father had died of pneumonia, so she was especially nervous about wet clothes and chills.

The moment Mrs. Raymond disappeared down the road, the angelic atmosphere changed. Gerald and Geraldine did not become monsters all at once. That would have been simpler. Instead they tested Anne in small, exhausting ways, turning an ordinary request into an argument and almost any harmless object into equipment for a new game. Gerald wanted to drag a coyote skin around the house and pretend to be a wolf, while Geraldine instantly supported whatever her brother wanted. When Anne suggested a quiet hour in the garden with stories and games, the twins treated the proposal as something that required negotiation.

Anne was still trying to guide them toward a less destructive form of amusement when the front bell rang. The twins raced down the stairs, sliding along the banister and reaching the door ahead of her. A woman stood outside with the determined expression of someone who had no intention of leaving quickly. Anne recognized Pamela Drake, a well-known canvasser whose visits were feared throughout Summerside. Miss Drake was selling a large national encyclopedia, and she regarded Anne's profession as proof that Anne ought to buy it.

Anne explained that the High School already had an encyclopedia and that she had no desire to spend a large sum on another one. Miss Drake treated every refusal as the beginning of a discussion rather than its conclusion. She settled herself beside Anne on a garden bench, opened her prospectus, and began praising the paper, the illustrations, the printing, and the financial terms. Anne tried to interrupt, but the woman moved smoothly past each objection. When Anne said she would think about it, Miss Drake produced a fountain pen and urged her to sign immediately.

By then Anne's attention was divided between irritation and anxiety. The twins had become far too quiet. She imagined boats in the bathtub, muddy shoes at the pond, or some experiment involving the kitchen stove. She was almost tempted to sign the order simply to get rid of Miss Drake and find out what the children were doing.

Before the pen reached the paper, a scream tore through the garden. Anne looked up and saw a scene so absurd that for a second she could not understand it. Miss Drake's hat, spectacles, and false hair were rising through the air toward an upstairs window. Gerald had lowered fishing hooks from the bathroom and, by astonishing luck, had caught all three items at once. Two golden heads appeared at the window while Miss Drake struggled below in speechless fury.

Anne rushed upstairs, but the twins had already abandoned the fishing rod. By the time she looked out again, Miss Drake had recovered her belongings and was marching toward the gate without the encyclopedia order. It was probably the first time in her career that she had been driven away before completing a sale. Anne knew Gerald's behavior could not be approved, yet she also knew he had rescued her from a remarkably determined saleswoman. Moral certainty was difficult when gratitude kept interfering with it.

She found the twins on the back porch eating apples with expressions of perfect innocence. Before Anne could decide how severe her lecture should be, Gerald announced that Geraldine had swallowed a worm. Geraldine immediately became sick, and the next part of the morning disappeared into practical care. By the time she recovered, lunch was ready and Anne's anger had lost much of its force. She gave Gerald a mild but serious warning about humiliating people for amusement. He admitted it had not been gentlemanly, although his pride in the fishing achievement remained obvious.

Lunch brought an unexpected peace. Mrs. Raymond had prepared excellent food, and hunger kept the twins busy. Afterward Anne involved them in washing and drying the dishes. Geraldine handled the towel with great importance, while Gerald stacked plates carefully in the cupboard. Watching them work, Anne began to understand why their mother found it easy to excuse them. They were quick, charming, and capable when their energy had somewhere useful to go.

For the first time that day, Anne relaxed. She decided that the twins were not impossible at all. What they needed, she thought, was consistent direction and boundaries that did not collapse the moment they objected. The conclusion restored her confidence. She had survived the morning, prevented any real harm, and even discovered a cooperative side in both children. With several hours still ahead, Anne allowed herself to believe that the most difficult part of the day might already be over.

## Vocabulary & Nuance

- **contradiction** — a situation in which two ideas or appearances do not fit together.
- **sincerity** — the quality of being honest and genuine.
- **philosophical** — concerned with general beliefs or principles rather than only practical details.
- **negotiate** — discuss conditions in order to reach an agreement.
- **persistent** — continuing despite resistance or refusal.
- **objection** — a reason for disagreeing with or refusing something.
- **divided attention** — attention shared between two competing concerns.
- **humiliate** — make someone feel ashamed or publicly embarrassed.
- **consistent** — acting according to the same standards over time.
- **boundary** — a limit that defines acceptable behavior.

## Conversation 1 — Dealing with a Pushy Salesperson

Pamela: You are a teacher, so this encyclopedia is practically essential.
Anne: I appreciate the pitch, but I am not interested in buying it.
Pamela: At least hear me out before you rule it out.
Anne: I already have. I do not want to be pressured into a decision.
Pamela: You may regret passing up the introductory price.
Anne: That is a risk I am comfortable taking.

### Phrasal Verbs & Expressions

- **hear someone out** — listen until someone has finished making a case.
- **rule something out** — decide something is not a possibility.
- **pressure someone into** — push someone to do something they do not freely choose.
- **pass up** — decline an opportunity.
- **be comfortable with** — accept a choice or risk without serious concern.

## Conversation 2 — Anne Sets a Boundary

Anne: Gerald, what you did was clever, but clever is not the same as acceptable.
Gerald: She would not leave you alone.
Anne: I know, and I was frustrated too. That still does not give you a free pass to embarrass her.
Gerald: So am I in serious trouble
Anne: You are getting a warning this time, but I expect you to take it seriously.

### Phrasal Verbs & Expressions

- **give someone a free pass** — excuse behavior without consequences.
- **leave someone alone** — stop bothering or pressuring someone.
- **get away with** — avoid consequences for something wrong.
- **take something seriously** — treat something as important.
- **draw a line** — set a clear limit on acceptable behavior.
