# Chapter 12 — Pauline’s One Great Wish

## Story Summary

Late in May, Anne writes to Gilbert in the full excitement of spring. Summerside has changed with the season: flowers reach over fences, trees are in bloom, and even places that seemed ordinary during winter now look renewed. Anne’s delight in the natural world is more than decoration. Spring makes her think about release, possibility, and the return of life. Those ideas become especially meaningful when she tells Gilbert about Pauline Gibson, a woman whose life has been almost completely confined by duty.

Anne first began visiting the Gibsons because Marilla had once known the family when they lived in White Sands. The visits soon became regular because Anne grew fond of Pauline. Pauline is forty-five, the youngest of a large family, and still unmarried. All her brothers and sisters have established homes of their own, but none of them wants responsibility for their elderly mother. As a result, Pauline has become the person who does everything.

Mrs. Adoniram Gibson is eighty and uses a wheelchair. She is not physically independent, but her personality dominates the household. Pauline cooks, cleans, waits on her, manages the house, and answers to every request. Even at night Pauline does not truly rest. She sleeps in the same room as her mother and is repeatedly awakened to adjust pillows, fetch medicine, refill hot-water bottles, investigate noises, or relieve some new discomfort. Mrs. Gibson sleeps during the day, which leaves Pauline exhausted and without a private life.

The control extends far beyond practical caregiving. Pauline cannot buy ordinary clothes without permission. Garments must be approved by her mother and worn far beyond the point at which Pauline would choose to replace them. She has worn the same hat for years. She is rarely able to attend church or join the organizations she would enjoy. Anne sees that Pauline is not poor and does not lack food or shelter; what she lacks is autonomy. She has become accustomed to asking permission for things most adults would consider personal choices.

What makes the situation harder for Anne to watch is Pauline’s temperament. She is not bitter. She remains gentle, patient, and affectionate. She even speaks kindly of her mother and seems to have trained herself not to examine too closely what her life has cost her. Her small dog is one of the few things she has been allowed to keep for herself, and even that affection must be shown carefully because Mrs. Gibson dislikes the animal. Pauline’s goodness has made her dependable, but it has also made her easy to control.

One evening Anne arrives and immediately realizes Pauline has been crying. The reason soon becomes clear. Pauline’s cousin Louisa is celebrating her silver wedding in White Sands. Pauline had been Louisa’s bridesmaid twenty-five years earlier and longs to attend the anniversary. The event is not merely a social outing. It connects Pauline with the young woman she once was, with old friendships, familiar places, and a part of life that has been inaccessible for years.

Mrs. Gibson reacts as though Pauline’s wish is a personal betrayal. She does not directly forbid the trip at first. Instead, she uses guilt. She describes how abandoned and helpless she will be and announces that she will leave the decision to Pauline’s conscience. Anne immediately recognizes the strategy. A direct “no” might be challenged; an appeal to conscience makes Pauline feel selfish for wanting anything at all. Pauline quickly gives up. She says she will not go if her mother does not want her to.

Outside, under the spring moon, Pauline admits to Anne how deeply she wants to attend the celebration. She does not speak angrily. Instead, she tries to put the disappointment away and turn her attention to the beautiful evening. That quiet resignation moves Anne more than open rebellion would have done. Pauline has spent so many years adjusting to disappointment that denying herself has become almost automatic.

Anne decides that this time Pauline will have her day. The decision requires sacrifice on Anne’s part because she had planned to spend the next weekend at Green Gables. But she is willing to give up that visit and stay with Mrs. Gibson so Pauline can go. The important point is that Anne is not trying to rescue Pauline from her entire life. She cannot solve decades of family habits in one visit. What she can do is create one opening.

The chapter therefore explores the difference between duty and self-erasure. Pauline genuinely loves her mother and believes she is needed. Caring for someone can be meaningful, but Mrs. Gibson has turned need into control. She repeatedly suggests that any independent desire in Pauline is evidence of selfishness or disloyalty. Anne sees the unfairness clearly because she is outside the family system. Pauline, who has lived inside it for years, has difficulty imagining that her own wishes might also matter.

Anne’s response is practical as well as emotional. She does not simply tell Pauline to be brave. She begins thinking about how to remove Mrs. Gibson’s objections one by one. Someone must stay with her. Transportation must be arranged. The mother must be persuaded that the trip is respectable. Anne understands that kindness sometimes involves logistics, not just sympathy.

Spring is an ideal setting for this story. All around Pauline, the natural world is opening, blooming, and moving forward. Pauline herself has been held in place. Her wish to see White Sands, old friends, and the gulf is a wish to step briefly back into the wider world. Anne cannot give her permanent freedom, but she can refuse to accept the idea that a good daughter must never have a day of her own.

By the end of the chapter, the trip has not yet happened. What has changed is Anne’s determination. Pauline has almost surrendered her wish, but Anne has not. She has decided that one day of happiness can matter enormously to someone whose life contains very little choice. That conviction sets up the next stage of the story: Anne must now turn compassion into action.

## Vocabulary & Nuance

- **domineering** — trying to control other people in an unpleasant or overbearing way. Mrs. Gibson is domineering toward Pauline.
- **autonomy** — the freedom to make your own decisions. Pauline has very little personal autonomy.
- **resignation** — acceptance of an unpleasant situation because you believe it cannot be changed.
- **self-effacing** — modest to the point of giving too little importance to your own needs or abilities.
- **obligation** — something you feel morally or practically required to do.
- **wear someone down** — gradually weaken a person’s resistance through repeated pressure.
- **give in** — stop resisting and accept what another person wants.
- **defer to** — allow another person’s judgment or wishes to take priority.
- **put something out of your mind** — deliberately stop thinking about something painful or tempting.
- **make room for** — allow space in your life for a need, person, or activity.

## Conversation 1 — When Duty Becomes Too Much

*A modern conversation inspired by Pauline’s situation.*

Sara: My mother needs a lot of help, and I feel guilty whenever I do anything for myself.
Elena: Caring for her matters, but your needs don’t disappear because she depends on you.
Sara: If I take a day off, I feel selfish.
Elena: That sounds as though guilt has **worn you down**.
Sara: Maybe. I keep **giving in** before I even ask what I want.
Elena: Then start small. A few hours away can be reasonable and healthy.
Sara: I suppose responsibility and total self-sacrifice aren’t the same thing.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- wear someone down — gradually reduce someone’s ability to resist.
- give in — stop resisting pressure.
- take time off — spend time away from work or responsibility.
- speak up for yourself — express and defend your own needs.
- set aside — reserve time or something for a particular purpose.

## Conversation 2 — A Practical Kind of Help

*A modern conversation inspired by Anne’s decision.*

Anne: I don’t want to tell Pauline simply to “be brave.” That won’t solve anything.
Rebecca: Then what will?
Anne: I can stay with her mother, arrange the day, and **take away** the practical excuses.
Rebecca: And if Mrs. Gibson still refuses?
Anne: I’ll try to **work around** her objections without humiliating her.
Rebecca: You do realize she may make the entire day difficult for you.
Anne: I do. But sometimes helping means doing the inconvenient part.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- take away — remove something that causes difficulty.
- work around — find a practical way to deal with an obstacle.
- follow through — do what you promised or planned to do.
- step up — accept responsibility when help is needed.
- back down — stop defending a position after pressure or opposition.
