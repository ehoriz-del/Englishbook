# Chapter 15 — Wedding Weekend at Bonnyview

## Story Summary

Anne arrives at Bonnyview on the Friday evening before Sally Nelson’s wedding. The house is a large, rambling summer home on a point of land with the bay on both sides. The setting is beautiful, but there is no quiet dignity inside the house that night. Bonnyview is overflowing with relatives, friends, wedding gifts, luggage, food, children, bridesmaids, ushers, and last-minute arrangements. Anne immediately enjoys the cheerful confusion.

Sally, the bride, is delighted with the excitement of getting married. She proudly shows Anne the sleeping arrangements and talks about her wedding dress, the gifts, and the crowd. Anne will share the north gable room with several other young women because every available space in the house is being used. Even a tent and the hayloft may be needed for guests.

The person everyone seems least pleased to see is Aunt Grace Kennedy, known as Aunt Mouser. Her nickname comes from her habit of “mousing” around in other people’s affairs. She notices everything, asks questions that should not be asked, and nearly always says the one thing most likely to make someone uncomfortable. She is not intentionally evil; in fact, much of what she says is comic. But her complete lack of tact makes her dangerous in a house full of sensitive wedding emotions.

Aunt Mouser quickly demonstrates her gift. She comments on Sally’s choice of husband, worries aloud about disasters that might occur during the ceremony, criticizes clothing, predicts missing gifts, and raises the subject of Sally’s older sister Nora. Nora is now the only unmarried Nelson daughter. Aunt Mouser asks directly about Jim Wilcox, the man Nora has been connected with for years.

Sally explains that Nora and Jim had a serious quarrel in January and that Jim has not been visiting since. Aunt Mouser, who considers frankness a virtue regardless of its effect, has already told Nora that Jim has supposedly been seen with another woman. Nora reacts badly. Anne begins to understand that beneath the noisy happiness of the wedding there is a much quieter story of disappointment.

The dinner on the glassed-in porch is lively despite Aunt Mouser’s remarks. The Nelson family is large, and nearly everyone treats the older woman’s gloomy predictions as part of the entertainment. Dr. Nelson laughs, the younger guests tease one another, and Sally remains absorbed in her own happiness. Nora, however, does not join the general mood.

Anne watches Nora with interest. Nora is older than Anne, reserved, proud, and not conventionally charming in the way Sally is. Yet Anne feels drawn to her. Nora has a strong presence and an emotional intensity that make Anne suspect there is much more beneath the surface than people usually see. During the evening, Nora’s expression seems increasingly dark and withdrawn.

After dinner there is dancing. Eventually Anne grows tired of the noise and slips outside toward the shore. The moonlit bay is calm and beautiful, and there Anne finds Nora alone in the shadow of a rock. The contrast is striking: behind them the house is full of music and celebration, while Nora is sitting by herself, unable to enter into the happiness around her.

When Anne asks whether she may sit down, Nora abruptly begins talking about what it feels like to have no romantic future when everyone around her seems to be marrying. Anne answers honestly rather than giving a polite reassurance. Something in Anne’s manner makes Nora continue. Soon she is telling Anne the whole story of Jim Wilcox.

Nora and Jim have known each other since childhood. They were companions for years, and Nora once used a light in the attic window as a signal when she wanted Jim to come over. Their relationship was so familiar that both may have assumed the other understood what was never clearly said. Yet pride and sharpness gradually entered between them. Nora often teased, rejected, or challenged Jim. He responded with his own pride. Eventually they quarreled badly.

Now Nora believes Jim was simply tired of her and used the quarrel as a way to escape. She is especially hurt by the rumor that he may be interested in someone else. At Sally’s wedding, all the things Nora has tried not to think about have become impossible to avoid. She wants marriage, companionship, a home, and even the ordinary rituals that come with being a bride. She hates admitting it because she sees such longing as humiliating.

Nora also confesses why she refused to be Sally’s bridesmaid. Other people assume she is superstitious, but the truth is more painful. She could not stand beside Sally and listen to wedding vows while believing she would never have the chance to make those promises to Jim. Her sarcasm has been protecting grief.

Anne does not solve the problem in this chapter. What she offers is a safe place for Nora to be honest. Nora cries against Anne’s shoulder and allows herself to say what she has hidden from everyone else. Anne’s ability to receive this confession without judgment changes their relationship. She has wanted to know Nora better, and now she understands that Nora’s difficult surface covers vulnerability rather than indifference.

The night ends with one more burst of social awkwardness. A young man jokes about when Nora will finally marry. Already raw from her confession and the wedding atmosphere, Nora responds by slapping him. Aunt Mouser concludes that Nora is emotionally overwrought—one of the rare moments when her observation is essentially correct.

This chapter explores the difference between public celebration and private pain. Sally’s wedding is joyful, but happiness in one person can sharpen another person’s sense of loss. Nora is not simply jealous of Sally; she is grieving a future she thinks she has lost. Anne’s role is not to lecture her out of jealousy. It is to recognize the grief beneath it.

The chapter also shows why people so often confide in Anne. She is curious about others, but when a real confidence comes, she can listen seriously. She does not rush Nora, mock her contradictions, or reduce the story to gossip. That emotional attentiveness prepares the way for Anne’s impulsive action in the next chapter.

## Vocabulary & Nuance

- **bustling** — full of busy movement and activity.
- **impertinent** — rude because it shows too little respect or asks something too personal.
- **ominous** — suggesting that something bad may happen.
- **reserve** — a tendency not to reveal feelings easily.
- **estrangement** — a state in which people who were once close are no longer friendly or connected.
- **confide in** — tell someone private feelings or information because you trust them.
- **bring up** — introduce a subject in conversation.
- **get under someone’s skin** — irritate or emotionally affect someone strongly.
- **keep something to yourself** — avoid telling other people something.
- **put on a brave face** — appear cheerful or calm when you are actually upset.

## Conversation 1 — Happiness and Jealousy

*A modern conversation inspired by Nora at Sally’s wedding.*

Nora: I hate that I’m jealous. Sally hasn’t done anything wrong.
Anne: Jealousy doesn’t always mean you want to take someone else’s happiness away.
Nora: Then what does it mean?
Anne: Sometimes another person’s happiness **brings up** something you’re grieving.
Nora: I’ve been trying to **keep it to myself**.
Anne: That may be why it feels so explosive now.
Nora: I suppose I’ve been **putting on a brave face** for too long.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- bring up — introduce a topic or cause a feeling to surface.
- keep something to yourself — not share something.
- put on a brave face — appear strong despite distress.
- bottle up — keep feelings inside instead of expressing them.
- open up — begin talking honestly about feelings.

## Conversation 2 — When Pride Creates Distance

*A modern conversation inspired by Nora and Jim.*

Evan: They were close for years. How did they end up so far apart?
Mira: Neither of them wanted to risk being rejected.
Evan: So they pushed each other away first?
Mira: Exactly. Pride can **get in the way** of saying what you actually feel.
Evan: And once the misunderstanding grows, it becomes harder to **clear it up**.
Mira: Especially if both people keep waiting for the other one to make the first move.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- get in the way — prevent progress or interfere.
- clear up — resolve a misunderstanding.
- push someone away — behave in a way that creates emotional distance.
- make the first move — be the first person to take action.
- drift apart — gradually become less close.
