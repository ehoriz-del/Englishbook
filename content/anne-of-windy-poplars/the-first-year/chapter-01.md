# Chapter 1 — A New Home at Windy Poplars

## Story Summary

Anne Shirley begins a new stage of her life in Summerside. She has finished college, become engaged to Gilbert Blythe, and accepted the position of principal at Summerside High School. Gilbert is studying medicine in Kingsport, so their engagement begins with a long separation. Anne therefore writes to him regularly, turning her letters into a place where she can describe the people around her, laugh at small disasters, and admit worries she would rather not show in public.

Her new address immediately delights her: Windy Poplars on Spook’s Lane. The lane has an official name, but almost nobody uses it. Anne likes the unofficial name because it gives the place mystery and personality. Windy Poplars itself soon becomes more than rented accommodation. It gives her a sense of belonging at a time when the rest of Summerside still feels unfamiliar.

The house is shared by two elderly widows, Aunt Kate and Aunt Chatty, and their capable housekeeper, Rebecca Dew. The three women have different temperaments, but all of them make Anne feel welcome. Aunt Kate is sensible and steady. Aunt Chatty is warmer and more talkative. Rebecca Dew is practical, outspoken, and deeply loyal to the household. Anne quickly realizes that Rebecca may complain about people she loves, but she will defend them fiercely if anyone else criticizes them. Dusty Miller, the household cat, completes the little community.

Anne is given a tower room of her own. She loves its windows, its changing light, and the feeling of privacy it provides. After years of shared rooms, school schedules, and college life, the room feels almost luxurious. It also becomes the place where she writes to Gilbert. Those letters matter because Anne is no longer simply a student dreaming about adulthood. She is now responsible for a school, a staff, and many young people. Writing to Gilbert helps her think through that transition.

Her professional life, however, begins less comfortably. Anne is young for a principal, and she knows that some people are ready to judge her before she has taught a single lesson. Katherine Brooke, the vice-principal, is intelligent and experienced but cold toward Anne. Katherine had wanted the principalship herself and appears to resent the younger woman who received it. She is not openly insubordinate, yet her sharp remarks and distant manner make it clear that Anne cannot automatically expect support from her own colleague.

A larger difficulty soon appears. Summerside is strongly influenced by the Pringle family. The Pringles are numerous, proud of their history, and accustomed to having considerable social power. They had preferred another candidate for Anne’s position, and many of them regard her appointment as an intrusion. Anne begins to understand that she is not facing one disappointed parent or one difficult student. She is facing a network of relatives who are used to acting together.

At first Anne is **apprehensive**. She wants to make a good impression, but she also knows that trying too hard to please everyone can weaken a leader. She cannot simply change school standards every time an influential family objects. At the same time, she does not want to arrive in Summerside behaving as though she already understands a community she has only just entered. She must learn when to be flexible and when to **hold her ground**.

This balance becomes one of the central challenges of her new life. Anne is naturally warm and imaginative, but warmth alone will not establish her authority. She must show that kindness and firmness can exist together. Her years at Avonlea school helped her learn this lesson, but Summerside is larger, more formal, and more political. Here, a careless decision can spread through the town as gossip before Anne even hears about it herself.

Fortunately, Windy Poplars gives her a safe place to return to. At supper she can listen to Rebecca Dew’s opinions, laugh with the widows, and temporarily forget the expectations surrounding the principal of Summerside High. The contrast between the welcoming house and the uncertain public world outside is important. Anne is **settling into** one community while still trying to **establish herself** in another.

Anne also begins to understand how much her new title changes the way people see her. At Redmond she was one student among many; in Summerside, ordinary actions can be interpreted as statements from “the principal.” A friendly remark to a pupil, a decision about discipline, or an invitation accepted or declined may attract attention. She has to become more conscious of boundaries without losing the spontaneity that makes her herself. This is an unfamiliar kind of adulthood: not simply having freedom, but being aware that other people may use your choices as evidence of your judgment.

She also takes comfort in her future with Gilbert. Their life together is still several years away, but the idea of it gives Anne perspective. A difficult day at school is temporary. A cold look from a Pringle does not define her worth. She can describe it all to Gilbert, find humor in it, and begin again the next morning.

By the end of this opening chapter, Anne has not solved her problems. In fact, she has only begun to see how complicated Summerside may be. Yet she has found a home, formed the beginnings of important relationships, and decided not to be intimidated by the social forces around her. Her task is no longer simply to teach lessons. She must learn how to lead, how to read a community, and how to remain herself while adapting to a new role. Windy Poplars gives her the emotional security to try.

## Vocabulary & Nuance

- **apprehensive** — worried about something that may happen. She felt apprehensive before meeting the school board.
- **formidable** — difficult to deal with because of strength, influence, or ability. The Pringle family seemed like a formidable group.
- **establish yourself** — gain a respected or recognized position in a new place or role. Anne needs time to establish herself as principal.
- **tactful** — careful not to upset or embarrass other people. A tactful answer can prevent unnecessary conflict.
- **settle into** — gradually become comfortable in a new place or routine. Anne quickly settles into life at Windy Poplars.
- **win someone over** — persuade someone to like or support you. She hopes to win people over through fairness rather than flattery.
- **hold your ground** — refuse to change your position when you believe it is right. A leader sometimes has to hold her ground.
- **live up to** — be as good as people expect. Anne wants to live up to the responsibility of her new position.

## Conversation 1 — Starting a New Role

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Rebecca: You looked tired when you came in. Rough day?
Anne: A little. I’m still trying to **settle into** the job.
Rebecca: Are the teachers giving you trouble?
Anne: Not exactly, but I can tell some people are **sizing me up**.
Rebecca: That happens when you’re new.
Anne: I know. I don’t need everyone to like me immediately, but I do need them to trust me.
Rebecca: Then don’t try too hard to impress them.
Anne: Exactly. I’d rather **win them over** slowly by being consistent.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- settle into — become comfortable in a new situation over time. It took me a few weeks to settle into the new office.
- size someone up — form an opinion about someone by observing them. The interviewers were clearly sizing me up.
- win someone over — gradually gain someone’s support or approval. Her calm explanation won the committee over.
- get off to a good start — begin successfully. Clear communication helped the team get off to a good start.

## Conversation 2 — Kindness and Authority

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Anne: I want to be fair, but I don’t want people to mistake kindness for weakness.
Gilbert: Those two things don’t have to be opposites.
Anne: True. Still, I may have to **hold my ground** with some parents.
Gilbert: Then explain your reasons and stay calm.
Anne: That sounds simple when you say it.
Gilbert: You don’t have to **back down** just because someone is influential.
Anne: And I don’t have to turn every disagreement into a battle either.
Gilbert: Exactly. Choose what matters and let the rest go.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- hold your ground — keep your position despite pressure. She held her ground during the difficult meeting.
- back down — stop defending your position because of pressure. He refused to back down without evidence.
- pick your battles — decide which problems are important enough to challenge. Good managers learn to pick their battles.
- let something go — stop worrying or arguing about something. I decided to let the small mistake go.
