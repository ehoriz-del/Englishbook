# Chapter 17 — The First Year Ends

## Story Summary

The final chapter of Anne’s first year in Summerside is told through another letter to Gilbert. School has closed, and Anne’s thoughts immediately fly toward Green Gables. After months of professional responsibility, difficult relationships, and constant involvement in other people’s lives, she is ready for two months of familiar woods, fields, brooks, and quiet places in Avonlea. Her excitement is intense, but the chapter is not simply about leaving. It is about recognizing how much has changed since she arrived in Summerside.

The clearest symbol of that change is Jen Pringle. At the beginning of the year, Jen was one of the strongest signs of Pringle hostility. She challenged Anne, represented family resistance, and helped make Anne’s first term extremely uncomfortable. Now Jen brings Anne a bouquet of lilies of the valley and sincerely wishes her a happy vacation. She is even planning to visit Anne at Green Gables for a weekend.

Anne can hardly believe the transformation. The gesture is not merely polite. It shows that the old conflict has truly ended. The Pringles no longer treat Anne as an outsider who must be defeated. Anne has also changed her view of them. She no longer reduces them to the family name. Jen is a student she understands, not simply “a Pringle.”

The farewell to Little Elizabeth is much more painful. Anne had hoped to take Elizabeth to Green Gables for a visit, but Mrs. Campbell decides it would not be advisable. Anne is disappointed, yet she is relieved that she had not told Elizabeth about the possibility. At least the child is spared the pain of having a promised happiness taken away.

Even without knowing about the cancelled possibility, Elizabeth is heartbroken that Anne is leaving for the summer. She says she may become “Lizzie” while Anne is away—a way of expressing that the brighter, more imaginative part of herself feels connected to Anne’s presence. Anne refuses to accept this gloomy version of Elizabeth. She reminds the child that they will have more adventures when she returns and promises to write every week.

The promise of letters is especially important to Elizabeth because she has never received one before. Anne turns separation into anticipation. Instead of presenting the summer as a blank space, she creates a thread that will connect them. Elizabeth says she will write back if she is allowed to have a stamp. If she cannot, Anne is to understand that she is thinking of her anyway.

Elizabeth has even named a chipmunk after Anne—or at least “Shirley,” because she worries that using Anne’s full name might be disrespectful. The detail is funny and tender. It shows how thoroughly Anne has entered the child’s imaginative life. Even in Anne’s absence, Elizabeth will create reminders of her.

Anne’s feelings about leaving are therefore mixed. Green Gables calls to her powerfully, but Summerside is no longer simply the place where she works. She now has people here whom she will miss. This is one of the emotional costs of belonging: once a place becomes home-like, departure cannot be completely simple.

Another unresolved relationship is Katherine Brooke, Anne’s fellow teacher. Katherine remains distant and unfriendly. When Anne asks where she will spend the summer, Katherine answers sharply that she will stay in Summerside. Anne feels an uncomfortable pull of conscience. She considers inviting Katherine to Green Gables but cannot bring herself to do it. She suspects Katherine would refuse, and she also honestly believes Katherine’s presence would spoil the holiday.

Anne’s reaction is important because it is imperfect. She does not always turn every lonely person into a friend. She feels guilty about Katherine, but she also recognizes her own limits. After a year of giving emotional attention to students, households, and troubled acquaintances, she wants Green Gables to remain a place of rest.

Windy Poplars continues producing comic domestic incidents even as Anne prepares to leave. Dusty Miller, the household cat, brings a live snake into the kitchen, horrifying Rebecca Dew. Rebecca is already irritated because she spends so much time removing beetles from the roses. Her gloomy prediction that insects will eventually consume the world provides the kind of ordinary humor Anne has come to love in the house.

News from Bonnyview also continues. Nora and Jim plan to marry quietly in September, deliberately avoiding the large spectacle that Sally’s wedding became. Nora has no intention of giving Aunt Mouser another opportunity to supervise or predict disaster. Anne, however, will be present in an unofficial role. Nora believes Jim would have sold his store and gone west if Anne had not placed the lamp in the attic window.

Anne reflects on the number of romances she is credited with helping along. She is amused, but after everything that has happened she is less likely to think of herself as a brilliant arranger of other people’s happiness. In Nora and Jim’s case, the lamp mattered, but their real difficulty was misunderstanding. Anne recognizes how much trouble people create when they assume they know what another person means instead of speaking honestly.

That observation turns naturally toward her own history with Gilbert. Anne and Gilbert also spent years misunderstanding feelings that were obvious in retrospect. Now their relationship gives Anne a sense of security and expectation. She can look back on old confusion with tenderness rather than pain.

The end of the first year therefore closes several circles. The Pringles have become friends. The Taylor household survived its crisis. Esme and Dr. Carter are moving toward marriage. Pauline has received one treasured day. Nora and Jim have finally understood each other. Little Elizabeth has found in Anne an adult who protects her imagination. Not every problem is solved, but Anne has become woven into the community.

At the same time, the chapter resists the idea that Anne alone has transformed everyone. Summerside has changed Anne too. She has learned more about boundaries, pride, loneliness, social pressure, caregiving, and the limits of good intentions. She has also learned that her greatest influence often comes not from dramatic intervention but from listening, noticing, and making people feel seen.

When Anne prepares to return to Green Gables, she is leaving with more than relief. She carries new attachments and responsibilities with her. Her first year began with opposition and uncertainty; it ends with flowers, letters, promises, and plans to return. That movement from outsider to trusted friend is the real achievement of the year.

The season closes, but Anne’s connection to Summerside does not. Like the letters she promises Elizabeth, the relationships will continue across distance. The first year has taught Anne that belonging does not replace old homes with new ones. The heart can enlarge enough to hold both.

## Vocabulary & Nuance

- **poignant** — producing a strong feeling of sadness mixed with tenderness.
- **reconciliation** — the restoration of a friendly relationship after conflict.
- **anticipation** — excited or anxious expectation about something in the future.
- **reciprocate** — respond to an action or feeling with a similar action or feeling.
- **unresolved** — not yet settled or completed.
- **keep in touch** — continue communicating despite distance.
- **look forward to** — feel pleased and excited about something in the future.
- **close out** — bring a period, project, or stage to an end.
- **make peace with** — accept a difficult reality or reach a calmer relationship with it.
- **carry forward** — continue something into a new period or stage.

## Conversation 1 — Staying Connected During Separation

*A modern conversation inspired by Anne and Elizabeth’s summer farewell.*

Elizabeth: Two months feels terribly long.
Anne: It will feel shorter if we **keep in touch**.
Elizabeth: What if I’m not allowed to send many letters?
Anne: Then write when you can. Friendship isn’t measured by postage.
Elizabeth: And you’ll really write every week?
Anne: I will. We can **look forward to** telling each other everything when I come back.
Elizabeth: That makes leaving feel less final.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- keep in touch — continue communicating.
- look forward to — anticipate with pleasure.
- come back — return.
- get through — manage a difficult period until it ends.
- stay connected — maintain a relationship across distance.

## Conversation 2 — Looking Back on a Difficult Year

*A modern conversation inspired by Anne’s reflections.*

Gilbert: Your first term sounded miserable. Do you regret taking the job?
Anne: Not at all. I wouldn’t want to repeat those first months, but I learned from them.
Gilbert: And now Jen Pringle is bringing you flowers.
Anne: That may be the strangest part.
Gilbert: So how would you **sum up** the year?
Anne: I arrived wanting to prove myself. I’m leaving more interested in understanding people.
Gilbert: That sounds like progress.
Anne: It feels like it.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- sum up — state the main meaning of something briefly.
- look back on — remember and reflect on the past.
- get through — successfully survive a difficult period.
- grow into — gradually become more capable or mature in a role.
- carry forward — take lessons or values into the next stage.
