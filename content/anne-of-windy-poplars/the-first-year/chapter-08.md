# Chapter 8 — A Truce with the Pringles

## Story Summary

The atmosphere in Summerside changes almost overnight after the unexpected visit from Miss Sarah and Miss Ellen Pringle. Anne writes to Gilbert from her tower room with a sense of relief that would have seemed impossible only a short time earlier. The same family that once tried to push her out of the school is now welcoming her into its social circle.

The change is so complete that Anne finds it almost comic. She is invited to Pringle parties, included in gatherings, and treated as if she has always belonged. Even the minister’s wife asks her to sing in the church choir, and Anne strongly suspects that the Pringles have encouraged the invitation. The family has not merely stopped opposing her; it has decided to approve of her enthusiastically.

Anne notices small signs of this new acceptance. Some of the younger Pringle women appear to be copying the way she arranges her hair. Jen Pringle, once the center of the student rebellion, can now be charming when she chooses to be. Anne had always believed she might like the Pringles if they would stop treating her as an enemy, and now she is surprised to discover that she was right. Once the conflict disappears, individuals begin to emerge from what had previously seemed like one united hostile clan.

This change teaches Anne something important about social groups. During the feud, every Pringle action looked like part of a campaign. Now that she is no longer under attack, she can see differences among them. Some are proud, some amusing, some warm, some difficult. The family still has enormous influence, but it no longer feels like a single threatening force.

Anne then decides to test whether the new peace has reached the household next door. She goes to The Evergreens and asks whether Little Elizabeth may accompany her on a walk. Anne expects a refusal. Mrs. Campbell is a Pringle, and Martha Monkman—the Woman—is hardly known for encouraging pleasure. To Anne’s surprise, permission is granted. She wonders whether Miss Sarah has quietly influenced that decision too.

Elizabeth comes down the stairs almost unable to contain her excitement. She and Anne set off along the road the child thinks of as leading toward the end of the world. Away from the dark house and constant supervision, Elizabeth changes. She becomes lively, imaginative, and eager. Anne holds her hand as they walk toward the harbor under a dramatic sunset.

The landscape gives Elizabeth’s imagination room to breathe. She wonders whether they could run fast enough to enter the sunset itself. Anne responds in the same spirit rather than correcting her. Together they talk about the distant horizon, mysterious places, and the possibility of happiness beyond what can presently be seen.

Elizabeth tells Anne about Flying Cloud, an island she can see from the upper windows of her home. It belongs to a man from Boston who has a summer house there, but in Elizabeth’s private world the island belongs to imagination. Its name seems to her like something that could exist in Tomorrow. Anne understands immediately why the place fascinates her. It represents distance, mystery, and a life beyond the rules of The Evergreens.

At the end of the walk, Anne kisses Elizabeth before the child returns home. The gesture is simple and spontaneous. For Anne, affection is natural. For Elizabeth, it is extraordinary.

The next evening Elizabeth arrives for the milk in tears. Martha Monkman has made her wash Anne’s kiss off her face. Elizabeth had resisted washing because she wanted to keep the physical sign of affection as long as possible. The story is both funny and painful. Anne has to keep herself from laughing at the literal idea that a kiss could be washed away, but she immediately understands that the child’s feelings are serious.

Anne reassures her. Elizabeth must wash her face, of course, but Anne can simply give her another kiss every evening. Affection does not disappear because soap removes its imaginary mark. The promise gives Elizabeth something dependable—an emotional routine that belongs to her relationship with Anne.

Then Elizabeth says something that disturbs Anne more deeply: she believes Anne is the only person in the world who loves her. Anne tries to defend Mrs. Campbell by saying that the grandmother surely loves her, but Elizabeth rejects the idea. She experiences her grandmother and Martha not as loving protectors but as authorities who are constantly correcting and restricting her.

Anne does not fully argue. She knows that the adults provide Elizabeth with food, clothing, shelter, and order, but she also knows the child is emotionally starved. Their sense of duty has not created warmth. They may believe they are raising her properly, yet Elizabeth does not feel cherished.

Elizabeth boldly calls the two women tyrants and announces that when Tomorrow arrives she will escape them. Anne suspects the child partly enjoys shocking her, but the statement still reveals genuine frustration. Tomorrow remains Elizabeth’s imagined country of freedom: a place where love is open, choices are possible, and every feeling is not examined for disobedience.

For Anne, the walk also marks a personal turning point. Looking over Summerside from her tower, she no longer sees a hostile town. Its roofs and lights now seem friendly. She will not have to return to Green Gables at Christmas carrying the shame of professional defeat. The Pringle crisis, which once seemed capable of destroying her career, has passed.

This new security allows her attention to move outward. She is no longer using all her emotional energy to defend herself. She can invest more of it in people like Elizabeth. That shift is significant. When Anne feels threatened, she naturally focuses on survival; when she feels secure, her instinct is to build relationships.

The chapter ends with domestic humor—food, Rebecca Dew, and Anne’s playful comments to Gilbert—but beneath the humor is a deep sense of relief. Anne has been accepted by the most powerful family in town and has gained a precious friendship with a child who desperately needs one.

The “truce” therefore operates on more than one level. It is a social truce between Anne and the Pringles, but it also creates space for Anne to bring peace and affection into Elizabeth’s life. Anne cannot transform The Evergreens, but she can offer the child walks, imagination, conversation, and an evening kiss. For Elizabeth, those small things are not small at all.

## Vocabulary & Nuance

- **reconciliation** — the restoration of a friendly relationship after conflict. Anne’s relationship with the Pringles moves toward reconciliation.
- **wholeheartedly** — with complete sincerity and enthusiasm. The Pringles now seem to accept Anne wholeheartedly.
- **affectionate** — showing warmth and love. Anne’s affectionate treatment is new and precious to Elizabeth.
- **restrictive** — limiting freedom or choice. Elizabeth lives in a highly restrictive household.
- **cherish** — love and care for someone deeply. Elizabeth wants to feel cherished, not merely supervised.
- **win someone over** — gradually gain someone’s approval or support. Anne has unexpectedly won the Pringles over.
- **open up** — become more relaxed and willing to express feelings. Elizabeth opens up when she is alone with Anne.
- **break free from** — escape a limiting situation or control. Elizabeth dreams of breaking free from the rules of The Evergreens.
- **reach out to** — make an effort to connect with or help someone. Anne reaches out to Elizabeth.
- **settle into** — gradually become comfortable in a new situation. Anne is finally settling into life in Summerside.

## Conversation 1 — When Conflict Finally Ends

*A modern conversation inspired by Anne’s changed relationship with the Pringles.*

Gilbert: You sound almost fond of the Pringles now.
Anne: I am beginning to be. It’s amazing what you notice once you stop expecting an attack.
Gilbert: So they’ve really **come around**?
Anne: Completely. I’m invited everywhere.
Gilbert: And Jen?
Anne: Even Jen can be delightful when she wants to be.
Gilbert: You always said you might like them if they gave you a chance.
Anne: Apparently they had to stop fighting me before either side could **settle into** being human.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- come around — change your opinion and become more supportive. Her parents eventually came around to the idea.
- win someone over — gain someone’s approval gradually. His calm explanation won the committee over.
- settle into — become comfortable in a new role or place. It took her a month to settle into the new job.
- let your guard down — become less defensive because you feel safer. Once the conflict ended, she could let her guard down.
- start over — begin again in a better way. After the argument, they decided to start over.

## Conversation 2 — Affection That Can’t Be Washed Away

*A modern conversation inspired by Anne and Elizabeth.*

Elizabeth: They made me wash my face.
Anne: Faces do need washing from time to time.
Elizabeth: But your kiss was there.
Anne: Then I’ll give you another one.
Elizabeth: Every day?
Anne: Every day I see you.
Elizabeth: What if they try to take it away again?
Anne: They can wash your cheek, but they can’t wash away what the kiss means.
Elizabeth: I wish everyone understood that.
Anne: Sometimes people care for children but forget to **show it** in a way the child can feel.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- show it — make a feeling visible through words or actions. If you appreciate someone, show it.
- reach out to — make a caring effort to connect. A teacher reached out to the lonely student.
- open up — begin to share feelings more freely. She opened up once she felt safe.
- break free from — escape control or a limiting pattern. He wanted to break free from constant criticism.
- hold back — stop yourself from showing or saying something. She stopped holding back and admitted how lonely she felt.
