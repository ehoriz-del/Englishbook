# Chapter 13 — A Day of Freedom

## Story Summary

Anne’s determination to give Pauline one free day now turns into a difficult negotiation with Mrs. Gibson. The old woman has spent years controlling Pauline through complaints, warnings, guilt, and the constant suggestion that something terrible will happen if Pauline leaves her. Anne knows that a direct argument about fairness will probably fail. Mrs. Gibson does not think of herself as a tyrant. She thinks of herself as an invalid whose needs naturally come first.

Anne therefore changes tactics. She promises to stay with Mrs. Gibson for the entire day of the silver-wedding celebration and to make sure she lacks nothing. Mrs. Gibson responds with familiar self-pity, implying that Pauline wants to abandon her and that nobody understands how much she suffers. Anne remains polite even when her patience is nearly exhausted. Then she discovers the argument that finally works: public opinion.

Anne suggests that people in Summerside might criticize Mrs. Gibson if Pauline is not allowed to attend her cousin’s anniversary. This strikes exactly the right nerve. Mrs. Gibson may be willing to make Pauline feel guilty, but she does not want the town describing her as a selfish old woman. She begins insisting that she is not stopping Pauline at all. In her version of events, Pauline is perfectly free to go. After more complaints and warnings, she gives a grudging consent.

Pauline is overwhelmed with gratitude. She tells Anne that the day means more than a wedding celebration. She wants to see Louisa, who has been part of her past, but she also wants to visit the old Luckley place, where she spent happy summers with her closest girlhood friend. Most of all, she longs to see the gulf again. She has lived near the harbor for years, yet she has not seen the open gulf in fifteen years. The simple fact reveals how narrow her life has become.

A new problem immediately appears: clothing. Mrs. Gibson refuses to let Pauline buy a new dress. Her old black taffeta, she says, was expensive and is still perfectly suitable. Pauline decides she will simply be happy inside and ignore what she is wearing. Anne, however, thinks of another solution. She owns a silver-gray poplin dress that will fit Pauline, and she offers to lend it to her.

Pauline is thrilled and terrified. She knows her mother would object. Anne and Pauline therefore arrange the matter quietly. The elegant gray dress can be worn beneath or concealed by the old black outer dress while Pauline leaves the house, then revealed when she reaches the celebration. The little plan has a comic quality, but it also shows how extreme the household has become: a forty-five-year-old woman feels like a rebellious teenager because she wants to wear a becoming dress for one day.

On the morning of the trip, Pauline is excited enough to seem transformed even before she changes clothes. Anne comes to help her prepare. Pauline worries that her mother may still change her mind. She has already arranged food, household tasks, and instructions so carefully that there is almost nothing left for Mrs. Gibson to complain about. Anne helps with Pauline’s hair and the gray dress, which suits her beautifully. Pauline barely recognizes the woman in the mirror.

Small details make the preparation feel almost ceremonial. Pauline has a new pair of shoes. Anne gives her a touch of apple-blossom perfume. Pauline is delighted by the scent because her mother considers perfume frivolous. Yet the joy is mixed with anxiety. Every pleasure must pass through the filter of what Mrs. Gibson might say.

Before leaving, Pauline must endure an inspection. Mrs. Gibson criticizes her color, her scent, her shoes, her manners, her posture, and almost anything else she can think of. She issues endless instructions about sitting properly, avoiding drafts, talking too much, returning bottles, and not accepting unwanted kittens. Pauline responds obediently, but when her mother brings up an absurd incident from Pauline’s youth as though it might happen again, Pauline finally protests. It is a tiny moment of resistance, yet it matters.

At last Pauline leaves for the train. Anne feels enormous relief, but her own work is just beginning. She has promised to care for Mrs. Gibson for the day. Anne soon discovers the full weight of Pauline’s daily routine. Mrs. Gibson wants objects moved by inches, blinds adjusted, food prepared exactly right, tea made to her standard, cushions shifted, and countless small discomforts corrected. Every task is accompanied by criticism or a gloomy prediction.

Anne remains remarkably patient. She even persuades Mrs. Gibson to sit on the porch, something the older woman claims she has not done in years. The fresh air and sunshine improve her mood temporarily. Anne prepares a good meal, listens to stories about long-dead relatives, and tries to keep conversation moving without becoming trapped in endless complaints. Mrs. Gibson begins to enjoy herself almost in spite of her determination not to.

The afternoon is harder. After a nap, Mrs. Gibson becomes irritable again. Nothing is at the right temperature or height. She complains of pain in multiple places and predicts illness or death. As evening approaches and Pauline does not immediately return, Mrs. Gibson’s anxiety increases. Anne keeps reassuring her, although she is tired herself.

By spending the day in Pauline’s place, Anne learns something sympathy alone could not teach her. Pauline’s burden is not one dramatic act of sacrifice. It is the accumulation of hundreds of small demands, repeated every day, with almost no private space. That experience deepens Anne’s respect for Pauline’s patience.

The title “A Day of Freedom” applies primarily to Pauline, but the chapter also explores what freedom costs. Pauline’s happy day becomes possible because Anne accepts inconvenience, Mrs. Gibson reluctantly releases control, and Pauline takes the frightening step of choosing joy without full approval. Freedom here is not grand rebellion. It is one train ride, one beautiful dress, one day near old friends and the sea. Yet because Pauline’s life has been so restricted, that small freedom becomes precious.

## Vocabulary & Nuance

- **grudging** — given unwillingly and without enthusiasm. Mrs. Gibson gives grudging permission.
- **adamant** — completely unwilling to change a decision or opinion.
- **meticulous** — extremely careful about small details.
- **transformed** — changed greatly in appearance, feeling, or character.
- **tenterhooks** — a state of nervous suspense or anxiety.
- **leverage** — an advantage that can be used to influence a situation. Anne uses Mrs. Gibson’s fear of gossip as leverage.
- **see someone through** — support someone until a difficult situation is finished.
- **look after** — take care of a person, animal, or place.
- **get away with** — succeed in doing something without suffering the expected consequence.
- **come around** — gradually change your opinion and become more willing to agree.

## Conversation 1 — Negotiating With a Difficult Person

*A modern conversation inspired by Anne’s strategy.*

Nina: I’ve explained why the schedule needs to change, but my manager refuses every suggestion.
Theo: Have you tried focusing on what matters to him?
Nina: You mean instead of repeating what matters to me?
Theo: Right. Sometimes you need to find the point that gives you **leverage**.
Nina: I don’t want to manipulate him.
Theo: There’s a difference between manipulation and framing a reasonable request in terms he understands.
Nina: So the goal is to help him **come around**, not force him.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- come around — gradually become willing to agree.
- give in — stop resisting.
- push back — resist or challenge a request or idea.
- get through to — make someone understand what you are saying.
- follow through on — complete what you promised to do.

## Conversation 2 — One Day Away

*A modern conversation inspired by Pauline leaving for White Sands.*

Pauline: I keep thinking something will go wrong the moment I leave.
Anne: You’ve prepared everything you can.
Pauline: I’m still **on tenterhooks**.
Anne: That makes sense. You aren’t used to putting your own plans first.
Pauline: What if Mother decides I’ve been selfish?
Anne: One day away does not erase years of care. Go and enjoy it.
Pauline: I’ll try.
Anne: Don’t just try. Let yourself have the day.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- put something first — treat something as the highest priority.
- go wrong — develop a problem or fail.
- look after — take care of.
- head off — leave for a destination.
- let yourself — allow yourself to experience or do something without guilt.
