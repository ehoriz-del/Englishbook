# Chapter 5 — The School Play and a New Ally

## Story Summary

Anne’s struggle with the Pringles moves from the classroom to the stage when she organizes a dramatic club at Summerside High. Her goal is practical as well as educational: the students can enjoy performing, and the school can raise money for improvements. Anne believes that a play may also create a sense of shared purpose among students who are usually divided by social groups.

Because she wants the project to involve the staff, Anne asks Katherine Brooke to help. The decision is generous, but cooperation is not easy. Katherine is competent and has strong opinions about everything. She insists that Jen Pringle should play Mary, Queen of Scots, the most important role. Jen has confidence, presence, and a natural ability to act. Anne cannot deny that she is talented, even though she does not fully trust her.

Another student, Sophy Sinclair, watches from the edge of the project. Sophy is not a Pringle and does not have the advantages that Jen takes for granted. She is quiet, serious, and deeply interested in drama, but financial limitations have kept her from joining the club. When Anne finds her upset, Sophy admits how much she wanted the role of Queen Mary. Her disappointment is not simple jealousy. Acting means something to her; it gives her a way to imagine a larger future.

Anne sees possibility in Sophy that others have overlooked. She also notices something unsettling in Jen’s behavior. Jen appears unusually cooperative during rehearsals, but from time to time Anne catches a look that suggests she is planning something. Anne has no proof, so she cannot accuse her. Instead, she prepares quietly.

Without turning the situation into a public contest, Anne begins helping Sophy learn the part as an **understudy**. This is a practical decision. If Jen becomes ill or cannot perform, the play needs someone ready to **step in**. Yet Anne also knows she is giving Sophy an opportunity that the girl would never have received under the original arrangement. Sophy works intensely. She studies the role, practices expression, and gradually develops confidence.

The preparations become more demanding as the performance approaches. Anne has to coordinate students, costumes, rehearsals, and school responsibilities. Katherine’s criticism does not make the work easier. Still, Anne remains focused. One of her developing strengths as a leader is the ability to plan for problems without constantly announcing that she expects them.

Her caution proves justified. At the crucial moment, Jen is suddenly unavailable to perform. Whether the timing is meant to damage Anne and the production or simply reflects Jen’s unpredictable nature, the effect is the same: the play could collapse. The audience is waiting, the students are nervous, and there is no time for a long argument.

This is exactly the situation Anne has prepared for. Sophy steps into the role.

At first, the change is risky. Sophy has never had Jen’s social confidence or experience. But once she is onstage, something changes. The girl who seemed ordinary in daily school life becomes vivid and commanding in performance. Her love for the character gives her courage. Instead of merely remembering lines, she seems to understand the emotional shape of the role. The audience responds enthusiastically.

For Anne, the success is satisfying for several reasons. The immediate crisis has been avoided. The school is not embarrassed. The play succeeds. More importantly, Sophy has discovered an ability that other people failed to notice. She has been given a chance and has **come through** when it matters.

Anne does not need to humiliate Jen publicly. The success itself is enough. If Jen expected the performance to fail without her, Sophy’s triumph is a much stronger answer than an angry speech. Anne has, in Rebecca Dew’s view, managed to get the better of the situation without becoming spiteful.

The episode also complicates Anne’s relationship with Katherine. Katherine had confidently declared that Jen was the only student with the necessary personality for the role. Sophy’s performance proves that this judgment was too narrow. Anne does not use the moment to embarrass Katherine. Instead, the event quietly demonstrates that talent can be hidden by lack of money, opportunity, or confidence.

After the excitement of the play, Anne later visits the old graveyard and meets Valentine Courtaloe, Summerside’s dressmaker and an enthusiastic keeper of local stories. Miss Valentine knows family histories, scandals, romances, and old disagreements connected with many of the people buried there. A walk that Anne expected to take alone becomes an informal tour of Summerside’s past.

Miss Valentine is not part of the Pringle clan, and her stories give Anne a different perspective on the town’s most powerful family. The Pringles like to present their history as dignified and impressive. Miss Valentine remembers a more human version, full of odd personalities, mistakes, rivalries, and secrets. Anne listens with interest but also with some caution. Gossip can reveal useful context, yet it can also be unfair to people who are not present to defend themselves.

This encounter makes Miss Valentine a kind of new ally—not necessarily because she plans to fight Anne’s battles, but because she gives Anne access to local memory outside the Pringle version of events. Knowledge is beginning to shift the balance of power. Anne is learning that the Pringles’ authority depends partly on the story the town tells about them. If there are other stories, the family may be less invulnerable than it appears.

The chapter therefore links two forms of hidden potential. Sophy has ability that remains invisible until someone gives her a chance. Summerside has stories that remain hidden until someone like Miss Valentine tells them. Anne benefits from both because she pays attention to people who are easy to overlook.

The school play also shows how much Anne’s leadership has developed. She does not simply react when trouble occurs. She anticipates risk, prepares an alternative, and then allows another person to succeed. This is more sophisticated than solving every problem personally. Good leadership sometimes means creating the conditions in which someone else can **pull something off**.

By the end of the chapter, Anne still has not defeated the Pringle opposition, but she has gained confidence and information. Jen’s attempt to control the situation has failed. Sophy has discovered a new sense of possibility. Miss Valentine has opened a door into Summerside’s complicated history. Anne is no longer merely enduring the town’s politics. She is beginning to understand how to move within them.

## Vocabulary & Nuance

- **understudy** — a performer who learns another actor’s role and can replace that person if necessary. Sophy prepares as an understudy.
- **sabotage** — deliberately damage or prevent the success of a plan. Anne worries that someone may try to sabotage the play.
- **resourceful** — good at finding effective solutions to unexpected problems. Anne is resourceful when the cast suddenly changes.
- **overlook** — fail to notice or recognize something valuable. Sophy’s talent has been overlooked.
- **step in** — take someone’s place or become involved when help is needed. Sophy is ready to step in.
- **pull something off** — succeed at something difficult or unlikely. The students pull off a successful performance.
- **come through** — succeed or provide what is needed in a difficult moment. Sophy comes through under pressure.
- **get the better of** — gain an advantage over someone or a difficult situation. Anne gets the better of the attempted disruption without open revenge.

## Conversation 1 — Preparing for a Problem

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Anne: I don’t know that Jen will cause trouble, but I don’t want the whole play depending on one person.
Sophy: Are you asking me to learn her part?
Anne: Yes—as an **understudy**. You may never need to perform it.
Sophy: I’d still like to try.
Anne: Good. If something goes wrong, you’ll be ready to **step in**.
Sophy: I’m nervous.
Anne: Nervous is fine. Unprepared is the real problem.
Sophy: Then I’ll be prepared.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- step in — take over a role or become involved when help is needed. Maya stepped in when the presenter became ill.
- be ready to — be prepared and willing to do something. Be ready to explain your decision.
- count on — depend on someone or something. We need a backup we can count on.
- have a backup plan — prepare an alternative in case the first plan fails. Always have a backup plan for outdoor events.

## Conversation 2 — Recognizing Hidden Talent

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Katherine: I admit it. Sophy surprised me.
Anne: She surprised herself too.
Katherine: I thought she lacked confidence.
Anne: She lacked opportunity. That isn’t the same thing.
Katherine: And when the pressure came, she **came through**.
Anne: More than that. She **pulled it off** beautifully.
Katherine: Perhaps I judged her too quickly.
Anne: People often look different once someone gives them room to grow.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- come through — succeed or provide needed help under pressure. The team came through when the deadline changed.
- pull something off — successfully do something difficult. They pulled off the event despite the short notice.
- rise to the occasion — perform especially well in an important or difficult situation. She rose to the occasion during the crisis.
- write someone off — decide too early that someone will fail or has no value. Don’t write him off after one bad week.
