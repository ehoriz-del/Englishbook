# Chapter 6 — Elizabeth and the Dream of Tomorrow

## Story Summary

Back in her tower room at Windy Poplars, Anne writes to Gilbert about her recent walk through the old Summerside graveyard. The visit has stayed in her mind because Miss Valentine Courtaloe’s stories mixed comedy and sadness in a way Anne finds strangely true to life. Some of the people buried there had lived ridiculous, difficult, or unhappy lives, and Anne keeps thinking especially about the married couple who were said to have spent decades disliking one another. She cannot quite accept such a simple explanation. Anne prefers to believe that strong feelings are rarely as neat as people make them sound. Her own history with Gilbert reminds her that affection can hide behind irritation, pride, and misunderstanding.

This reflection shows how much Anne has changed. When she was younger, she often judged a situation quickly and dramatically. Now she is more willing to imagine what may exist beneath the surface. She still has a romantic mind, but it has become more compassionate. She does not want to reduce people to one story about them, even when those people are dead and unable to explain themselves.

The letter then shifts from serious thoughts to the small private comedy of Windy Poplars. Anne catches Aunt Kate using buttermilk on her face as a beauty treatment. Aunt Kate is embarrassed and asks Anne not to tell Aunt Chatty, who would tease her. Anne agrees to keep the secret. The moment is light, but it also shows how completely Anne has become part of the household. She is no longer merely a boarder. She is trusted with the little vanities, habits, and confidences that make a home feel intimate.

The most important part of the chapter, however, concerns Little Elizabeth. Although the Woman has nearly recovered from her bronchitis, Elizabeth is still allowed to come to Windy Poplars for the evening milk. Anne is surprised that Mrs. Campbell and Martha Monkman continue to permit these visits, especially because Mrs. Campbell belongs to the Pringle family and the Pringles are still hostile toward Anne. Whatever the reason, Anne is grateful for the opportunity.

Elizabeth’s home life remains painfully strict. One evening Anne hears the Woman criticize the child for singing too close to Sunday. The correction may seem small, but to Anne it represents something larger. Elizabeth’s natural joy is constantly being controlled. She is told when to speak, how to behave, what she may enjoy, and even when happiness itself is considered inappropriate. Anne senses that Elizabeth is not physically neglected; she is neatly dressed and carefully supervised. What she lacks is emotional freedom.

That lack becomes especially clear when Elizabeth arrives wearing a new dark wine-colored dress. She admits that she thought she looked a little pretty in it and immediately wished her absent father could see her. Her father has become part of the child’s imagined future. Elizabeth believes that when “Tomorrow” finally arrives, he will somehow be present and everything in her life will be different. Yet on this evening she confesses that Tomorrow seems very slow in coming. She wishes time could move faster.

Anne understands the feeling more deeply than Elizabeth realizes. Anne herself spent much of childhood waiting for a home where she would truly belong. She knows what it means to create an imagined future because the present feels too narrow. For Elizabeth, “Tomorrow” is not simply the next day. It is a private language for freedom, affection, choice, and possibility. It is the place where she will not be watched constantly and where love will not have to be earned by perfect behavior.

Anne is careful not to destroy this dream. A practical adult might tell Elizabeth that life does not suddenly become perfect, but Anne knows that imagination can sometimes protect a child until reality becomes more bearable. She does not encourage false promises, yet she respects the hope behind them. Instead of laughing at Elizabeth’s fantasies, she treats them seriously. That response is one reason the child trusts her.

The contrast between Elizabeth and Anne is important. Anne is now an adult with professional responsibilities, but she has not forgotten how a child experiences the world. She can recognize the emotional meaning behind language that other adults might dismiss as foolish. When Elizabeth speaks about Tomorrow, Anne hears loneliness as well as imagination.

Anne’s own worries appear less dramatic but are still real. She must prepare geometry exercises for school and is nervous that a difficult problem may appear in class that she cannot solve. Because the Pringles are watching her so critically, an ordinary teacher’s mistake could become evidence against her. The anxiety is almost comic, but it reminds us that Anne, too, is waiting for a different kind of Tomorrow: a time when she will no longer feel that every professional weakness might be used to discredit her.

Her response is to prepare carefully rather than give in to fear. This is one of the quieter signs of her maturity. She cannot control the Pringles, but she can control how seriously she does her work. The pressure forces her to become more disciplined without taking away her humor.

The chapter ends with another domestic irritation. A mouse runs across Rebecca Dew’s foot, and Rebecca immediately blames Dusty Miller for failing in his duties as a cat. She begins chasing him away from comfortable places and treating him as if he has betrayed the household. Anne reports the situation to Gilbert with affectionate exaggeration, asking for sympathy for the unfairly accused animal.

The movement from old graves to beauty treatments, from Elizabeth’s longing to geometry, and from emotional deprivation to a household cat may seem scattered. Yet that mixture is exactly what gives Anne’s letters their character. Life at Windy Poplars is made of serious concerns and ridiculous details at the same time. Anne is learning to hold both without losing perspective.

Most of all, she is becoming a safe person for Elizabeth. She cannot yet change the child’s home, and she cannot bring the promised Tomorrow immediately. What she can do is listen, notice, and make one small part of Elizabeth’s present kinder. The chapter suggests that hope is not always a grand solution. Sometimes it begins when one person takes another person’s dreams seriously.

## Vocabulary & Nuance

- **wistful** — quietly sad because you want something that seems distant or difficult to have. Elizabeth is wistful when she wishes her father could see her.
- **deprivation** — a lack of something necessary or deeply important. Elizabeth suffers more from emotional deprivation than material deprivation.
- **confide in** — tell someone private thoughts because you trust them. Elizabeth begins to confide in Anne.
- **dismissive** — showing that you think something is unimportant or foolish. Anne is never dismissive of Elizabeth’s imagination.
- **underlying** — existing beneath the obvious surface. Anne notices the underlying loneliness in Elizabeth’s talk about Tomorrow.
- **hold on to** — continue to keep or believe in something. Elizabeth holds on to the idea that a happier future will come.
- **brush aside** — treat an idea or feeling as unimportant. Anne refuses to brush aside the child’s hopes.
- **weigh on** — cause worry or emotional pressure over time. The Pringles’ scrutiny weighs on Anne at school.
- **keep up with** — continue doing what is necessary to meet a standard or demand. Anne works hard to keep up with difficult geometry lessons.
- **take someone seriously** — respect what a person says or feels instead of treating it as childish or unimportant. Anne takes Elizabeth seriously.

## Conversation 1 — Holding On to Tomorrow

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Elizabeth: Sometimes I think the future is taking forever to arrive.
Anne: What do you imagine will be different when it does?
Elizabeth: I’ll be able to make choices for myself. I won’t feel watched all the time.
Anne: That sounds less like a date on the calendar and more like a kind of freedom.
Elizabeth: Do you think it’s silly?
Anne: Not at all. But while you **hold on to** that hope, don’t forget that some good things can begin now.
Elizabeth: Such as?
Anne: Being heard. Being understood. Having someone who believes your feelings matter.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- hold on to — keep believing in or keeping something. She held on to hope during a difficult year.
- look forward to — feel happy or hopeful about something in the future. I’m looking forward to having more independence.
- open up to — begin sharing private thoughts or feelings. He finally opened up to a close friend.
- get through — survive or complete a difficult period. We’ll get through this stressful month.
- take someone seriously — treat a person’s concerns as important. Good teachers take students seriously.

## Conversation 2 — Pressure at Work

*A modern conversation inspired by Anne’s professional worries.*

Gilbert: You sound anxious about tomorrow’s class.
Anne: I am. I keep imagining someone will ask the one geometry question I can’t answer.
Gilbert: Is the problem really geometry, or is something else **weighing on** you?
Anne: The Pringles. I feel as though every mistake will be reported and exaggerated.
Gilbert: Then prepare well, but don’t expect perfection from yourself.
Anne: Easier said than done.
Gilbert: True. Still, being competent doesn’t mean never being caught off guard.
Anne: I suppose I need to stop treating every difficult question as a public trial.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- weigh on — remain in your mind and cause worry. The unresolved issue weighed on her all week.
- catch someone off guard — surprise someone when they are not prepared. The interviewer caught me off guard with the final question.
- live up to — meet a standard or expectation. She feels pressure to live up to everyone’s expectations.
- work through — deal with a problem gradually and carefully. We can work through the difficult questions together.
- let go of — stop holding onto an unrealistic expectation, fear, or emotion. He needs to let go of the idea that every mistake is a failure.
