# Chapter 10 — Breaking Cyrus Taylor’s Silence

## Story Summary

When Anne arrives at the Taylor home the next evening, she knows immediately that Trix’s fears were justified. The house is elegant and the dinner has been carefully prepared, but the emotional atmosphere is cold. Anne catches sight of Mrs. Taylor moving anxiously between rooms with tears in her eyes. Cyrus has not recovered from his bad mood.

In fact, several small events have made it worse. Earlier in the day he lost a game of checkers, which he dislikes doing. He became angry when he saw Esme looking at herself in the mirror before the important dinner and locked her out of her room before she could finish dressing. Trix has not dared curl her hair because her father dislikes artificial curls. The flowers Mrs. Taylor arranged for the table have been removed because Cyrus objected to them. Even Mrs. Taylor’s jewelry has been affected by his preferences.

These details show that Trix had not exaggerated. The whole family is adjusting its appearance and behavior around one man’s mood.

At the table, the tension becomes almost unbearable. Mrs. Taylor looks pale and frightened. Esme, normally the beauty of the family, is even paler because she is terrified the evening will destroy her hopes with Dr. Lennox Carter. Young Pringle, usually cheerful, is quiet. Trix looks more like a frightened schoolgirl than the lively young woman Anne knows.

Dr. Carter senses that something is wrong but does not understand the cause. Cyrus enters, takes his place, and refuses to speak. He will not even lead the usual prayer before the meal, forcing Mrs. Taylor to do it in a trembling voice.

The family’s nervousness spreads. Esme drops a fork, and everyone jumps except Cyrus. He uses looks rather than words to show displeasure. A glance at his wife is enough to make her stop eating a sauce she enjoys because she suddenly worries about her health. Conversation dies almost completely.

Trix had asked Anne to talk as much as possible, but even Anne finds herself unable to keep the room alive. Every ordinary subject seems foolish. Dr. Carter is equally cautious, perhaps because he does not want to offend the father of the woman he may wish to marry.

Anne becomes increasingly angry. Cyrus is not merely silent; he appears to enjoy the discomfort his silence creates. She begins to see him less as an impressive patriarch and more as a spoiled person who has trained everyone to react to his displeasure.

Her strongest impulse is to make him speak. She imagines dramatic ways of provoking him, but then a different idea arrives. When Mrs. Taylor asks Anne to pass something at the table, Anne suddenly tells Dr. Carter that Cyrus has recently become deaf.

The statement is not true. Anne says it impulsively because she knows Cyrus is listening and because she wants to remove the power of his silence. If everyone believes he cannot hear them, his refusal to speak no longer controls the conversation.

Trix and her younger brother immediately understand the opportunity. They begin asking Dr. Carter what he would think about a man who behaved in a series of ridiculous, selfish, or embarrassing ways. They do not directly accuse their father. Instead, they present each behavior as a hypothetical question.

Because Dr. Carter believes Cyrus cannot hear, he answers more freely than he otherwise might. The children become bolder. The examples grow more outrageous. Some concern controlling a wife, embarrassing behavior, foolish opinions, or strange habits. Anne begins to regret what she has started. She intended to loosen the atmosphere, not to give the family unlimited permission to mock Cyrus in front of a guest.

Cyrus’s face shows that he is hearing every word. He struggles to maintain the silence because speaking would expose the fact that he is not deaf. For the first time, the weapon he normally uses against his family traps him instead.

The scene could easily become cruel. Then Esme changes its direction. Unlike Trix and Pringle, she offers an example that reveals her father’s kindness: once he spent a long time trying to rescue helpless kittens because he could not bear the thought of them suffering. Her defense reminds everyone that Cyrus is not only his worst habit.

Mrs. Taylor, wanting to support her husband too, adds another detail about something he did while ill. Unfortunately, that final detail touches Cyrus’s pride. He reaches the limit of what he can endure.

He pushes his chair back so violently that it strikes a small table and breaks an old vase that the family has secretly disliked for years. Then Cyrus finally speaks.

Once the silence breaks, everything changes. He protests the exaggerated stories, denies the ridiculous accusations, and demands to know why anyone would believe them. Trix points out that Anne never literally said he was deaf in the way he assumed, and the family begins responding instead of shrinking.

Then something even more unexpected happens: Cyrus laughs.

The laughter transforms his face and the room. He admits that sulking is a bad habit. He insists that it is his one bad habit, which may not be entirely accurate, but the admission matters. He recognizes that the family has successfully turned his method of control back on him.

He also notices that Esme defended him when the others were teasing him. Rather than punishing the family, he allows the evening to move forward. The broken vase is cleared away, dessert arrives, and the dinner becomes pleasant.

Anne is astonished by how quickly the mood changes. Cyrus, when he is no longer sulking, can be warm and entertaining. Dr. Carter relaxes. The family’s fear disappears because the forbidden silence has been broken.

A few days later, Trix tells Anne that she finally found the courage to tell Cyrus about her engagement to Johnny. She expected a terrible reaction. Instead, her father responds with surprising practicality, suggesting that Johnny has taken long enough to make the relationship official. The experience shows Trix that years of fear had made the anticipated confrontation larger than the confrontation itself.

The chapter is comic, but it also examines a serious pattern. Silence can be used as a form of emotional control when everyone around the silent person becomes responsible for restoring peace. Cyrus’s family has been trained to guess, appease, and apologize. Anne’s reckless strategy works because it changes the rules. For one dinner, the family behaves as though Cyrus’s silence has no power.

Yet Anne also learns something. Intervention is unpredictable. Once she starts the joke, Trix and Pringle take it much farther than she intended. Anne cannot completely control the consequences of her clever idea. She succeeds, but she is left with a healthy respect for how quickly a social experiment can get out of hand.

Esme’s defense of her father is equally important. She refuses to reduce Cyrus to his worst behavior. She reminds the table that a difficult person can also be generous and tender. That does not excuse his sulking, but it prevents the scene from becoming simple revenge.

By the end, the Taylor family has not become perfect. Cyrus will still have faults, and the others will still have to learn to speak more honestly. But the balance has shifted. They have seen that his silence can be challenged, and Cyrus has seen himself reflected in the reactions of the people he loves.

Anne came to dinner hoping merely to prevent an awkward evening. Instead, she helps expose a family pattern that everyone had accepted as permanent. The result is messy, risky, funny, and ultimately freeing.

## Vocabulary & Nuance

- **oppressive** — causing a heavy feeling of pressure or discomfort. Cyrus creates an oppressive silence at the table.
- **provocative** — likely to cause a strong reaction. Trix and Pringle begin asking provocative questions.
- **hypothetical** — imagined rather than stated as an actual fact. The children frame their accusations as hypothetical situations.
- **defuse** — make a tense or dangerous situation calmer. Cyrus’s eventual laughter helps defuse the conflict.
- **retaliate** — respond to an attack by attacking in return. Anne worries Cyrus may retaliate when he finally speaks.
- **get out of hand** — become difficult to control. The teasing quickly gets out of hand.
- **call someone’s bluff** — challenge someone to prove a threat or position they may not be able to maintain. Anne effectively calls Cyrus’s bluff by treating him as if he cannot hear.
- **break the tension** — end or reduce an uncomfortable atmosphere. Cyrus’s laughter breaks the tension.
- **stand up for** — defend someone when others criticize them. Esme stands up for her father.
- **backfire** — produce the opposite result from what was intended. Anne fears her plan may backfire before it finally succeeds.

## Conversation 1 — Calling Someone’s Bluff

*A modern conversation inspired by the turning point at dinner.*

Maya: He refuses to answer anyone when he’s angry.
Jon: And everyone stops talking?
Maya: Pretty much.
Jon: Then the silence works because everyone obeys it.
Maya: What would you do?
Jon: I wouldn’t humiliate him, but I might **call his bluff** by continuing the meeting normally.
Maya: That could **backfire**.
Jon: It could. But rewarding the behavior every time has a cost too.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- call someone’s bluff — challenge a position or threat instead of automatically accepting it. The negotiator called their bluff.
- backfire — have an unintended negative result. The joke backfired badly.
- get out of hand — become difficult to control. The discussion got out of hand after people began shouting.
- push back — resist pressure or challenge a behavior. The staff finally pushed back against the unreasonable demand.
- keep something going — continue an activity or conversation. Anne tries to keep the dinner conversation going.

## Conversation 2 — Defending the Whole Person

*A modern conversation inspired by Esme’s response.*

Trix: After the way he’s behaved, why are you defending him?
Esme: I’m not defending the sulking.
Trix: Then what are you doing?
Esme: I’m reminding everyone that he’s more than one bad habit.
Anne: That matters.
Esme: We can **stand up to** the behavior without pretending there’s nothing good in him.
Anne: That may be the hardest kind of honesty.
Trix: Criticizing what’s wrong without rewriting the whole person?
Anne: Exactly.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- stand up to — resist someone confidently when their behavior is unfair. She finally stood up to the bully.
- stand up for — defend a person who is being criticized or attacked. Esme stands up for her father.
- calm down — become less upset or angry. Everyone calmed down once Cyrus began to laugh.
- own up to — admit that you are responsible for something. Cyrus partly owns up to his bad habit of sulking.
- move past — stop being controlled by a previous conflict or mistake. The family is finally able to move past the disastrous beginning of dinner.
