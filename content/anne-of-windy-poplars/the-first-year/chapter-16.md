# Chapter 16 — The Light in the Attic Window

## Story Summary

The morning of Sally Nelson’s wedding begins in a rush of last-minute work. Anne helps in the kitchen, while Nora prepares food and tries to recover from the emotional honesty of the previous night. Nora is embarrassed that she told Anne so much about Jim Wilcox. She becomes sharp and defensive, as though irritation can rebuild the wall she temporarily lowered.

Aunt Mouser continues to move through the house predicting disasters. Her gloomy comments should make everyone miserable, but by now most of the younger people treat them as part of the comedy of the occasion. The household is chaotic, yet by noon everything is ready. Flowers fill the rooms, the tables are prepared, and the wedding party dresses upstairs.

Anne looks beautiful in her bridesmaid clothes and briefly wishes Gilbert could see her. Nora also looks striking, though she claims there is no one who cares how she looks. Her bitterness has not disappeared. Because the woman who was supposed to play the wedding march develops a severe headache, Nora has to take over the music. For someone already struggling with romantic disappointment, playing the music for her sister’s ceremony feels almost cruel.

The wedding itself succeeds. Sally and Gordon are married, the family celebrates, and the large dinner proceeds. After the main excitement, however, the house takes on the untidy, exhausted feeling that follows a major event. Flowers are crushed, chairs are out of place, dishes are everywhere, and the beautiful order of the ceremony has dissolved into cleanup.

Nora’s mood becomes darker. She talks about leaving Summerside and training as a nurse simply to escape the constant attention given to her unmarried status. She does not actually sound enthusiastic about nursing; the plan is more an attempt to imagine a life that will take her away from reminders of Jim. Anne helps her clean up, understanding that Nora needs company more than advice.

That evening the younger guests plan a bonfire and dance on the shore. The bay is shining in the moonlight, and under different circumstances Anne would have enjoyed the scene without reservation. But Nora’s unhappiness stays in her mind. Anne remembers the story Nora told the night before: when Nora and Jim were young, a light in the attic window had been their signal. If Nora wanted Jim to come over, she put a light there, and he would cross the bay.

An idea comes to Anne suddenly. Impulsiveness has caused trouble for her many times, but it is also part of what makes her capable of unexpected acts of courage and kindness. She takes a small lamp, runs up to the attic, and places it in the dormer window facing the harbor. She knows Nora may be furious if she discovers the plan. Anne decides that anger will be worth it if Jim sees the light and comes.

At first, nothing happens. The shore dance continues. Anne watches for Jim, then gradually forgets about the signal in the pleasure of the evening. More importantly, she forgets to remove the lamp from the attic window. This mistake turns a brief romantic gesture into a beacon that remains visible for hours.

Long after everyone has gone to bed, Aunt Mouser wakes the household because she hears a suspicious noise downstairs. She is convinced there may be a burglar. Dr. Nelson, several guests, and Anne gather and move cautiously toward the library. The scene is half frightening and half ridiculous: people are in nightclothes, someone carries a poker, and everyone has a different theory about the sounds.

In the library they discover Nora with a young man—Jim Wilcox. Nora has a bleeding nose because she ran into a door in her excitement. Jim is holding a handkerchief to her face, which Aunt Mouser initially misinterprets in the most dramatic possible way. Jim explains that he saw the old signal after returning late to Summerside and crossed the bay because he believed Nora wanted him.

Nora immediately denies sending the signal. Pride returns at once. Jim, embarrassed and hurt, prepares to leave. The whole situation could collapse into another misunderstanding. Anne realizes she must tell the truth. She admits that she was the one who placed the lamp in the attic window and then forgot about it. Nora is furious.

The confusion, however, finally forces Jim and Nora to say aloud what they have refused to say privately for years. Aunt Mouser remarks that Jim may now have to marry Nora because of the scandal of being found together in the middle of the night. Jim explodes: marrying Nora is exactly what he has always wanted.

Nora is shocked. She asks why he never said so. Jim answers that she had repeatedly rejected, mocked, and frozen him out. From his perspective, Nora had made it clear that a proposal would be useless. Nora fires back that Jim had pushed her into their quarrel and seemed eager to be rid of her. The conversation reveals that both have interpreted pride as indifference.

At last Jim asks Nora directly to marry him. This time there is no coded signal, teasing, or defensive sarcasm. Nora accepts immediately. The absurd midnight audience—family members, Aunt Mouser, Anne, and even the household cat—makes the proposal far from romantic in the conventional sense, yet its emotional truth is stronger than a carefully planned scene would have been.

The next morning Nora looks transformed by happiness. She tells Anne that she owes the reconciliation to the attic lamp, even though for a few minutes she was furious enough to hate Anne for interfering. Anne’s impulsive act has worked.

Yet the chapter does not present Anne’s interference as a method to imitate casually. The entire episode depends on coincidence. Jim happens to see the light. Nora happens to be awake. The household happens to discover them. The pressure of the moment happens to break through years of pride. It could easily have ended badly.

What matters most is not Anne’s cleverness but the underlying problem between Nora and Jim: neither had been willing to risk honest vulnerability. Their old signal belonged to childhood, a time when communication between them was simpler. The lamp brings Jim physically back, but the reconciliation only becomes real when they stop relying on hints and finally speak plainly.

The chapter therefore turns a romantic symbol into a lesson about communication. Signals can open a door, but relationships cannot survive on signals alone. Nora and Jim need direct words. Anne’s lamp creates the crisis; honesty resolves it.

## Vocabulary & Nuance

- **impulsive** — acting suddenly without fully considering the consequences.
- **aftermath** — the situation that remains after an important or disruptive event.
- **mortified** — extremely embarrassed or ashamed.
- **incredulous** — unable or unwilling to believe something because it is surprising.
- **reconciliation** — restoration of a friendly or loving relationship after conflict.
- **come clean** — admit the truth about something, especially after hiding it.
- **turn someone down** — reject a request, invitation, or romantic proposal.
- **freeze someone out** — deliberately make someone feel unwelcome or excluded.
- **set something off** — cause a chain of events or reactions to begin.
- **clear the air** — talk honestly in order to remove tension or misunderstanding.

*A modern conversation inspired by Nora and Jim.*

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- turn someone down — reject a request or proposal.
- clear the air — discuss tension honestly.
- read into — find a meaning in something that may not be intended.
- shut someone out — refuse emotional access or communication.
- come out with — finally say something directly.

## Conversation 2 — Owning an Impulsive Decision

*A modern conversation inspired by Anne admitting she lit the lamp.*

Anne: I need to tell you something before this gets worse.
Nora: What did you do?
Anne: I put the lamp in the attic window.
Nora: You what?
Anne: I thought Jim might see it. Then I forgot to take it down.
Nora: I’m furious.
Anne: I know. I’m not going to **make excuses**. I should have thought it through.
Nora: At least you **came clean**.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- come clean — admit the truth.
- make excuses — give reasons to avoid responsibility.
- think something through — consider consequences carefully.
- set off — cause a sequence of events.
- own up to — admit responsibility for something.
