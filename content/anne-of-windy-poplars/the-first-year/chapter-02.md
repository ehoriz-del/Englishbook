# Chapter 2 — The Pringle Problem

## Story Summary

As Anne’s first weeks at Summerside High continue, the vague warnings about the Pringle family become a practical problem. She discovers that the clan’s disapproval is not limited to private conversation. It affects the classroom, the parents, and the general atmosphere of the school. Anne has dealt with difficult pupils before, but she has never faced a group whose resistance is supported by a powerful family network outside the classroom.

The Pringle objection to Anne is partly personal and partly political. They had wanted someone connected with their own circle to become principal, and they dislike the fact that an outsider has been appointed. Anne’s youth gives them another reason to criticize her. Some suggest that she is too inexperienced. Others question whether a young woman should have such authority. The complaints are often indirect, which makes them difficult to answer. If someone openly accused Anne of incompetence, she could respond with evidence. Instead, she faces hints, gossip, and comments that are designed to **undermine** confidence without creating one clear issue to debate.

At school, Jen Pringle becomes the most obvious symbol of the resistance. Jen is bright, capable, and fully aware of her family’s influence. This makes her more challenging than a student who simply dislikes studying. She is intelligent enough to test rules without always breaking them openly. A late assignment can be explained. A disrespectful expression can be denied. A coordinated lack of effort among several cousins can be presented as coincidence. Anne senses that much of the behavior is deliberate, but she knows that punishing students without clear evidence would only strengthen the family’s argument that she is unfair.

Other Pringle students follow Jen’s lead. Some refuse to cooperate fully. Some perform poorly in ways that seem calculated to embarrass the school. When Anne corrects them, relatives are ready with explanations or complaints. There are also rumors about Anne’s background. The fact that she was an orphan is turned into a reason to question her social standing, as though character and professional ability could be measured by family connections.

Anne finds this deeply frustrating because the conflict is based on **prejudice** rather than reason. She can explain a school rule, but she cannot logically prove that she deserves respect to people who have already decided that she does not. She is tempted to imagine dramatic forms of revenge, and in her private thoughts she allows herself the humor of those fantasies. In practice, however, she chooses restraint.

Her strategy is to remain consistent. She expects the same work from Pringle students that she expects from everyone else. She avoids **singling them out** simply because she distrusts their motives. When they do something wrong, she responds to the specific behavior instead of attacking the family. This approach is slower than open confrontation, but it protects Anne’s integrity. She refuses to become the unreasonable person her opponents expect her to be.

Katherine Brooke is not much help. As vice-principal, she could make Anne’s position easier, but Katherine’s own disappointment about the principalship makes her distant and sarcastic. She does her work, yet she offers little emotional support. Anne therefore has to manage both upward and downward pressure: she must answer to parents and school authorities while also supervising staff and students.

Still, not everyone in Summerside is hostile. Anne has pupils outside the Pringle circle who respond to her teaching, and these small successes matter. A student who finally understands a difficult point or a class that becomes interested in a lesson reminds her why she accepted the position. She also receives invitations from families who are curious about the new principal and willing to know her for herself.

Outside school, Anne begins to notice a lonely child who lives near Windy Poplars. Little Elizabeth has lost her mother and is being raised in a strict household while her father remains far away. The adults responsible for her provide order and material care, but not much warmth. Elizabeth’s reserve catches Anne’s attention because it is different from Jen’s defiance. Jen uses confidence as a weapon; Elizabeth seems to protect herself by expecting very little.

Anne’s first conversations with the child are cautious. She does not immediately try to rescue or advise her. Instead, she listens. Anne has learned from her own childhood that lonely children do not always need adults to solve everything at once. Sometimes they need someone who takes their thoughts seriously.

This contrast between Jen and Elizabeth deepens the chapter. Anne is learning that difficult behavior has different causes. One student may challenge authority because she feels powerful enough to do so. Another child may withdraw because she feels powerless. Treating them exactly the same would not be fairness; it would be a failure to understand them.

The pressure from the Pringles continues, and Anne does not pretend it is easy. She sometimes goes back to Windy Poplars angry, tired, or discouraged. Yet she manages to **keep her composure** in public. Rebecca Dew is furious on Anne’s behalf and would happily condemn the entire clan, but Anne recognizes that open warfare would make her job almost impossible.

By the end of this chapter, Anne has not won the conflict. What she has achieved is more subtle: she has refused to let opposition define her behavior. She is beginning to see the social structure of Summerside clearly. She understands that authority is not something a job title automatically gives her. She will have to build it through fairness, patience, judgment, and the ability to **push back** without becoming hostile herself.

## Vocabulary & Nuance

- **undermine** — gradually weaken someone’s confidence, authority, or position. Repeated rumors can undermine a new manager.
- **prejudice** — an unfair opinion formed before knowing the facts. Anne realizes that some criticism comes from prejudice rather than evidence.
- **resistance** — opposition to a change, rule, or authority. The students’ resistance is subtle but coordinated.
- **calculated** — deliberately planned to produce a particular effect. Her silence seemed calculated to make the meeting uncomfortable.
- **keep your composure** — remain calm and in control of your emotions. Anne keeps her composure during tense conversations.
- **push back** — resist pressure or challenge an unfair demand. She pushes back without becoming rude.
- **single someone out** — treat one person differently from the group, often unfairly. Anne avoids singling out Pringle students without evidence.
- **back someone up** — support someone when they face difficulty or criticism. A good colleague should back you up when a decision is fair.

## Conversation 1 — Handling Subtle Resistance

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Anne: The hardest part is that no one says directly what they are doing.
Katherine: Then perhaps there is nothing to confront.
Anne: I don’t think that’s true. Some of the students are clearly trying to **push back** against every rule.
Katherine: They know exactly how far they can go.
Anne: Which means I have to respond to facts, not to what I suspect.
Katherine: That sounds exhausting.
Anne: It is, but if I **single them out** without proof, I’ll make the situation worse.
Katherine: At least you understand the game they’re playing.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- push back — resist or challenge pressure, a decision, or a demand. Employees pushed back against the unrealistic deadline.
- single someone out — choose one person for special attention, often unfairly. Don’t single her out unless you have clear evidence.
- get away with — do something wrong without being punished or noticed. He thought he could get away with ignoring the rule.
- call someone out — publicly challenge someone’s behavior or statement. She called him out for changing the numbers.

## Conversation 2 — Staying Professional Under Pressure

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Gilbert: Do you ever feel like telling them exactly what you think?
Anne: Frequently. But I’m trying to **keep my composure**.
Gilbert: That doesn’t mean you have to accept unfair treatment.
Anne: No. I can **stand up for myself** without turning every problem into a personal fight.
Gilbert: That sounds like progress.
Anne: I’m learning that leadership is partly about not reacting too quickly.
Gilbert: Especially when someone wants a reaction.
Anne: Exactly. I won’t give them that advantage.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- keep your composure — stay calm even when you are upset or under pressure. She kept her composure during the argument.
- stand up for yourself — defend your rights or position. You can be polite and still stand up for yourself.
- take the bait — react to something designed to provoke you. He insulted her, but she refused to take the bait.
- play into someone’s hands — act in a way that helps an opponent. Losing your temper would play into their hands.
