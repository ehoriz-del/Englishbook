# Chapter 7 — Captain Abraham’s Secret

## Story Summary

Near the end of a mild December week, Anne travels to Lowvale with one of her students, Wilfred Bryce. He has invited her to a church turkey supper and to spend the following day at the home of his uncle and aunt. Anne accepts partly because she likes Wilfred and partly because she hopes to help him with a serious problem. He is intelligent and ambitious, but his uncle is considering taking him out of school after Christmas. Anne believes that, with more education, Wilfred could create a very different future for himself.

The visit is not especially comfortable. Wilfred’s relatives are rougher and less polished than the people Anne usually visits, and Saturday turns cold, windy, and snowy. Wilfred must help with farm work, leaving Anne with little to do. In another household she might have spent the morning reading, but there are almost no books available.

Then Anne remembers an old sailor’s chest she noticed upstairs. She also remembers a request from Mrs. Stanton, who is preparing a history of Prince County. Mrs. Stanton has asked Anne to watch for old diaries, maps, letters, or documents that might contain useful information about local life. Because the Stantons and the Pringles are not friendly, Mrs. Stanton cannot easily ask the most historically prominent family in Summerside for access to its private records.

With permission from Wilfred’s uncle, Anne searches the chest. Most of its contents are unimportant, but she finds an old, yellowed sea diary kept by Andy Bryce, a sailor who once traveled with Captain Abraham Pringle. The writing is rough and ungrammatical, but the journal is vivid. Andy clearly admired Abraham. He describes him as courageous and highly capable, especially during dangerous voyages.

The diary also mentions Abraham’s brother, Captain Myrom Pringle, in a very different way. One entry records an ugly domestic incident. Another tells a much darker story: after Myrom’s ship was destroyed and the survivors were stranded with little food, the men resorted to eating a dead crewman in order to survive. The detail is presented almost casually in the old diary, which makes it even more shocking to Anne.

Anne immediately understands the social importance of what she has found. The Pringles take enormous pride in their ancestors and in the dignity of the family name. A story suggesting that one of their captains had taken part in cannibalism would be humiliating if it became public. Yet Anne has not been searching for a weapon. She is interested in the parts of the diary concerning Captain Abraham because his daughters, Miss Sarah and Miss Ellen Pringle, worship his memory. She thinks they might treasure a record that praises their father.

At first she decides not to give them the diary. Why should she do anything kind for people who have worked so hard to undermine her? The Pringle campaign against her has damaged her confidence and threatened her position at school. Anne feels they are succeeding in driving her toward defeat. Her resentment is understandable.

Before returning to Summerside, however, Anne accomplishes the original purpose of the visit. She talks with Wilfred’s uncle and persuades him to let the boy remain in high school for the rest of the year. Wilfred is deeply grateful. He hopes eventually to attend Queen’s and then work while continuing his education. Anne has quietly changed the direction of one student’s life simply by taking his ambitions seriously and speaking on his behalf.

That success contrasts with her bitterness toward the Pringles. During the night at Windy Poplars, Anne keeps waking and changing her mind about the diary. One hour she decides to send it. Later she decides the Pringle sisters do not deserve the kindness. Eventually she realizes that refusing only out of spite would make her behave in a way she dislikes in others. She does not want resentment to make her petty.

On Monday, Anne wraps the diary and sends it to Miss Sarah with a formal note explaining that it contains material about Captain Abraham that the sisters may find interesting. Anne expects either silence or a cold return of the book.

Instead, Windy Poplars receives an extraordinary visit. A carriage from Maplehurst arrives, and both Miss Ellen and Miss Sarah appear at the front door. Sarah has rarely left her home for years, so the visit is startling. Rebecca Dew nearly loses control of herself with curiosity.

Anne enters the parlor expecting another confrontation. She gets one, but not the confrontation she imagined. Miss Sarah announces that the Pringles are ready to give in. She assumes Anne deliberately sent the diary as a threat. In Sarah’s interpretation, Anne discovered the scandal about Myrom and is warning the family that she could give the story to Mrs. Stanton’s county history. If Anne remains silent, the Pringles will stop their campaign against her. Jen will apologize, and the family will cooperate.

Anne is horrified. She had never considered blackmail. She explains that she sent the diary because she thought the sisters would enjoy the passages praising their father and that she has no intention of sharing the disturbing entry about Myrom.

The old women suddenly see Anne differently. They realize that they have judged her according to their own expectations of conflict. Sarah admits that the family has treated Anne badly and asks forgiveness. The meeting changes from tense negotiation to friendly conversation. The Pringle sisters leave with the diary and with a new understanding of Anne.

The effect is immediate. Jen returns to school, apologizes publicly, and becomes a cooperative pupil. The younger Pringles follow her example. Complaints disappear. Invitations begin to arrive. Adults who once ignored or opposed Anne now make an effort to include her.

Ironically, people in Summerside assume Anne must have defeated the Pringles through some brilliant strategy. Rebecca Dew is convinced there is more to the story than Anne will tell. In reality, the turning point came because Anne chose not to be petty. She did not use the secret, but the Pringles’ fear that she might use it forced them to reconsider their behavior.

The chapter is therefore not simply about finding damaging information. It is about what a person does when suddenly given power. Anne could have exposed the family or allowed them to believe she meant to threaten them. Instead, she protects their dignity. Her restraint wins more than revenge could have won. At the same time, she does not need to surrender her position. The Pringles end the conflict because they finally understand that Anne is both stronger and kinder than they expected.

## Vocabulary & Nuance

- **capitulate** — stop resisting and accept defeat or another person’s terms. The Pringles arrive prepared to capitulate.
- **trepidation** — fear or nervousness about what may happen. Anne goes downstairs with trepidation when the Pringle sisters arrive.
- **leverage** — information or power that can be used to influence a situation. The diary unexpectedly gives Anne leverage.
- **discretion** — careful judgment about what should remain private. Anne shows discretion by refusing to spread the scandal.
- **petty** — too concerned with small resentments or insults. Anne decides she does not want to behave in a petty way.
- **speak up for** — defend or support someone, especially when that person has less power. Anne speaks up for Wilfred’s education.
- **come across** — find something unexpectedly. Anne comes across an old sea diary in the chest.
- **give in** — stop resisting. The Pringles give in because they fear the family story will become public.
- **hold something over someone** — use private information as a threat or source of control. The Pringles wrongly assume Anne plans to hold the diary over them.
- **turn the tide** — change a situation so that it begins moving in a different direction. The discovery of the diary turns the tide in Anne’s conflict with the Pringles.

## Conversation 1 — Having Leverage Without Abusing It

*A modern conversation inspired by this chapter.*

Maya: I found an email that proves the director made the mistake, not our team.
Jon: That gives you a lot of **leverage**.
Maya: I know, but I don’t want to **hold it over** anyone.
Jon: You don’t have to. You can correct the record without humiliating him.
Maya: So I should use the information only as far as necessary?
Jon: Exactly. Power and discretion can exist together.
Maya: That’s harder than simply winning the argument.
Jon: Usually the better choices are.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- hold something over someone — use information to threaten or control someone. He kept holding an old mistake over her.
- come across — find unexpectedly. I came across the document while sorting old files.
- give in — stop resisting. They finally gave in after weeks of disagreement.
- speak up for — publicly support or defend. She spoke up for the junior employee.
- turn the tide — cause a major change in the direction of a situation. One strong presentation turned the tide of the negotiations.

## Conversation 2 — Choosing Not to Be Petty

*A modern conversation inspired by Anne’s decision to send the diary.*

Anne: I could keep the diary and let them wonder what I know.
Gilbert: Would that make you feel better?
Anne: For about five minutes.
Gilbert: And after that?
Anne: I’d dislike myself for letting resentment make the decision.
Gilbert: Then you already know what to do.
Anne: I’ll send it. Not because they deserve a favor, but because I don’t want to **sink to** pettiness.
Gilbert: That sounds like you.
Anne: I hope so.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- sink to — behave at a lower moral standard than usual. Don’t sink to personal insults just because the discussion is heated.
- act on — take action because of a feeling, idea, or piece of information. She was angry but chose not to act on it.
- let go of — stop holding onto resentment or anger. He finally let go of the old grievance.
- rise above — behave better than a difficult or insulting situation might encourage. She rose above the gossip and stayed professional.
- make amends — do something to repair harm you caused. The family tried to make amends after realizing they had been unfair.
