# Chapter 9 — Dinner with the Taylors

## Story Summary

One cold February evening, Trix Taylor visits Anne in the tower room at Windy Poplars. Snow moves against the windows while the small stove keeps the room warm, and Trix settles in to tell Anne about a family crisis. By this point in Summerside, Anne has become someone people trust with private information. Because she is engaged to Gilbert, young women do not see her as a romantic rival, and because she listens without immediately judging, people feel safe bringing her secrets.

Trix is twenty, cheerful, lively, and normally not the sort of person who appears burdened by life. Yet she is deeply worried about a dinner her family is giving the following evening. Their guest will be Dr. Lennox Carter, a young professor who teaches modern languages at Redmond College. Trix’s older sister Esme is in love with him, and the family believes he may be close to proposing marriage.

The dinner therefore seems unusually important. Esme is shy, and Dr. Carter is intelligent, serious, and somewhat formal. Trix believes he is evaluating not only Esme but the entire Taylor family. She has heard that he thinks a man should be careful about the family he marries into. If the evening goes badly, Esme fears he may decide that marriage would be a mistake.

Trix’s greatest worry is not the food, the conversation, or even Esme’s nervousness. It is her father, Cyrus Taylor.

Cyrus is successful, generous in many practical ways, and capable of being entertaining when he is in a good mood. But when something displeases him, he has a habit of becoming completely silent. He does not shout. He does not explain what is wrong. Instead, he withdraws into a long, deliberate sulk, and the entire household becomes tense.

Trix explains that the family has learned to fear these silences. Her mother becomes anxious, Esme loses confidence, and even Trix and her younger brother Pringle—both lively by nature—become cautious. Cyrus has discovered that he does not need to raise his voice to control a room. His refusal to speak does the work for him.

The immediate danger is absurdly small. Cyrus cannot find a new flannel nightshirt because Esme placed it in the wrong drawer. Trix does not know whether he will still be offended about it the next evening. The ridiculous cause makes the family’s fear seem even more troubling. A misplaced piece of clothing can influence the emotional life of the entire household because nobody knows how long Cyrus will choose to remain displeased.

Trix and her mother have planned the dinner around him. They want Anne there because Cyrus likes her and because Anne can keep a conversation moving. Even the menu is designed to avoid irritating him. The family chooses a dessert he prefers rather than the pie most guests might expect. Trix asks Anne to wear a blue dress, hoping that if Cyrus admires Anne’s blue clothing he may be less critical of Esme’s new blue dress.

This level of preparation reveals how much the family has adapted itself around Cyrus’s moods. Instead of expecting him to behave reasonably as a host, everyone else tries to remove every possible cause of annoyance. Their actions may look like care, but they are also a form of fear.

Trix then confesses another problem. She is secretly engaged to Johnny, a young man her father does not consider financially suitable. Cyrus himself began life without much money, but he disapproves of Johnny’s lack of wealth. Trix has delayed telling her father because she cannot face the silence and anger she expects to follow. She plans to wait until Esme’s romantic future is settled.

Anne listens with sympathy, but she also sees the situation differently because she is outside the family. She suggests that Trix has lost perspective. When a person has lived inside a pattern for years, that pattern can begin to feel unavoidable. Anne suspects the family’s fear may be giving Cyrus more power than he would have if they simply continued speaking and behaving normally during his sulks.

Trix insists Anne does not understand. The silence, she says, paralyzes them. They have tried living through it, and they cannot treat it as ordinary. Even the strongest family members become nervous when Cyrus refuses to speak.

The conversation is important because it shows a form of power that does not depend on open aggression. Cyrus does not need to threaten anyone directly. The family anticipates his displeasure and changes its behavior in advance. Everyone is managing his emotions for him.

Anne’s outsider perspective is both useful and limited. She can see that the family has become trapped in a pattern, but she has not yet experienced Cyrus’s silence at the dinner table. She believes that if everyone stopped reacting so strongly, the sulks might lose their force. The next evening will test that theory.

The chapter also complicates Cyrus as a character. Trix does not describe him as cruel in every situation. She admits he can be very agreeable and even the life of a party. He likes his daughters to have attractive clothes and wants Esme to make a good marriage. The problem is that his family never knows which version of him will appear.

That unpredictability is what makes the household anxious. When affection depends on another person’s mood, people begin scanning constantly for signs of trouble. Trix’s elaborate planning for the dinner is therefore less about hospitality than about emotional risk management.

After Trix leaves, Anne goes downstairs, relieved to escape the stream of worries. Rebecca Dew has her own opinion: she believes Cyrus continues sulking because his family allows the behavior to dominate them. Rebecca is convinced that if everyone were less afraid of him, he would not enjoy the power of silence so much.

By the end of the chapter, the dinner has not yet begun, but the tension is already fully established. Esme’s hoped-for engagement, Trix’s secret engagement, Mrs. Taylor’s anxiety, Pringle’s behavior, Dr. Carter’s judgment, and Cyrus’s mood all seem to depend on one evening.

Anne has been invited as a stabilizing influence. She expects to help the conversation along, perhaps soften Cyrus, and protect Esme from an awkward disaster. She does not yet know that the dinner will challenge even her ability to talk—and that her attempt to break the silence will set off consequences no one at the table could predict.

## Vocabulary & Nuance

- **apprehensive** — anxious because something unpleasant may happen. Trix is apprehensive about the dinner.
- **volatile** — likely to change suddenly or become difficult. The emotional atmosphere of the Taylor home feels volatile even though Cyrus is silent.
- **intimidate** — make someone feel afraid or less confident. Cyrus can intimidate the family without raising his voice.
- **anticipate** — expect something and prepare for it in advance. Everyone anticipates Cyrus’s reactions.
- **appease** — try to calm or satisfy someone by giving them what they want. The family arranges the dinner partly to appease Cyrus.
- **walk on eggshells** — behave very carefully because someone may become upset. The Taylors walk on eggshells around Cyrus.
- **come around** — change your opinion or mood and become more reasonable. Anne thinks Cyrus may come around if Trix speaks honestly.
- **back someone up** — support another person when they face opposition. Trix wishes someone would back the family up.
- **build up to** — gradually prepare for a difficult action or event. Trix keeps building up to telling Cyrus about Johnny.
- **set someone off** — cause someone to become angry or upset. The family worries that a small mistake could set Cyrus off.

## Conversation 1 — Walking on Eggshells

*A modern conversation inspired by the Taylor household.*

Trix: We plan everything around Dad’s mood.
Anne: Everything?
Trix: Dinner, guests, what we wear—even when we tell him ordinary news.
Anne: That sounds exhausting.
Trix: We’re always **walking on eggshells**.
Anne: What happens if you stop trying to **appease** him?
Trix: He goes silent, and the whole house freezes.
Anne: Then the silence has become a kind of control.
Trix: Exactly. And tomorrow is the worst possible night for it.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- walk on eggshells — act very carefully because someone may react badly. Everyone walks on eggshells around the unpredictable manager.
- set someone off — trigger anger or distress. One careless remark can set him off.
- come around — become more reasonable or supportive after resisting. Her father eventually came around.
- build up to — prepare gradually for something difficult. I’ve been building up to that conversation for weeks.
- tiptoe around — avoid dealing directly with a sensitive person or issue. The team kept tiptoeing around the real problem.

## Conversation 2 — Losing Perspective

*A modern conversation inspired by Anne’s advice to Trix.*

Anne: You keep saying there is no way to talk to your father.
Trix: There isn’t.
Anne: Or perhaps it feels that way because you’ve lived with the pattern for so long.
Trix: You think I’ve lost perspective?
Anne: A little. Outsiders sometimes notice choices insiders no longer see.
Trix: Such as?
Anne: Telling him the truth and allowing him to be unhappy about it.
Trix: That sounds simple when you say it.
Anne: Simple and easy are not the same thing.

### Phrasal verbs & expressions

- get used to — become so familiar with something that it feels normal. People can get used to unhealthy patterns.
- step back — create emotional or mental distance in order to see a situation more clearly. Step back and ask what you would advise a friend.
- speak up — say what you think despite fear or pressure. She finally spoke up at the meeting.
- back someone up — support someone’s position. I’ll back you up if the conversation becomes difficult.
- give someone room — allow a person space to react or decide. Tell him clearly, then give him room to respond.
